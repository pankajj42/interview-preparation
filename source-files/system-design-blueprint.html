Welcome

This guide turns three sources into one interview-ready curriculum: your GeeksforGeeks System Design course (319 pages, distilled), the topic map and real-system "metro lines" you saved from LinkedIn, and a cross-check against the 2026 editions of Grokking the System Design Interview, ByteByteGo's System Design Interview (Vols. 1–2), and Hello Interview's fundamentals — plus the two things none of those courses had two years ago: agentic AI infrastructure, and the fact that "system design" at most companies quietly means two different interviews wearing one name.

Two interviews, one name. Low-Level Design (LLD) asks you to design classes, not clusters — a parking lot, an elevator bank, a movie-ticket system — and it's really testing OOP, SOLID, and design patterns. High-Level Design (HLD) asks you to design a service that runs on thousands of machines — Twitter's timeline, Uber's dispatch, a URL shortener at Google scale — and it's testing distributed-systems judgment. Some interview loops run both as separate rounds; some only run HLD; a few (Amazon especially) lean hard on LLD. Ask your recruiter which one(s) you'll face, and weight your prep accordingly. This guide covers both in full, in that order, because LLD's vocabulary (interfaces, composition, single-responsibility) quietly underpins how you name and split components in HLD.

  • Part I — Low-Level Design — OOP foundations, UML, SOLID, the 23 classic design patterns, and four fully worked LLD interview problems.

Part 0 — The Interview Itself

0.1What's actually being graded

A system design interview is not a test of whether you can produce a correct architecture — there rarely is a single correct one. GeeksforGeeks' own framework material is blunt about this: "There is no one-size-fits-all solution in system design." Interviewers are grading four things, roughly in this order of importance:

  1. Structured thinking under ambiguity. Do you ask clarifying questions before designing, or do you start drawing boxes immediately? Rushing to an answer without clarifying scope is explicitly called out as a red flag in interviewer training material.
  2. Communication. Can you narrate trade-offs out loud, in real time, the way you'd explain a decision to a teammate? Staff-level bars especially reward this over textbook completeness.
  3. Depth on demand. When the interviewer says "go deeper on the database," can you go three layers deeper than your initial box-and-arrow diagram?
  4. Judgment about trade-offs. Every component you add costs something (latency, cost, operational complexity). Naming the cost is worth more than naming the component.

You are expected to lead the interview — it's your design, your whiteboard, your pacing. The interviewer will stay quiet unless you ask.

0.2The end-to-end framework

Every major course converges on some variant of the same four movements. Grokking's original version calls it a step-by-step framework; Educative's newer edition names it RESHADED (Requirements → Estimation → Storage/data model → High-level design → Detailed design → Evaluate trade-offs → Distill); Hello Interview calls its version the Delivery Framework. They are the same shape:

Step What you do Time (45–50 min interview)
1. Requirements Clarify scope, functional + non-functional requirements, state assumptions out loud 5–10 min
2. Estimation Back-of-envelope: scale, QPS, storage, bandwidth folded into step 1 or 3
3. High-Level Design Draw the major components and how data flows between them 10–15 min
4. Detailed Design Deep-dive into 1–2 components the interviewer steers you toward 15–25 min
5. Wrap-up Bottlenecks, single points of failure, what you'd do with more time 3–5 min

A real requirements-gathering exchange (paraphrased from course interview transcripts) shows the rhythm:

Interviewer: Let's design Facebook. You: Which part or component? Interviewer: Let's design the feed. You: Should it contain photos and videos, or just text? Interviewer: Let's start simple — we can extend later. You: Should the feed be searchable? Real-time, or is some staleness acceptable?

Notice the candidate never guesses. Each answer narrows scope before any design happens.

0.3Functional vs. non-functional requirements

  • Functional requirements — what the system does: user-facing features and actions ("users can post a tweet," "users can search by hashtag").
  • Non-functional requirements — how well it does it: scale, latency, availability, consistency, security, cost. These usually decide your architecture more than the functional list does — a feed that must be "real-time for 500M users" implies a completely different design than one that tolerates a 10-second delay for 50,000 users.
  • Constraints — budget, team size, existing tech stack, timeline, legal/compliance limits.

Generic questions that unlock 90% of interviews (compiled from course frameworks):

  • What's the scale — DAU/MAU, and how much data per user?
  • Is this global, regional, or single-market?
  • What's the expected growth rate over 1–3 years?
  • Availability vs. consistency — which one bends when the network partitions?
  • What's an acceptable latency for the core action?
  • What's in scope for an MVP vs. a "nice to have" you'd flag and defer?
  • Any compliance constraints — GDPR, HIPAA, PCI-DSS, data residency?
  • Read-heavy or write-heavy? What's the read:write ratio?

0.4Back-of-the-envelope estimation

A back-of-envelope calculation is a rough, defensible estimate — not a precise one. The goal is to pick the right class of solution (single Postgres instance vs. a sharded cluster; a queue vs. a raw HTTP call), not to produce a number you'd bet money on. Talk through it out loud; a silent calculation is wasted signal.

Useful powers of 2 and time constants:

Value Approx.
2^10 ~1 thousand (KB)
2^20 ~1 million (MB)
2^30 ~1 billion (GB)
2^40 ~1 trillion (TB)
Seconds in a day ~86,400 (~10^5)
Seconds in a month ~2.6 million
Seconds in a year ~31.5 million (~3.15 × 10^7)

Worked example — IoT health-monitoring fleet (from course material, reproduced because the mechanics generalize to almost any "design a system that ingests X" problem):

Scenario: 1 million active cars, each emitting a telemetry event every 10 seconds. Dashboards need 1-minute granularity. Target read latency < 50 ms.

  1. Throughput: 1,000,000 cars ÷ 10 s = 100,000 events/sec ingested.
  2. Record size: id (4B) + attribute (8B) + value (4B) + timestamp (8B) ≈ 25 bytes/event.
  3. Ingestion rate: 25 B × 100,000/s = 2.5 MB/s.
  4. 1-year storage: 2.5 MB/s × ~31.5M s/year ≈ ~79 TB/year (round to ~100 TB for headroom).
  5. 5-year storage: ≈ 500 TB, before compression or downsampling.
  6. Now the follow-ups write themselves: Is one server enough for 100K req/s? What does this do to the storage layer? Do you downsample old data?

This is the whole game: convert a vague prompt into three or four numbers, then let the numbers argue for a design.

Rules of thumb for interpreting your own numbers:

  • L1 cache < L2 cache < RAM < SSD < spinning disk < network, in both speed and typical cost per GB — cheapest last.
  • A single well-tuned relational database can usually handle low tens of thousands of QPS before you need to shard.
  • If your QPS estimate is in the hundreds, you probably don't need to shard, queue, or introduce Kafka — resist the urge to over-architect a small problem.
  • Round aggressively. "About 100K QPS" beats a false-precision "94,608 QPS" — the interview rewards the right order of magnitude, not decimal accuracy.

0.6The excellence checklist — what separates hire from no-hire

Nine habits, cross-checked across multiple interviewer-side training materials, that consistently distinguish a strong signal from a merely-passing one:

  1. State assumptions out loud and revisit them when the scale changes — "Assuming 10K QPS; if it were 1M, I'd shard here instead."
  2. Quantify before and after every design choice — a decision without a number attached is an opinion, not an engineering judgment.
  3. Name 2–3 alternatives for every major decision, then pick one and justify the pick — not just "I'll use Kafka," but "queue vs. stream vs. direct call, and here's why streaming wins here."
  4. Drive the conversation — "The hardest part here is X — shall we go deep on that?" rather than waiting to be steered.
  5. Discuss failure modes without being asked: node death, network partition, hot keys, thundering herd, poison messages, clock skew, replication lag.
  6. Know the hot-key problem cold — it recurs in every cache and KV-store answer (Section 2.5).
  7. Know the thundering-herd fixes cold — jittered TTLs, staggered refresh, mutex/single-flight, stale-while-revalidate (Section 2.5).
  8. End with monitoring and SLOs for your own design — "p99 < 200ms, 99.95% availability, dashboards on error rate and queue depth."
  9. If stuck, don't freeze: re-anchor on requirements, draw boxes-and-arrows, verbalize trade-offs. Partial structure beats silence.

A longer list of anti-patterns worth actively avoiding, several of which are subtle enough that candidates repeat them without noticing:

  • Starting with technology names before requirements exist ("I'll use Kubernetes and Kafka!" said before anyone asked what the system does).
  • Designing before clarifying scope, or silently assuming scope instead of stating it.
  • Producing a design with no numbers anywhere in it.
  • Claiming "exactly-once" delivery as a casual, unqualified fact — say at-least-once plus idempotent processing instead (Section 2.7).
  • Saying the CAP theorem means a system "picks two of three" permanently — it describes behavior during a partition specifically; outside a partition, the real everyday trade-off is latency vs. consistency (Section 2.7's PACELC discussion).
  • Treating a cache as a database with slightly different syntax, without qualifying its weaker durability guarantees.
  • Reaching for microservices, Kafka, or sharding reflexively, without a requirement that actually demands them — see Section 0.4's rule of thumb about not over-architecting a small problem.
  • Treating "eventual consistency" as if it meant "data loss" — it means bounded, temporary staleness, not lost writes.
  • Assuming retries are always safe by default, rather than checking idempotency first (Section 2.7).
  • Explaining every possible feature instead of the two or three that are actually hard.

Part I — Low-Level Design (LLD)

LLD interviews (sometimes called "machine coding" or "OOD" rounds) hand you a real-world object — a parking garage, an elevator bank, a deck of cards — and ask you to model it in code: classes, interfaces, relationships, and enough working logic that a compiler wouldn't reject it. The bar is different from HLD: fewer moving distributed parts, far more scrutiny on whether your abstractions are right.

1.1Object-Oriented Programming Foundations

Concept What it means Interview-relevant nuance
Class vs. Object A class is the blueprint; an object is an instantiated instance with actual state in memory. Be ready to explain this in one sentence — it's a genuine warm-up question.
Constructors Special methods that initialize an object's state at creation time. A default (no-argument) constructor is provided implicitly if you declare none. Overloading constructors is a common way to offer multiple valid initialization paths.
Access modifiers private (class-only), default/package-private, protected (package + subclasses), public (everywhere). Interviewers watch whether you default to public out of laziness — over-exposing state is a real code-smell flag.
Method overloading Same method name, different parameter lists, resolved at compile time. Contrast with overriding, which is runtime polymorphism via inheritance.
Encapsulation Bundling data with the methods that operate on it, and hiding internal state behind a controlled interface (getters/setters, validation). The reason you encapsulate — reusability and preventing invalid state — matters more than the definition.
Abstraction Exposing only essential behavior while hiding implementation detail, typically via abstract classes or interfaces. Abstract classes can hold partial implementation + state; interfaces (pre-Java 8 style) are pure contracts. Know when you'd choose one over the other: an abstract class when subclasses share code, an interface when unrelated classes need to promise the same capability.
Inheritance A subclass acquires the fields/methods of a superclass. Types: single, multilevel, hierarchical, multiple (via interfaces in Java/C#), hybrid. Overused inheritance is a classic LLD anti-pattern — it's why "favor composition over inheritance" shows up in almost every pattern discussion below.
Association, Aggregation, Composition Association — two classes relate but have independent lifecycles (a Teacher and a Student). Aggregation — a "has-a" whole/part relationship where the part can outlive the whole (a Department has Professors, but a professor can exist without the department). Composition — a stricter "owns-a" relationship where the part's lifecycle is bound to the whole (a House and its Rooms — destroy the house, the rooms are gone). This distinction is asked constantly and is exactly the thing "favor composition" is telling you to reach for over inheritance.

1.2UML — communicating the design

You don't need to hand-draw textbook-perfect UML, but you need its vocabulary to narrate a design out loud and sketch it legibly.

  • Class diagram — the workhorse. Boxes for classes (name / attributes / methods), lines for relationships: solid line = association, hollow diamond = aggregation, filled diamond = composition, hollow triangle arrow = inheritance, dashed arrow = dependency/interface implementation.
  • Use-case diagram — actors (primary actors on the left — the people initiating action; secondary actors on the right — systems being acted upon, like a Bank or a Notification service) connected to the actions they can trigger. Good first artifact to align on scope with an interviewer.
  • Sequence diagram — actors and objects as vertical lifelines, arrows as messages between them over time (solid = synchronous call, dashed = return/async), activation bars showing when an object is actively processing. Best for narrating one specific flow ("what happens when a seat is booked").
  • Activity diagram — a flowchart of control flow: start/end nodes, actions, decision diamonds, fork/join bars for parallel flows.
  • Component & deployment diagrams — component diagrams show module-level structure (interfaces as "lollipop/socket" notation); deployment diagrams show physical placement (which service runs on which node) — more HLD-flavored, but they bridge the two rounds.

A repeatable LLD process:

  1. Extract the nouns from the problem statement and assumptions — each becomes a candidate class.
  2. Draw the use-case diagram: who are the actors, what can each trigger?
  3. Draw the class diagram: relationships, then narrow to the key attributes/methods; mark abstract classes and interfaces; add enums for fixed sets of states (OrderStatus, SeatType).
  4. Write skeleton code: class shells, constructors, getters/setters.
  5. Fill in the two or three methods that actually contain interesting logic — this is where design patterns tend to show up.

1.3SOLID Principles

Principle One-line definition Smell it fixes
S — Single Responsibility A class should have one reason to change. "God classes" that handle validation, persistence, and business logic all at once.
O — Open/Closed Open for extension, closed for modification — add new behavior via new code, not by editing existing, tested code. Long if/else or switch chains that grow every time a new case appears; polymorphism replaces the branch.
L — Liskov Substitution A subclass must be usable anywhere its superclass is expected, without breaking correctness. The classic "Square extends Rectangle" trap, or a Duck subclass that can't actually fly().
I — Interface Segregation Many small, client-specific interfaces beat one fat interface that forces classes to implement methods they don't need. A Worker interface with eat() that a RobotWorker is forced to implement meaninglessly.
D — Dependency Inversion Depend on abstractions, not concrete implementations; high-level modules shouldn't reach directly into low-level details. Hard-wiring new MySQLDatabase() inside a service class instead of injecting a Database interface — the reason dependency injection frameworks exist.

Three more OOD principles, predating and underlying SOLID, that show up constantly in code review and pattern discussions independent of which pattern is on the table:

  • Program to an interface, not an implementation — declare variables, parameters, and return types using an abstract type (an interface or abstract class) rather than a concrete one, even when only one implementation exists today. This is the design habit that makes Dependency Inversion and most of the Creational patterns below (Factory Method, Abstract Factory) actually pay off — swapping an implementation later becomes a one-line change instead of a search-and-replace across the codebase.
  • Principle of Least Knowledge (the Law of Demeter) — an object should only "talk to its immediate friends": the objects passed directly to its methods, objects it creates itself, and its own direct fields — not objects it reaches by chaining through one of those (getEngine().getCarburetor().adjust() is the textbook violation). Following it means writing more small forwarding methods, in exchange for classes that can change their own internals without breaking every caller that reached through them.
  • The Hollywood Principle ("don't call us, we'll call you") — low-level components don't call back up into high-level ones directly; instead, high-level code hooks itself into low-level extension points and waits to be invoked. This is the exact mechanic behind the Template Method and Observer patterns below (a subclass never calls its own template steps directly — the base class calls them at the right moment) and the general principle behind inversion of control / dependency-injection frameworks and most plugin architectures.

1.4The 23 Design Patterns (Gang of Four)

Design patterns are named, reusable solutions to recurring OOD problems — not a checklist to force into every answer, but a shared vocabulary that lets you say "Strategy pattern" instead of re-explaining an idea from scratch.

Creational — how objects get created

Pattern Problem it solves Example
Singleton Ensure exactly one instance of a class exists, with global access. A config manager or connection pool. Watch for thread-safety and testability critiques — it's the most over-used pattern in real codebases.
Factory Method Delegate object creation to subclasses instead of calling new directly. A NotificationFactory that returns an SMSNotification or EmailNotification based on input.
Abstract Factory A factory of related factories — produce families of related objects without specifying concrete classes. A UI toolkit producing a matched set of Button/Checkbox for Windows vs. macOS.
Builder Construct a complex object step-by-step, avoiding telescoping constructors; supports method chaining. Building an immutable Pizza with optional toppings, or an HTTP request object.
Prototype Create new objects by cloning an existing instance rather than building from scratch. Cloning a pre-configured Car template for many similar instances.

Structural — how objects and classes compose

Pattern Problem it solves Example
Adapter Let two incompatible interfaces work together by wrapping one in a translator. Wrapping a legacy Printer.print() API to match a new Document.render() interface.
Facade Provide one simplified interface over a complex subsystem. A MediaFacade.playMovie() that hides codec, subtitle, and audio subsystem calls.
Composite Treat individual objects and compositions of objects uniformly via a shared interface — the classic fix for "folders containing files and other folders." A filesystem tree, or a UI component tree.
Decorator Attach responsibilities to an object dynamically, as an alternative to subclassing for every combination. Wrapping a Coffee with MilkDecorator, then SugarDecorator, stacking behavior at runtime.
Proxy A stand-in that controls access to a real object — for lazy loading, access control, caching, or remote calls. A RealImage only loaded from disk when display() is actually called.
Flyweight Share common state across many fine-grained objects to cut memory use. Rendering millions of trees in a game by sharing one mesh/texture object and only storing per-instance position.
Bridge Decouple an abstraction from its implementation so the two can vary independently. Separating a Shape hierarchy from a Renderer hierarchy so any shape can use any renderer.

Behavioral — how objects communicate and share responsibility

Pattern Problem it solves Example
Observer One-to-many dependency: when a subject changes state, all dependents are notified automatically. A weather station pushing updates to multiple display subscribers; the pub/sub pattern at object scale.
Strategy Encapsulate interchangeable algorithms behind a common interface, selected at runtime. Swapping payment methods (CreditCardStrategy, UPIStrategy) without touching the checkout class.
Template Method Define the skeleton of an algorithm in a base class, letting subclasses override specific steps. A Beverage base class fixing "boil water → brew → pour → add condiments," with Tea/Coffee overriding just the condiment step.
Chain of Responsibility Pass a request along a chain of handlers until one handles it. A support-ticket escalation chain: L1 → L2 → supervisor.
Command Encapsulate a request as an object, enabling queuing, undo/redo, and logging of operations. A text editor's undo stack, or a remote-control button bound to an action object.
Iterator Provide a way to traverse a collection's elements without exposing its internal structure. Standard-library iterators over lists, trees, or custom collections.
State Let an object alter its behavior when its internal state changes, appearing to change class. An Order behaving differently in Placed, Shipped, and Delivered states.
Mediator Centralize complex communications between a set of objects so they don't reference each other directly. An air-traffic-control tower coordinating planes that never talk to each other directly.
Memento Capture and externalize an object's internal state so it can be restored later, without violating encapsulation. Save/checkpoint functionality in a game.
Visitor Separate an algorithm from the object structure it operates on by adding a "visit" method per element type. Running multiple unrelated operations (export, validation, pretty-print) over a fixed AST/document tree.
Interpreter Define a representation for a grammar and an interpreter to evaluate sentences in it. A simple rule engine or expression evaluator.

Compound patterns — MVC as the running example: patterns are frequently used together rather than in isolation, and Model-View-Controller is the classic illustration of three patterns cooperating: the Model notifies interested parties of state changes via Observer; a View can be swapped out (a web view vs. a mobile view over the same Model) because the View treats the Model through the Strategy-like separation of what changes from what stays fixed; and the Controller mediating between View and Model is a variant of Adapter — translating raw user input into the operations the Model actually understands. Recognizing that a familiar architecture is a composition of smaller patterns, rather than something unrelated to them, is itself a useful interview reflex — it's the difference between having memorized 23 flashcards and actually understanding the vocabulary.

1.5Worked LLD Case Studies

Every LLD round follows the same rhythm: nouns → actors → class diagram → skeleton → key methods. Below are the four problems that show up most often (parking lot and elevator are near-universal; movie ticketing and library systems are close seconds), condensed to their skeleton.

Design a Parking Lot

Why this problem, specifically: it's the most-asked LLD question precisely because it's rich enough to sustain 45 minutes without being exotic — it has a natural class hierarchy, a genuinely interesting assignment algorithm, a real concurrency race condition, and an easy bridge into HLD if the interviewer wants to keep going. Treat everything below as material for one continuous conversation, not a checklist to recite.

Clarifying questions to ask before designing anything:

  • Is this a single-level lot or multi-level? (Assume multi-level — it's more general and costs nothing extra in the class design.)
  • How many entry/exit gates? Can a vehicle pay at exit, or is this a reserve-ahead model?
  • Which vehicle types and spot types — motorcycles, cars, buses/trucks, handicapped, electric-charging?
  • Is pricing flat, hourly, or dynamic (surge-style)?
  • Is this a whiteboard/verbal round, or will you be asked to produce compilable code? (Changes how much you write out vs. describe.)
  • Scale: is this "one physical garage" (pure LLD) or "10,000 garages across a city" (the HLD bridge, Section 0's note on which round you're actually in)? Confirm you're solving the single-garage version unless told otherwise.

Functional requirements (state these out loud before class design):

  1. A vehicle can enter, be assigned an available spot matching its size, and receive a ticket recording entry time and spot.
  2. A vehicle can exit by presenting its ticket; the system computes a fee and processes payment, then frees the spot.
  3. The system tracks real-time availability per spot type and can report "full" before a vehicle enters a level that has none free.
  4. An admin can add/remove levels and spots, and mark a spot out of service (maintenance) without deleting it.

Non-functional requirements: thread-safe spot assignment under concurrent entries (the sharpest technical requirement in the whole problem); extensible to new vehicle/spot types and new pricing schemes without modifying existing classes (Open/Closed, Section 1.3); reasonably fast assignment (a linear scan of a few hundred spots is fine — this is not a distributed-systems performance problem).

Why each design decision is made, not just what it is:

  • ParkingLot as Singleton. There is exactly one physical lot represented by exactly one in-memory coordinator — two instances would let two entry gates independently believe a spot is free. Prefer enum-based singleton or eager initialization over classic lazy double-checked locking: DCL is the answer interviewers expect you to know exists, but naming its historical pitfall (needing the volatile keyword in Java to stop instruction-reordering bugs, in languages where that's relevant) and preferring a simpler eager or enum singleton unless lazy loading is genuinely required is the stronger answer. Also flag the trade-off unprompted: singletons make unit testing harder (global mutable state) and are frequently cited as an overused pattern — a strong candidate says this even while choosing it here, because a single physical resource is one of the few cases where it's actually the right call.
  • ParkingSpot as an abstract class, not an interface. Spots share real state (status, occupying vehicle) and real behavior (assignVehicle/removeVehicle), not just a contract — that's exactly the "abstract class holds shared implementation" case from Section 1.1, versus an interface which would force every subtype to reimplement identical logic.
  • Spot assignment as Strategy, injected into Level. The assignment policy (nearest spot, best-fit by size to minimize wasted large spots, or a policy that spreads vehicles across levels for fire-safety egress) is exactly the kind of "this will change" axis the Head First principle "identify what varies and encapsulate it" (Section 1.3) is describing — hardcoding one policy into Level.findAvailableSpot() would force a code change every time the business wants a different policy, whereas injecting a SpotAssignmentStrategy lets you swap it with zero changes to Level itself.
  • Pricing as Strategy too, for the identical reason — flat rate, hourly, and dynamic pricing are three implementations of one interface, selected at ParkingLot construction time or even per-vehicle-type.

The spot-assignment algorithm, worked through:

function findAvailableSpot(level, vehicleType):
    requiredSpotType = mapVehicleToSpotType(vehicleType)  // e.g. Motorcycle -> Compact-or-larger OK
    for spot in level.spots:
        if spot.isAvailable() and spot.type >= requiredSpotType:
            return spot
    return null  // this level is full for this vehicle type; caller tries next level

This is intentionally simple (linear scan) — say so, and justify it: a real garage has hundreds, not millions, of spots, so O(n) is fine, and prematurely reaching for an index here is exactly the over-engineering anti-pattern from Section 0.6's checklist. The one real optimization worth naming if pushed: maintain a small free-count per spot-type, per level counter, updated on assign/release, so getAvailableSpotCount() and "is this level even worth scanning" are O(1) instead of re-scanning every time — a cache in front of a linear scan, not a replacement for one.

The concurrency race condition — this is the question that separates candidates: two vehicles arrive at two different entry gates simultaneously; both threads call findAvailableSpot(), both see the same single remaining compact spot as available, and both try to assign it. A naive if (spot.isAvailable()) { spot.assignVehicle(v); } has a check-then-act race between the if and the assignment. Fixes, from simplest to most scalable:

  1. Synchronize at the spot level — assignVehicle() itself is synchronized (or wraps its state change in a lock), and returns false if the spot was already taken between the caller's check and the call; the caller loops to the next candidate spot on false. This is the answer to actually give — it makes the spot responsible for its own atomicity, rather than trusting every caller to lock correctly.
  2. A single lock around the whole assignment section — correct but serializes every entry gate through one lock, a real throughput problem once you have many gates; worth naming as the naive-but-safe baseline before presenting option 1 as the improvement.
  3. Compare-and-swap on spot status (status.compareAndSet(FREE, OCCUPIED)) — the lock-free version of option 1, worth naming if the interviewer pushes on lock contention specifically.

Concrete method walkthrough — parkVehicle:

function parkVehicle(vehicle):
    for level in levels:                       // in some priority order
        spot = level.findAvailableSpot(vehicle.type)
        if spot != null and spot.assignVehicle(vehicle):   // atomic; see above
            ticket = new Ticket(vehicle, spot, now())
            return ticket
    throw LotFullException()

And unparkVehicle:

function unparkVehicle(ticket):
    fee = pricingStrategy.calculateFee(ticket)
    receipt = paymentProcessor.charge(ticket.vehicle.owner, fee)   // Section 2.12's idempotency key applies to this call
    ticket.spot.removeVehicle()

Extensions an interviewer will likely steer toward, and the right shape of answer for each:

  • "Add electric vehicle charging spots that bill differently while occupied." — a new ElectricSpot subclass with its own metered-billing hook, and a PricingStrategy variant that reads meter data instead of just elapsed time; no change to ParkingLot or Level.
  • "Support monthly pass holders who skip the ticket/payment flow." — a Vehicle gets an optional subscription reference; parkVehicle checks for an active subscription before falling through to the normal ticket path — an argument for a Customer/Subscription class you'd add, not a change to the spot-assignment logic at all.
  • "What if there are multiple entry gates and you want to load-balance which level each gate sends vehicles to?" — this is explicitly the bridge to HLD (Section 0's two-interview framing): now findAvailableSpot needs to be backed by a shared, low-latency availability service (Redis counters per level/type) rather than an in-process object, because multiple gate processes need a consistent view — say this transition out loud rather than trying to keep solving it as pure OOD.
  • "How would you test this?" — unit tests per spot-assignment strategy in isolation (inject a fake Level with a fixed spot layout), a concurrency test that fires N threads at a lot with fewer than N free spots and asserts exactly the free-spot-count worth of successful assignments, and a fee-calculation test per pricing strategy independent of parking logic — the fact that Strategy makes each of these testable in isolation, without spinning up the whole ParkingLot, is itself worth saying as a payoff of the pattern choice.

Design an Elevator System

Why this problem, specifically: unlike the parking lot, the state machine is the star here, not the class hierarchy — this is the LLD question that most directly tests whether you actually understand the State pattern and can compare scheduling algorithms with real trade-offs, rather than just naming them.

Clarifying questions: how many elevators, how many floors — and critically, is this one elevator or a bank of elevators sharing a call system? (Assume a bank — it's the more general, more interesting version, and the single-elevator case falls out of it as N=1.) Are there separate express/service elevators? Does the building have a maximum weight/capacity to enforce? Do you need to model a real-time simulation (clock ticks) or just the request-handling logic?

Functional requirements:

  1. A passenger inside a car can select a destination floor (an internal request).
  2. A passenger on a floor can press up/down (an external request — notice this does not specify a destination floor, only a direction, which is exactly why it's modeled as a separate request type).
  3. A central controller assigns each external request to one elevator in the bank.
  4. Each elevator reports its current floor, direction, and door state, and the system displays it.

Non-functional requirements: minimize average wait time; avoid starvation (a request in the "wrong" direction shouldn't wait forever while the elevator keeps servicing same-direction requests); safe behavior under door-obstruction and overweight conditions.

Why State, concretely, not just "it fits": an elevator's response to "a button was pressed" genuinely depends on what it's currently doing — if DoorOpenState, a floor request just resets the door-close timer; if MovingState, it enqueues the floor as a stop; if IdleState, it starts moving. Without the State pattern this becomes a wall of if (state == IDLE) ... else if (state == MOVING) ... inside every single method — exactly the "long conditional chain that grows every time a new case appears" smell the Open/Closed principle (Section 1.3) names, and exactly what State fixes by giving each state its own class implementing a shared handle()-style interface, with the elevator simply delegating to currentState.handle(this).

The dispatch algorithm — the single most-probed part of this problem. Compare at least two, concretely:

  • Nearest-car: assign the closest idle-or-same-direction elevator. Simple, minimizes wait time for that request, but can strand a far-away request indefinitely if closer elevators keep getting the newer calls first (starvation).
  • SCAN / LOOK (the disk-scheduling algorithm, repurposed): an elevator moving up continues servicing every up-request in its path until it runs out of up-requests above it, then reverses — it never reverses direction just because a new request appeared behind it. Maintained with two sorted sets (upStops, downStops in the class diagram above — a TreeSet/balanced BST gives O(log n) insert and O(1) "next stop" via first()); on arriving at a floor, pop it from the current-direction set, and only switch to the other set once the current one is empty. This bounds worst-case wait time far better than nearest-car and is what real elevator systems actually approximate.
  • Trade-off to state explicitly: nearest-car optimizes average wait time for the next request at the cost of fairness; SCAN optimizes worst-case fairness at a modest cost to average-case time for an isolated request. A strong answer picks one, states which metric it's optimizing for, and names the other as the alternative with the opposite trade-off — reciting only one algorithm without contrasting it against another is a memorized answer, not a reasoned one.

Concurrency: multiple floor-button presses can arrive at the controller concurrently from different floors; the controller's requestElevator() needs its elevator-selection-plus-assignment to be atomic per request (the same check-then-act shape as the parking lot's spot assignment), or two external requests can both be assigned to the same elevator under the mistaken belief each is "the only one this elevator just picked up," overloading its effective stop count. In practice: each Elevator's stop-set mutation is synchronized, exactly like ParkingSpot.assignVehicle() above.

Extensions an interviewer will likely steer toward:

  • "What about weight/capacity limits?" — Elevator gains a currentLoad and refuses new internal requests (door won't close, or an overweight alarm state) past a threshold — a new OverweightState in the state machine, not a change to the dispatch logic.
  • "VIP/express floors that skip intermediate stops?" — a second DispatchStrategy implementation reserved for those elevators; the ElevatorController already supports per-elevator or per-zone strategies if strategy is modeled as potentially different per elevator group, which is worth mentioning as a reason to keep DispatchStrategy pluggable rather than a single global field.
  • "Fire alarm — all elevators must go to ground floor and stop responding to calls." — a system-wide override state, most cleanly modeled as the controller broadcasting a forced state transition to every elevator rather than each elevator polling for the condition itself (Section 1.3's Hollywood Principle: the controller calls the elevators, they don't each independently poll for an alarm).

The seat-locking algorithm, worked through precisely — this is the entire interview:

function holdSeats(showId, seatIds, userId):
    bookingId = generateId()
    for seatId in seatIds:
        // atomic conditional update — the same primitive as Section 2.18's
        // fix for the lost-update anomaly, applied at LLD scale
        success = db.updateShowSeat(
            showId, seatId,
            setStatus = HELD, heldBy = bookingId, holdExpiry = now() + 5min,
            WHERE status = AVAILABLE
        )
        if not success:
            rollbackAnyHeldSeatsSoFar(bookingId)
            throw SeatUnavailableException(seatId)
    return new Booking(bookingId, PENDING_PAYMENT, seatIds, userId)

function confirmBooking(bookingId, paymentResult):
    if paymentResult.success:
        db.updateSeatsStatus(bookingId, newStatus = SOLD)
        booking.status = CONFIRMED
    else:
        db.releaseSeats(bookingId)   // status back to AVAILABLE
        booking.status = CANCELLED

// a background sweep, independent of any user action:
function expireStaleHolds():
    for seat in db.findSeatsWhere(status = HELD, holdExpiry < now()):
        db.updateShowSeat(seat, status = AVAILABLE, heldBy = null)

The line to say explicitly: the correctness guarantee comes from the atomic conditional update (WHERE status = AVAILABLE), not from checking status first and writing second — a plain if (seat.status == AVAILABLE) { seat.status = HELD; } has exactly the same race as the parking lot's naive spot check, and this problem exists specifically to test whether you reach for the fix reflexively by now. In a single-process in-memory version this conditional update can be a synchronized block or a compareAndSet; in a real multi-server deployment it's a database row-level lock or a Redis SET ... NX — the LLD answer and the HLD answer are the same primitive at different scales, which is worth saying outright.

Why a 5-minute TTL hold, specifically, and what governs the number: too short, and a slow-but-genuine payment flow loses the seat out from under a paying customer; too long, and seats sit artificially unavailable while an abandoned session holds them — this is the exact same TTL-sizing trade-off named generically in Part II's distributed-locks discussion (Section 2.7), here applied to a business-visible, user-facing timer rather than an internal system lock.

Concurrency edge case interviewers probe directly: two users select the same two seats at the same instant. Walk through it: both call holdSeats; for the first seat, one succeeds and one fails (the atomic update guarantees exactly one AVAILABLE → HELD transition succeeds); the failing user's entire request should roll back cleanly (release any seats from that same request it did manage to hold a moment earlier), not leave them holding one seat out of the two they wanted — this all-or-nothing behavior is exactly why the pseudocode above rolls back on the first failure rather than continuing to try the remaining seats.

Extensions an interviewer will likely steer toward:

  • "What about a multiplex chain — same movie, many theaters, many cities?" — the bridge to HLD's Ticket-Booking case study (Part III, Group F): search becomes a dedicated search/filter service, and the seat-hold TTL mechanism generalizes directly into the "virtual waiting room" pattern for a high-demand on-sale.
  • "Group booking where seats must be adjacent?" — a seat-adjacency query against the Screen's seat layout before attempting holds, so you don't burn atomic-update attempts on a combination that was never geometrically valid.
  • "Refunds and cancellations after confirmation?" — a Booking state machine gains a CANCELLED_POST_CONFIRMATION transition with its own compensating action (seat back to available, refund initiated) — a small, self-contained Saga (Part II, Section 2.1) at LLD scale.

Design a Library Management System

Why this problem, specifically: it's usually treated as the "easier" LLD warm-up, and the risk is under-preparing for it — the reservation queue and fine calculation still deserve the same rigor as the harder problems above, and a surprising number of candidates fumble the Book vs. BookItem distinction that the whole design hinges on.

Clarifying questions: how many copies per title, typically? Are reservations first-come-first-served, or can a librarian prioritize? Are fines a hard block on further borrowing, or just tracked? Multiple branches sharing a catalog, or one building?

Why Book and BookItem are two classes, not one — the single most important modeling decision here: "Clean Code, 2nd Edition" is one catalog entry (Book) but a library might own five physical copies of it (five BookItems), each independently checked out, returned, or lost. Collapsing them into one class makes it impossible to represent "3 of 5 copies are out" at all — a strong signal to an interviewer that you model from access patterns (Section 8, Part II's "query-driven design" idea) rather than from the nouns in the problem statement alone.

The reservation-queue algorithm: a FIFO queue per Book (not per BookItem — a reservation is for the title, satisfied by whichever copy comes back first). On returnItem():

Naming Observer here explicitly is worth doing unprompted — "notify waiting members when a copy returns" is a textbook one-to-many, subject-changes-state-and-dependents-are-told relationship, and connecting a fresh problem back to a named pattern is exactly the "shared vocabulary" payoff Section 1.4 opens with.

Fine calculation as Strategy, again — flat-per-day-late vs. a capped maximum are two implementations of one FineStrategy interface, injected into Member or into the checkout/return flow, for the identical reasoning already given twice above: it's an axis that varies by library policy, not by any other part of the design, so it should be free to change in isolation.

Concurrency note, and why it's lighter here than the other three problems: two members can't race for the same BookItem the way two drivers can race for the same parking spot, because checkout already requires physically holding a unique barcode-scanned copy — the real race is on the reservation queue itself (two returns happening concurrently, each trying to pop the next reservation), which is a standard concurrent-queue/producer-consumer problem, solvable with a synchronized queue or an atomic dequeue — worth naming as "the same shape of problem, smaller stakes" rather than a new concept.

Extensions an interviewer will likely steer toward:

  • "Multiple branches sharing one catalog?" — Book stays global; BookItem gains a branchId, and search/reservation logic needs to decide whether a reservation is branch-specific or system-wide (a requirements question to ask back, not assume).
  • "Digital/e-book copies with no physical scarcity?" — a DigitalBookItem with a licensing-seat-count instead of a single physical copy, reusing the exact same BookStatus/checkout flow with a different availability rule (available while activeLoans < licenseSeats).
  • "A member with unpaid fines shouldn't be able to check out more books." — a guard clause in checkout() checking member.outstandingFines < threshold before proceeding; worth noting this is a business rule best kept as an explicit, visible check in the checkout flow rather than buried inside Member, so it's easy to find and change.

Part II — High-Level Design (HLD)

This is the reference core of the guide — every building block that recurs across "design X" problems, organized the way your saved topic map laid it out, expanded with the depth each topic needs and the 2026 additions (AI infrastructure, updated consensus and consistency coverage) that the major courses added this year.

2.1Architecture Styles

Style What it is Strengths Costs Real examples
Monolith Single codebase, single deployable unit, shared memory space, typically synchronous internal calls. Simple to build, debug, and deploy; no network hops between modules; fastest to ship v1. Scaling means replicating the whole app even if only one part is hot; one bug can take down everything; large codebases get harder to onboard into. Most startups' first architecture.
Modular monolith A monolith with enforced internal boundaries (separate modules/packages with clear interfaces) but still one deployable. Keeps monolith simplicity while making a future microservices split tractable. Discipline-dependent — boundaries erode without enforcement. Shopify (famously) at large scale.
Microservices Independent services, each owning its own data store, communicating over the network (usually REST/gRPC/events). Independent scaling and deployment per service; fault isolation (one service's crash doesn't cascade); teams can pick different tech per service. Network latency between calls; distributed-transaction complexity; much higher operational overhead (service discovery, distributed tracing, versioning). Netflix, Amazon (both migrated from monoliths explicitly to handle scale and team-independence).
Event-driven architecture (EDA) Components communicate by producing and reacting to events through a broker, rather than direct calls. Strong decoupling — producers and consumers don't know about each other; natural fit for async workflows and real-time pipelines. Harder to trace a single request across the system; eventual consistency by default; duplicate-event handling (idempotency) becomes mandatory, not optional. Order-placed → payment, inventory, and notification services all reacting independently.
Serverless (FaaS) Cloud provider manages all infrastructure; you deploy functions triggered by events (HTTP call, file upload, queue message). Zero idle cost, scales to zero and to huge burst automatically, no server management. Cold-start latency; vendor lock-in; harder to reason about long-running or stateful workloads; cost can spike unpredictably under sustained high load. AWS Lambda, Azure Functions, Google Cloud Functions — great for unpredictable-traffic APIs and event-driven glue code.

Scaling Scale the entire app Scale only the hot service
Fault isolation A crash can take down everything A failure is contained to one service (with the right patterns)
Operational overhead Low High — needs service discovery, distributed tracing, API versioning

How real migrations happen: understand the domain and its natural seams → extract one bounded service at a time (start with something low-risk, e.g., notifications) → keep the monolith and new service talking over a stable API during the transition → repeat. Nobody rewrites everything in one shot.

Supporting patterns for microservices:

  • Circuit Breaker — wraps a call to a potentially-failing service in a state machine with three states: Closed (requests flow normally, failures are counted), Open (once failures cross a threshold, all calls fail fast without even attempting the network call, giving the failing service room to recover), Half-Open (after a cooldown, a trial request is let through to test recovery). This exists specifically to stop cascading failures — one slow downstream dependency shouldn't be able to exhaust every thread/connection in the callers upstream of it.
  • Bulkhead — named after a ship's watertight compartments: give each downstream dependency its own isolated pool of threads/connections, so a slow or hung dependency can only exhaust its own pool rather than starving every other call the service makes. Circuit breakers and bulkheads are usually deployed together — the bulkhead limits the blast radius while the breaker stops sending doomed requests entirely.
  • Retry and timeout discipline — exponential backoff with jitter (Section 2.8) on retries, a hard cap on retry count, and a retry budget (never let retries exceed some percentage of total traffic, or a failing dependency's load multiplies under you). Timeouts must cascade correctly: a caller's timeout should always be longer than its callee's, or the caller gives up and frees resources while the callee is still doing now-wasted work — a subtle bug that shows up under load but not in testing.
  • Sidecar pattern — a helper process deployed alongside a service (sharing its lifecycle) that handles cross-cutting concerns — logging, metrics, service discovery, mTLS termination — so the main service's code stays focused on business logic. This is the mechanism a service mesh (Section 2.15) is built from; Envoy proxy is the canonical example.
  • Saga pattern — the microservices answer to "how do I do a multi-step transaction when each step lives in a different service's database and 2PC is off the table." A saga is a sequence of local transactions, each publishing an event that triggers the next step; if a step fails, previously completed steps run compensating transactions to undo their effect (e.g., "refund payment" compensates "charge card"). Two coordination styles: choreography (each service listens for events and decides what to do next — no central brain, but harder to see the whole flow) and orchestration (a central saga orchestrator explicitly calls each step and handles failures — easier to reason about, but a new single component to keep highly available).
  • Outbox pattern — solves a narrower but very common version of the same problem: how do you update your database and publish an event, atomically, without a distributed transaction? Write the event into an outbox table in the same local transaction as the business-data change, then have a separate relay process (often a CDC connector, immediately below) read the outbox and publish to the broker. This guarantees "the event is published if and only if the transaction committed," which a direct "write to DB, then call the broker" sequence cannot guarantee — either step can fail independently and leave the two out of sync.
  • Inbox pattern (the consumer-side twin of Outbox) — the same guarantee is needed on the receiving end: track processed message IDs in an inbox table (or a unique constraint on an event-ID column) so a redelivered message is recognized and skipped rather than reprocessed. This is one concrete implementation of the idempotent-consumer idea from Section 2.7.
  • Change Data Capture (CDC) — rather than hand-writing the outbox relay, a CDC tool (Debezium is the standard open-source answer) tails a database's own replication/commit log and turns every row change into an event stream automatically. This is now the default way production systems keep a search index, a cache, or a downstream analytics store in sync with a system-of-record database, without the application ever explicitly publishing "I changed a row."

2.2Client & API Layer

  • Web and mobile clients — the same backend increasingly serves both; a Backend-for-Frontend (BFF) layer is a common pattern when mobile and web need meaningfully different response shapes or aggregation logic.
  • API Gateway — a single entry point in front of your services that handles routing, authentication, rate limiting, request/response transformation, and sometimes protocol translation (public REST in, internal gRPC out). Almost every microservices design should have one — it's the answer to "how do clients avoid knowing about 40 internal services."

Choosing an API style:

Style Model Best for Watch-outs
REST Resources + standard HTTP verbs (GET/POST/PUT/DELETE), typically JSON Public APIs, simple CRUD, wide client compatibility, easy HTTP-level caching Over- or under-fetching — a mobile client often gets more fields than it needs, or needs three round trips to assemble one screen
GraphQL Clients specify exactly the fields/shape they want in a single query Mobile clients with limited bandwidth, products with many client types wanting different data shapes Harder to cache at the HTTP layer; a naive resolver can trigger the "N+1 query" problem; more server-side complexity
gRPC Contract-first via Protocol Buffers, HTTP/2 transport, strongly typed, supports bidirectional streaming Internal service-to-service calls at high throughput/low latency; polyglot microservices Not naturally browser-native (needs gRPC-Web); binary format is harder to debug by eye than JSON
SOAP XML-based, heavily specified (WS-* standards), built-in transactional/security standards Legacy enterprise systems, banking/financial integrations with strict contracts Verbose, slower to parse, largely legacy at this point
WebSocket Persistent, full-duplex connection Real-time bidirectional needs — chat, live dashboards, multiplayer Keeps a connection (and server resources) open per client — a scalability cost REST doesn't have; needs its own connection-lifecycle and reconnection handling
Long polling Client sends a request; server holds it open without responding until new data exists (or a timeout), then the client immediately re-requests A simple stand-in for real-time updates on infrastructure that doesn't support persistent connections well (older load balancers, restrictive corporate firewalls) Each pending request still ties up a server-side connection/thread while waiting, and it's strictly worse than WebSocket/SSE wherever those are actually available — treat it as a fallback, not a first choice

A useful rule of thumb repeated across the 2026 course updates: REST for anything public-facing or simple; gRPC for internal high-throughput service-to-service calls; GraphQL when your clients have very different data-shape needs; WebSocket/SSE when the interaction is inherently a live stream, not a request/response.

Pagination — offset vs. cursor: offset pagination (?page=4&size=20) is simple but breaks quietly under concurrent writes — if a row is inserted or deleted while a user is paging through, later pages skip or repeat items, since "page 4" is a moving target defined purely by position. Cursor pagination (?after=eyJpZCI6...) encodes an opaque pointer to the last-seen item's stable sort key, so a page is defined relative to actual data rather than a row count, and stays correct under concurrent inserts/deletes. Cursor pagination is the standard answer for any feed, timeline, or large/frequently-changing result set; offset is fine for small, relatively static admin-style listings.

A concrete API contract, worked through (illustrating idempotency keys and response shape together, since this is easier to internalize from one example than from a description):

POST /v1/urls
Idempotency-Key: 7c1e2f-a91b-44d0
Content-Type: application/json

{ "long_url": "https://example.com/a-very-long-article-path" }

{
  "short_code": "aZ91k",
  "short_url": "https://short.example/aZ91k",
  "expires_at": null
}

A retried request carrying the same Idempotency-Key returns this exact same response without creating a second record — the server stores (key → result) the first time and replays it on any repeat, which is the concrete mechanism behind the idempotency concept in Section 2.7.

2.3Networking Fundamentals

  • OSI model (7 layers) vs. TCP/IP model (4 layers) — you won't be asked to recite every layer, but you should recognize that HTTP lives at the application layer, TCP/UDP at the transport layer, and IP at the network layer, because load balancer "Layer 4 vs. Layer 7" terminology (Section 2.4) refers directly to this stack.
  • TCP — connection-oriented, reliable, ordered, retransmits lost packets. Cost: more overhead and latency than UDP. Used for anything where correctness beats speed (web pages, email, file transfer).
  • UDP — connectionless, no delivery guarantee, no ordering guarantee, minimal overhead. Used where low latency matters more than perfect delivery (live video/audio, gaming, DNS lookups).
  • HTTP/HTTPS — HTTP is stateless by design (hence cookies/tokens for session state); HTTPS adds TLS encryption. HTTP/2 added multiplexing (many requests over one connection) and header compression; HTTP/3 (QUIC) moves the transport onto UDP to avoid TCP head-of-line blocking entirely.
  • WebSocket — upgrades an HTTP connection to a persistent, bidirectional socket; see 2.2.

Proxies:

Type Sits between... Typical use
Forward proxy Client and the wider internet Corporate networks controlling/monitoring outbound traffic, hiding client identity from the destination
Reverse proxy External clients and internal servers Load balancing, TLS termination, caching, hiding internal topology from the outside world — nginx, Cloudflare, and most load balancers act as reverse proxies

DNS — how a domain name becomes an IP address:

  1. Browser checks its local cache → OS cache → router cache.
  2. On a miss, the query goes to a recursive resolver (typically run by your ISP).
  3. Resolver asks a root server → directed to the right TLD server (.com, .org) → directed to the domain's authoritative name server, which returns the actual IP.
  4. The result is cached at every layer along the way, governed by a TTL on the DNS record.

Common DNS record types: A (domain → IPv4), AAAA (→ IPv6), CNAME (alias to another domain name), MX (mail server), NS (authoritative name server), TXT (arbitrary text, often used for domain verification). Split-horizon DNS — the same domain name resolves to different answers depending on who's asking (an internal resolver returns a private IP for internal clients; the public internet gets a public-facing IP) — is a small but occasionally-tested detail worth recognizing, used for exposing internal-only services under the same domain scheme as public ones.

DNS as a load-balancing and failover tool (Route 53-style routing policies):

Policy Behavior
Simple One resource, optionally multiple values returned
Weighted Split traffic across resources by a configured proportion (canary releases, A/B infra tests)
Latency-based Route to the region with the lowest latency for that user
Geolocation / Geoproximity Route by the user's location, optionally shifting the boundary between regions
Failover Primary/secondary — reroute automatically when health checks fail
Multivalue Return several healthy IPs, checked for health before being returned

2.4Load & Traffic Management

Load balancers sit in front of a pool of servers and distribute incoming requests so no single server is overwhelmed. They can live in several places in a stack: between client and server, between servers and internal app/job servers, or between app servers and cache/database layers.

By network layer:

Type OSI layer Decision made on Notes
L4 (Network Load Balancer) Transport IP + port, TCP/UDP info Very high throughput (millions of req/s), doesn't inspect payload, so it can't route on URL path or headers
L7 (Application Load Balancer) Application HTTP headers, cookies, URL path, request body Enables content-based routing ("/api/ → service A, /static/ → CDN") at the cost of more per-request processing
GSLB (Global Server Load Balancing) — Geography + health Distributes traffic across data centers/regions, usually built on DNS-level routing

Load-balancing algorithms:

Algorithm How it picks a server Best for
Round robin Cycles through servers in order Uniform servers, uniform request cost
Weighted round robin Round robin, but bigger servers get proportionally more requests Heterogeneous server capacity
Least connections Sends to whichever server currently has the fewest open connections Highly variable request duration (DB connections, WebSockets)
Hashing (consistent hash of IP/URL) Same client/key always routes to the same server Session persistence, or maximizing cache-hit rate per server
Least bandwidth Sends to the server currently moving the least data Streaming/bandwidth-heavy workloads

Load balancers themselves need to scale: vertically (bigger box), horizontally (a pool of LBs, often fronted by DNS round robin so each public IP maps to a highly-available LB pair), and they typically lean on a service registry (e.g., ZooKeeper, Consul, or a cloud-native equivalent) to track which backend instances are currently healthy — tracking that metadata directly inside the load balancer doesn't scale. Two operational details worth naming alongside health checks: connection draining (when an instance is being removed — a deploy, a scale-down — stop routing new requests to it but let its already-in-flight requests finish naturally, rather than cutting them off mid-response) and sticky sessions (routing a given client to the same backend instance for the duration of a session, via a cookie or the hashing algorithm above — useful for stateful backends, but a stopgap; Section 2.16's stateless-services principle is the more durable fix).

CDN (Content Delivery Network) — a globally distributed network of edge servers that cache content physically close to users. Components: edge servers (cache + serve), origin server (source of truth), PoPs (points of presence — clusters of edge servers per region). Two delivery models: push CDN (you upload content ahead of time — more control, more manual work) and pull CDN (the CDN fetches and caches on first request — less manual work, a cold first request is slower). CDNs solve four problems at once: latency (physical distance), origin bandwidth cost, traffic spikes, and — via built-in WAF/DDoS mitigation — a chunk of your security surface. Two refinements worth naming for anything beyond the basics: an origin shield — one additional caching layer sitting between the many edge PoPs and the origin, so a viral piece of content causes at most one cache-miss request per region to reach the origin, rather than every individual edge location missing independently and hammering it simultaneously; and signed URLs — a time-limited, cryptographically-signed link for content that shouldn't be freely cacheable/public (a private video, a paid download), letting the CDN still cache and serve the bytes efficiently while the origin retains control over who is allowed to fetch them and until when.

Rate limiting protects a system from being overwhelmed (accidentally or maliciously) by capping how many requests an identity can make per time window.

Algorithm Mechanism Burst handling Best for
Fixed window counter Count requests in discrete windows (e.g., per minute); reset at boundary Poor — allows up to 2× the limit right at a window boundary Simple, low-stakes internal limits
Sliding window log Keep a timestamp log of every request, count how many fall in the trailing window Excellent precision Strict fairness enforcement, but memory-heavy at scale
Sliding window counter Weighted average of the current and previous fixed windows Good precision, moderate memory Scalable public APIs — the practical default at scale
Token bucket Bucket holds tokens that refill at a fixed rate; each request consumes one; empty bucket = reject/delay Allows controlled bursts up to the bucket size The most common default for developer-facing APIs
Leaky bucket Requests queue and drain at a constant rate regardless of arrival burstiness None by design — smooths output Protecting a downstream system that needs a steady, predictable load (payment processing, DB writes)

In a distributed rate limiter (multiple API gateway nodes, not one), the counter state has to live somewhere shared — typically Redis, using atomic increment operations (or a Lua script, since Redis executes each script atomically, closing the check-then-increment race a naive GET + INCR pair would otherwise have), so two nodes can't both think a client has quota left. A rejected request returns 429 Too Many Requests with a Retry-After header, and well-behaved APIs also return X-RateLimit-Limit / X-RateLimit-Remaining / X-RateLimit-Reset on every response (accepted or not) so clients can self-throttle before they ever get rejected. Enforcement is rarely single-level in practice: a cheap, approximate per-IP check at the edge/CDN, a more accurate per-user check at the API gateway, and a business-specific per-endpoint check inside the service itself.

Autoscaling — automatically add/remove compute based on load (CPU, queue depth, custom metrics), usually with a cooldown to avoid thrashing.

Backpressure — when a downstream component can't keep up, it needs a way to signal "slow down" upstream rather than silently dropping work or falling over. Options, roughly from least to most disruptive: buffer with a bounded queue, apply rate limiting to the producer, shed load intentionally (drop low-priority requests first), or block/deny new requests outright once queues are full.

2.5Data Layer

Relational (SQL) databases store data in tables with fixed schemas and relationships enforced via keys. They're typically ACID-compliant:

Property Guarantee
Atomicity A transaction's operations all succeed or all roll back — no partial writes
Consistency A transaction moves the database from one valid state to another, respecting all constraints
Isolation Concurrent transactions don't see each other's intermediate, uncommitted state
Durability Once committed, a write survives a crash or power loss

Examples: PostgreSQL, MySQL, SQL Server, Aurora. Best when relationships between entities are central to the data, and schema is relatively stable.

NoSQL databases trade some of the above for flexibility and horizontal scalability. Five families, each solving a different access pattern:

Type Model Example systems Best for
Key-value Simple key → opaque value lookup, extremely fast reads/writes Redis, Memcached, DynamoDB Session storage, user preferences, caching
Document JSON/BSON-like nested documents, queryable and indexable on nested fields MongoDB, DocumentDB General-purpose app data that doesn't fit rigid tables well
Wide-column / columnar Rows with flexible columns, grouped in column families; excellent for aggregation over one column across many rows Cassandra, HBase, Redshift, Snowflake, BigQuery High-write-throughput workloads (Cassandra) or analytics/BI over huge datasets (Redshift/BigQuery)
Graph Nodes + first-class relationship edges, optimized for traversal Neo4j, Amazon Neptune Social graphs, recommendation engines, fraud-ring detection — anywhere "how are these connected" beats "join these tables"
Text-search Inverted index over document text, supports fuzzy/semantic matching, built on libraries like Lucene Elasticsearch, Apache Solr Full-text search, log analytics

Also worth naming: time-series databases (InfluxDB, TimescaleDB) — optimized for (timestamp, value) pairs at huge write volume with time-range queries, used for IoT and monitoring data; and object storage (S3, GCS, Azure Blob) — flat key → blob storage for large unstructured files (images, video, backups), not a database in the traditional sense but almost always part of a modern architecture (Section 3, Group G, covers S3's internals).

SQL vs. NoSQL decision guide:

Choose SQL when... Choose NoSQL when...
Relationships between entities matter and need to stay consistent (transfers, inventory, bookings) Access pattern is a simple key lookup at very high volume
You need multi-row ACID transactions Schema will change frequently, or data is naturally nested/document-shaped
Your query patterns are ad hoc and varied You know your query patterns up front and need to shard cheaply for scale
Data volume fits on a well-tuned single cluster with read replicas You need to scale writes horizontally across many commodity nodes from day one

Modern databases blur this line deliberately: Postgres and MySQL both support native JSON columns and horizontal sharding extensions; MongoDB supports multi-document transactions. Don't present SQL vs. NoSQL as a hard binary in an interview — present it as a spectrum, and justify your pick by the access pattern, not the label.

OLTP vs. OLAP is a second, orthogonal axis worth naming explicitly rather than folding silently into "SQL vs. NoSQL": OLTP (Online Transaction Processing) systems (Postgres, MySQL, DynamoDB) are optimized for many small, fast, concurrent reads/writes of individual records — the day-to-day operational path. OLAP (Online Analytical Processing) systems (Redshift, BigQuery, Snowflake, ClickHouse) are optimized for the opposite shape entirely: infrequent but huge queries that scan and aggregate across millions of rows for one or a handful of columns, typically stored columnar rather than row-oriented so a "sum this one column across a billion rows" query never has to read the columns it doesn't need. Running analytics queries against your OLTP database directly is a common early-scaling mistake — the fix is a pipeline (often the CDC or Outbox mechanism below) that continuously copies data from the OLTP system into a dedicated OLAP store, so a slow analytical query can never compete with production traffic for the same resources.

How OLAP data is actually modeled and stored, two details worth knowing by name beyond "it's columnar":

  • Star and snowflake schemas — the standard way analytical data is modeled: one large fact table at the center (one row per event — a sale, a click, a play) with foreign keys out to smaller dimension tables (product, customer, date, region) that hold the descriptive attributes. A star schema keeps dimensions fully denormalized (flat, one level); a snowflake schema normalizes dimensions further into sub-dimensions (a product dimension pointing to a separate brand dimension) — trading a little query simplicity for less redundancy. Almost every "design a metrics/analytics pipeline" follow-up question about "how is the data actually modeled once it lands in the warehouse" is answered by naming this pattern.
  • Column compression — storing each column contiguously (columnar storage) doesn't just let a query skip unwanted columns, it also makes compression dramatically more effective: a column of repeated or low-cardinality values (a status column with 5 possible values across a billion rows) compresses far better sitting next to millions of copies of itself than interleaved with unrelated columns from the same row. Bitmap encoding (one bit-vector per distinct value, marking which rows contain it) is the classic technique this enables, turning "how many rows have status = shipped" into a fast bitwise population-count instead of a row-by-row scan.

Multi-tenancy — when one system serves many independent customers ("tenants," common in B2B SaaS), the data layer has to decide how much isolation each tenant gets, and it's a spectrum rather than one right answer: shared database, shared schema (every table has a tenant_id column; cheapest to run and easiest to scale the fleet, but a query missing a tenant_id filter is a real data-leak risk, and one tenant's usage spike can degrade performance for every other tenant sharing the same database); shared database, separate schema per tenant (stronger isolation, still one database to operate); separate database per tenant (strongest isolation and the easiest to reason about for compliance, at real operational cost — migrations, backups, and scaling all now happen per-tenant instead of once). A reasonable default answer: shared schema with tenant_id for most tenants, with an escape hatch to a dedicated database for the largest or most compliance-sensitive customers — a pattern SaaS companies commonly call a "pool vs. silo" model.

Caching — storing a copy of data in a faster medium to avoid repeating expensive work.

Where a cache lives:

  • Client-side — browser/app cache for static assets.
  • Application-server cache — in-process memory on a single node; simplest, but doesn't help once you have multiple app servers (each has its own cache, so hit rate suffers and each miss still hits the DB).
  • Distributed cache — a cache cluster (e.g., Redis Cluster) that all app servers share, typically partitioned via consistent hashing so each node owns a slice of the keyspace.
  • Global cache — one centralized cache all nodes hit; simpler mental model, but the cache itself becomes a bottleneck/single point of failure unless it's also made highly available.
  • CDN — see 2.4; effectively a geographically-distributed cache for content.

Cache population strategies:

Strategy How it works Trade-off
Cache-aside (lazy loading) App checks cache; on a miss, reads from DB and populates the cache itself Only requested data is cached; a cache failure just degrades to slower reads, doesn't break writes
Read-through The cache library sits in front of the DB and populates itself transparently on a miss Simpler application code; cache and DB access are more tightly coupled
Write-through Every write goes to the cache and the DB synchronously Cache is always fresh, but every write pays the latency of both writes
Write-behind (write-back) Write hits the cache immediately, then is flushed to the DB asynchronously Fast writes, but a cache crash before flush can lose data
Write-around Write goes directly to the DB, bypassing the cache entirely Good for data written once and rarely re-read soon after (avoids polluting the cache with write-only data); the next read is a guaranteed miss
Refresh-ahead The cache proactively re-fetches a popular entry shortly before its TTL expires, rather than waiting for a request to trigger the refresh Keeps hot keys perpetually warm, directly preventing the thundering-herd scenario below — the cost is guessing which keys are worth refreshing speculatively

Eviction policies (what to remove when the cache is full): LRU (Least Recently Used — the default choice in most systems), LFU (Least Frequently Used — better when popularity is more stable than recency), FIFO, Random Replacement (simplest to implement, surprisingly competitive when access patterns are close to uniform).

Cache invalidation — the harder half of "there are only two hard problems in computer science": a TTL handles staleness passively; explicit invalidation (delete/update the cache entry the moment the source changes) handles it actively. Most production systems use both — a TTL as a safety net, explicit invalidation for anything user-visible and time-sensitive (a price change, a profile update).

The three named cache failure modes — asked about constantly, specifically because each looks identical from the outside ("the database is suddenly overloaded") but has a different root cause and fix:

Failure mode What's actually happening Fix
Cache penetration Requests repeatedly ask for a key that doesn't exist in the database at all, so every request is guaranteed to miss the cache and hit the DB — often malicious (probing for valid IDs) or a client bug Cache the negative result too (a short-TTL "not found" marker), and/or put a Bloom filter in front of the DB so a definite-absent key is rejected before it ever reaches storage
Cache breakdown (a.k.a. thundering herd, or dogpile) One specific hot key expires, and the flood of requests that were all being served from cache now all hit the database simultaneously for that one key A mutex/single-flight per key (the first request to miss recomputes the value; concurrent requests for the same key wait on that one in-flight computation instead of each starting their own), or refresh-ahead so the key never actually goes cold
Cache avalanche Many keys expire at (or close to) the same moment — often because they were all set with the same TTL at the same time, or because the cache node/cluster itself went down Randomize/jitter TTLs so keys don't expire in lockstep; keep a multi-tier cache (a second cache layer, or the origin behind a circuit breaker) so a cold cache degrades gracefully instead of transferring 100% of load to the database instantly

The hot-key problem is the more general version of cache breakdown, and shows up anywhere a partitioned system routes by key — not just caches: one key (a viral post, a celebrity's user ID, a popular product) receives wildly disproportionate traffic relative to every other key, so whichever single cache node or shard owns it becomes a bottleneck no matter how well the rest of the system is provisioned. The standard toolkit, roughly in order of how often each is reached for:

  • Local (in-process) caching of the single hottest few keys on every app server, so the vast majority of requests for that key never even reach the distributed cache tier.
  • Key salting / splitting — replicate the hot key's value under key:0, key:1, ... key:9 across multiple shards, and have readers pick a suffix at random, turning one overloaded owner into ten evenly-loaded ones (writes now have to fan out to all the copies, which is the trade-off).
  • Request coalescing (single-flight) — the same mechanism used for cache breakdown above, applied generally: when many concurrent requests need the same not-yet-cached value, only one actually computes it; the rest wait on that result rather than each hitting the backing store independently.

2.6Scalability

Vertical scaling (scale up) — add more CPU/RAM/disk to an existing machine. Simple (no code changes), but has a hard ceiling (physical hardware limits) and a non-linear cost curve (the biggest machines cost disproportionately more per unit of capacity), and represents a single point of failure until you also add redundancy.

Horizontal scaling (scale out) — add more machines. Roughly linear cost scaling and no hard ceiling, but requires the workload to be splittable (often meaning stateless services), and adds real complexity: you now need a load balancer, service discovery, and distributed debugging.

Vertical Horizontal
Implementation effort Low Higher — needs load balancing, coordination
Ceiling Hard limit (biggest available machine) Effectively unlimited
Fault tolerance Single point of failure Built-in redundancy — one node's death doesn't stop the system
Cost curve Non-linear, gets expensive fast Roughly linear

The Scale Cube (AKF) is a useful three-axis way to organize how you scale out, beyond just "add more machines": the X-axis is cloning — running identical copies of the whole application behind a load balancer, the simplest form of horizontal scaling. The Y-axis is functional decomposition — splitting by responsibility (this is what turns a monolith into microservices, Section 2.1). The Z-axis is data partitioning — splitting by some attribute of the data itself (this is sharding, immediately below). Most real systems eventually combine all three; naming the axis explicitly is a fast way to signal that a scaling decision is deliberate rather than "just add servers."

Database replication — keeping copies of data across multiple nodes for availability, read scaling, and durability.

  • Leader-follower (primary-replica) — one node accepts writes; followers replicate from it and can serve reads. Synchronous replication guarantees followers are caught up before a write is acknowledged (strong consistency, higher write latency); asynchronous acknowledges the write immediately and replicates in the background (lower latency, a window where a follower can lag — and can lose the most recent writes if the leader dies before replicating them).
  • Multi-leader — each data center (or node) has its own leader, replicating asynchronously to the others. Enables local low-latency writes in each region, at the cost of needing conflict resolution when two leaders accept conflicting writes concurrently.
  • Leaderless replication — no single leader; a client writes to (and reads from) multiple replicas directly, reconciling via a quorum (e.g., write to W replicas, read from R replicas, where W + R > total replicas guarantees overlap) and version metadata. Cassandra and Riak use this, propagating updates between replicas via a gossip protocol and resolving conflicting reads at read time (returning the most recent value and repairing stale replicas in the background).
  • Leader election for the failover case (promoting a new leader when the old one dies) is typically done via a consensus protocol — Raft, Paxos, or ZooKeeper's Zab — covered in 2.7.

Replication lag is a named problem, not just a vague "eventual consistency" wave of the hand — asynchronous leader-follower replication produces three specific, distinct user-facing symptoms, each with its own targeted fix, and naming which one a design is vulnerable to is worth more than saying "there might be some lag":

Symptom What the user sees Targeted fix
Read-after-write (read-your-own-writes) A user submits a change, then immediately reloads and doesn't see their own update, because the read hit a follower that hasn't caught up yet Route a user's reads of their own data to the leader (or a replica confirmed caught-up) for a short window right after they write — the same technique named in Section 2.11
Monotonic reads A user reloads a page twice in quick succession and the second load shows older data than the first, because the two reads happened to land on two different followers at two different lag amounts Pin one user's reads to the same replica consistently (e.g., hash the user ID to pick a replica), so they can never see time run backward even if the whole system is eventually consistent
Consistent prefix reads Replicated writes are applied out of causal order on a follower — an answer to a question appears to arrive before the question itself, because the two were written to different partitions and replicated independently Ensure causally-related writes are routed through the same partition/replication stream, so they're guaranteed to be applied everywhere in the order they actually happened

Sharding (horizontal partitioning) — splitting one logical dataset across many physical nodes, each holding a subset of the rows. Sharding is a last resort, not a first move — try purging old data, archiving to cold storage, or simple denormalization first, since sharding is genuinely hard to undo.

Sharding strategy How keys map to shards Strength Weakness
Key/hash-based hash(key) % N decides the shard Even distribution Adding/removing a shard reshuffles almost everything unless you use consistent hashing
Range-based Contiguous key ranges go to the same shard (e.g., usernames A–P, Q–Z) Simple, efficient range queries Easily creates hot shards if the key distribution is skewed
Directory-based A lookup service maps each key to its shard explicitly Most flexible — rebalancing is just updating the directory The directory itself is a new single point of failure/bottleneck
Vertical Split by column, not row — different attributes of the same entity live in different shards Lets you apply different consistency/replication rules to critical vs. non-critical columns Queries needing columns from multiple shards get expensive

Partitioning secondary indexes is the sharding question interviewers reach for once the primary-key case feels solved, precisely because the obvious answer ("just shard like before") doesn't actually work — a secondary index (e.g., "find all cars where color = red," when the data is sharded by car_id) doesn't share the partitioning key, so it can't just live on one shard. Two real strategies, each with an opposite trade-off:

  • Document-partitioned (local) index — each shard maintains its own secondary index, covering only the data that shard already holds. Writes stay local and cheap (touch one shard), but a query has to scatter-gather — fan out to every shard, since the matching rows could be on any of them — which is exactly the scatter-gather pattern already named in the Web Crawler/search-engine and Leaderboard case studies (Part III), recurring here for the same underlying reason.
  • Term-partitioned (global) index — the secondary index itself is partitioned separately, by the indexed value rather than by the primary key (e.g., all color = red entries live together, on whatever shard owns that term, regardless of which shard the underlying car row lives on). Reads become efficient (query one or a few index shards directly, no scatter-gather), but writes get more expensive and slower — updating one row's indexed field means writing to a different, remote shard than the one holding the row itself, and that write typically can't be made part of the same local transaction, pushing the design toward eventual consistency between the row and its own index entry.

Rebalancing — redistributing data as shards are added/removed. Good rebalancing minimizes data movement, keeps the system available for reads/writes throughout, and results in a fair distribution afterward. Naive hash % N fails this badly (changing N reshuffles nearly every key) — which is exactly the problem consistent hashing exists to solve.

Choosing a shard key is arguably the single highest-stakes decision in this whole section, since it's expensive to change later. A good shard key has: high cardinality (enough distinct values to spread across many shards — a boolean is_active column makes a terrible shard key, since it only ever produces two shards' worth of distribution); even distribution (no single value dominates the dataset); query locality (the application's actual queries can be answered by hitting one or a few shards, not by fanning out to all of them); and low hot-spot risk going forward, not just today. Common traps: a monotonically increasing key (an auto-incrementing ID, or a plain timestamp) concentrates all new writes onto whichever shard currently owns the highest range — every write lands on the same "hot" shard even though the key technically has high cardinality; and a field that's frequently used as an exact-match filter in queries (like country for a global product with a US-heavy user base) can look high-cardinality in theory while being wildly skewed in practice.

Consistent hashing — the single most-tested HLD concept across every major course. Servers and keys are both hashed onto positions on a conceptual ring (0 to 2^32, typically); a key belongs to the first server found walking clockwise from its position. When a server is added or removed, only the keys between it and its immediate neighbor on the ring move — everything else stays put. Compare this to naive modulo hashing, where adding one server reshuffles nearly the entire keyspace. In practice, each physical server is usually mapped to many points on the ring (virtual nodes) so that load stays balanced even with a small number of physical servers, and so that when one server fails, its load is spread across many other servers rather than dumped entirely on one neighbor. Used by Cassandra and DynamoDB (data partitioning), CDNs (routing to edge caches), and hash-based load balancers (session persistence).

2.7Distributed Systems

Why distributed systems exist: a single machine has hard limits on processing power, storage, and traffic it can absorb. Splitting work across many machines that cooperate to appear as one system to the end user buys you scalability (handle more load), reliability (keep working when a component fails), and often lower latency (place data close to users) — at the cost of a category of new problems single-machine systems never had.

The CAP theorem — in the presence of a network partition, a distributed system must choose between:

  • Consistency — every read reflects the most recent write (all nodes agree).
  • Availability — every request receives a (non-error) response, even if it isn't the most recent data.
  • Partition tolerance — the system keeps functioning despite dropped/delayed messages between nodes.

Because network partitions are a physical inevitability in any real distributed system, P is not optional — the real, practical choice is CP vs. AP when a partition occurs.

Choice Behavior during a partition Real examples
CP Reject requests that can't be guaranteed consistent MongoDB (configurable, but defaults toward consistency — rejects writes it can't safely replicate), most traditional banking ledgers
AP Keep answering with best-available (possibly stale) data Cassandra, DNS — a DNS resolver would rather answer with a slightly stale record than refuse to resolve a domain at all
CA Both — only really achievable when there's no partition to tolerate, i.e., a single node or single-datacenter system A non-distributed MySQL instance

A good interview answer names the business reason for the choice, not just the label: a bank balance transfer favors consistency (an incorrect balance is a real financial/legal problem); a social feed favors availability (a slightly stale like-count is invisible to the user, but an error page isn't).

PACELC extends CAP to cover the other 99.9% of the time — the moments when there isn't a partition happening at all, which CAP simply has nothing to say about. It reads: if there's a Partition, choose Availability or Consistency (that's plain CAP); Else (normal operation), choose Latency or Consistency. This matters because a system can be perfectly healthy, with no partition in sight, and still choose to serve a slightly-stale read from a nearby replica rather than pay the round-trip latency to confirm it against the leader — that's a PACELC trade-off, not a CAP one, and it's the more common decision a real system actually makes day to day. Naming PACELC specifically (rather than stretching CAP to cover it) is a small but reliable signal of depth.

Distributed clocks, briefly — never trust wall-clock timestamps alone to order events across machines, because clocks drift (clock skew) and NTP corrections can even move a clock backward. Lamport clocks provide a simple logical ordering (a counter incremented on every event, carried along with messages) sufficient to say "A happened before B," without capturing full causality between independent branches. Vector clocks (used in the Key-Value Store case study, Part III) extend this to detect concurrent, causally-unrelated writes, at the cost of a vector that grows with the number of nodes. Hybrid Logical Clocks (HLC) combine a physical timestamp with a logical counter, giving both real-world time meaning and safe ordering. Google Spanner's TrueTime takes a different approach entirely — atomic clocks and GPS receivers in every data center bound clock uncertainty to a few milliseconds, and Spanner deliberately waits out that uncertainty window before committing a transaction ("commit-wait"), which is what lets it offer external consistency across continents — an approach worth naming if a design question pushes into multi-region strong consistency, even without reproducing Spanner's implementation.

Consensus algorithms — how a group of nodes agrees on a single value (who's the leader, what's the next log entry) despite failures and message delays.

Algorithm Core idea Where you'll see it
Paxos The original provably-correct consensus protocol; notoriously difficult to implement correctly and to explain Google Chubby, historically foundational
Raft Designed explicitly to be more understandable than Paxos while providing the same guarantees — leader election + replicated log, with a clear "term" concept etcd, Consul, CockroachDB
ZAB (Zookeeper Atomic Broadcast) Raft-like leader-based protocol purpose-built for ZooKeeper's coordination workload Apache ZooKeeper

You don't need to implement any of these from scratch — you need to recognize when a design needs one at all (leader election after a node failure, keeping a replicated log consistent) and be able to name the tool that already solves it (ZooKeeper/etcd/Consul), rather than hand-rolling consensus in an interview.

What consensus is actually for, in one sentence: at the bottom of Raft, Paxos, and ZAB is the same abstraction, worth naming because it demystifies why "leader election" and "keep this replicated log consistent" turn out to be the same problem — total order broadcast, the guarantee that every node delivers the same messages in the same order. A replicated log where every node agrees on "entry 5, then entry 6, then entry 7" is total order broadcast; an elected leader is just a convenient way to decide that order without every node needing to negotiate it from scratch on every single message.

Byzantine faults — everything in this guide so far assumes a node either works correctly or stops (a crash fault); a Byzantine fault is a node that keeps running but sends incorrect or actively malicious messages — lying about its state, forging messages from other nodes. Standard consensus protocols (Raft, Paxos, ZAB) do not tolerate this — they assume every node that's still talking is being honest, just possibly slow or out of date. This matters almost exclusively in adversarial or safety-critical settings without a trusted operator (blockchain/cryptocurrency consensus, aircraft control systems) — worth knowing the term exists and that it's a genuinely different, harder problem than the crash-fault tolerance this entire guide otherwise assumes, but rarely worth designing for in a typical backend system where you control every node.

Process pauses deserve to be named as their own category of failure, distinct from a crash: a process can appear dead (missing heartbeats, holding a lock past its TTL) and then simply resume, having no idea any time passed — caused by a GC pause, a hypervisor live-migrating a VM, laptop lid closure during local testing masking the issue, or heavy disk I/O blocking a thread. The dangerous part isn't the pause itself, it's that the paused process cannot tell it was paused — from its own point of view, no time passed at all, so it wakes up and confidently continues exactly where it left off, potentially long after everyone else has decided it was dead and moved on without it. This is precisely why the fencing-token mechanism below exists — the protection has to come from the resource the process is about to act on, since the process itself has no reliable way to know its own assumptions are stale.

Distributed locks — coordinating exclusive access to a resource across multiple processes/machines (not just threads on one box). Typically implemented via Redis (SET key value NX PX ttl — set only if not already present, with an expiry so a crashed lock-holder can't wedge the resource forever) or ZooKeeper (ephemeral sequential nodes). The TTL is the whole design problem in miniature: too short, and a slow-but-alive process loses its lock mid-operation to another process; too long, and a genuinely crashed process blocks everyone else until it expires.

Two refinements come up in almost every deeper follow-up:

  • Release safety — never release a lock with a blind DEL; a lock-holder whose TTL just expired might DEL a lock that a different process has since legitimately acquired. The safe release is a small Lua script that checks the stored value still matches this holder's unique token before deleting — an atomic "delete only if I'm still the owner."
  • Fencing tokens — the TTL problem above (a process pauses for longer than the TTL — a GC pause is the classic culprit — and loses the lock without realizing it, then resumes and writes anyway) can't be fixed by the lock alone. The fix is a monotonically increasing fencing token returned on every acquisition; the protected resource itself rejects any write carrying a token older than the newest one it's already seen, so a "zombie" holder that wakes up after losing the lock gets rejected downstream even though it still thinks it holds the lock.

Redlock is Redis's own proposal for making a lock more robust: acquire the same lock across N independent Redis instances and require a majority, with a validity window shortened by however long acquisition took. It's worth knowing the name — and worth knowing that it's genuinely contested: Martin Kleppmann's widely-cited critique argues Redlock's safety depends on timing assumptions (bounded clock drift, bounded pauses) that a GC pause or a slow network can violate in practice, and that a lock's real safety should come from fencing tokens validated by the resource, not from the locking protocol's timing alone. Citing both sides — "Redlock for best-effort coordination where the cost of a rare double-acquisition is low; a consensus-backed store like ZooKeeper/etcd, plus fencing tokens, when correctness genuinely can't bend" — reads as more senior than picking one without acknowledging the debate exists.

Idempotency — an operation that produces the same end result no matter how many times it's retried. This matters enormously in distributed systems because at-least-once delivery is often the best guarantee you can get cheaply — a network timeout doesn't tell you whether the server actually processed the request before failing to reply, so clients retry, and the server needs to handle "I've seen this exact request before" gracefully (typically via a client-generated idempotency key attached to the request, checked against a dedupe table before executing).

Distributed transactions — committing a change atomically across multiple services/databases. Two-Phase Commit (2PC) is the classical approach (a coordinator asks all participants to "prepare," then tells everyone to "commit" only if all said yes) but it blocks if the coordinator dies mid-protocol, so it's rare in modern web-scale systems. Three-Phase Commit (3PC) adds a non-blocking timeout step to fix that specific failure mode, at the cost of more round trips — worth naming as "the thing that fixes 2PC's blocking problem" if asked, but rarely deployed in practice either. TCC (Try-Confirm-Cancel) is a middle ground closer to application logic than protocol: a Try phase tentatively reserves resources (hold funds, hold inventory) without committing, a Confirm phase finalizes if every participant's Try succeeded, and a Cancel phase releases the reservations if any participant failed — conceptually similar to the seat-hold pattern in the Ticket Booking case study (Part III), generalized across services. The Saga pattern (Section 2.1) remains the practical default almost everyone reaches for now — trading strict atomicity for a sequence of compensable local transactions — with TCC as the answer when you specifically need the reservation held (not yet committed) during the in-between window, rather than a compensating action after the fact.

2.8Messaging & Events

A message queue decouples producers from consumers in time — a producer publishes work and moves on; a consumer picks it up whenever it's ready. This buys resilience (a consumer outage doesn't lose work, it just backs up) and lets each side scale independently.

Queue vs. topic (pub/sub):

Model Delivery Example
Queue (point-to-point) Each message is delivered to exactly one consumer AWS SQS, Kafka "queue" consumption
Topic (pub/sub, broadcast) Each message is delivered to every subscriber AWS SNS, Kafka topics read by multiple independent consumer groups

A common pattern combines both: publish once to a topic, and let each downstream concern (send an email, update a search index, trigger analytics) consume that same event independently through its own queue — this is exactly the SNS-fans-out-to-SQS-queues pattern, and the general shape of most event-driven order-processing pipelines.

Failure modes queueing systems have to plan for, explicitly:

Problem Fix
Producers faster than consumers Add more consumers; apply backpressure to producers; as a last resort, drop low-priority messages
Broker crashes Durability (persist messages to disk before acking the producer); run multiple brokers so one crash doesn't lose the queue
Head-of-line blocking One slow/stuck message blocks everything behind it in its partition — mitigate with per-key parallel partitions, or move poison messages aside quickly
Poison messages (a message that always fails processing) Route to a dead-letter queue (DLQ) after N failed attempts, so it stops blocking the healthy stream and can be inspected/replayed manually
Duplicate delivery Idempotent consumers (Section 2.7) — most brokers guarantee only at-least-once delivery, not exactly-once

Retry strategy: exponential backoff (wait longer between each retry) with jitter (randomize the wait slightly) to avoid every failed client retrying in lockstep and re-creating the exact same spike that caused the failure ("thundering herd").

Apache Kafka, specifically, is worth knowing at the component level since it shows up constantly as "the" answer to event streaming:

  • Topic — a named, append-only log of records.
  • Partition — a topic is split into partitions for parallelism; each partition is a strictly ordered log (ordering is only guaranteed within a partition, not across the whole topic).
  • Broker — a server that stores partitions and serves producer/consumer requests; brokers form a cluster.
  • Replication — each partition has a configurable number of replicas across brokers; one is the leader (handles all reads/writes for that partition), the rest are followers. The set of replicas fully caught up with the leader is the In-Sync Replica (ISR) set; a message is only considered "committed" once all ISR members have it, which is what lets Kafka survive a broker failure without losing acknowledged data.
  • Consumer group — a set of consumers that split a topic's partitions between them, so each partition is read by exactly one consumer in the group at a time (this is how Kafka parallelizes consumption while keeping per-partition order).
  • Offset — each consumer group tracks its own position (offset) in each partition, which is what lets a consumer restart and resume exactly where it left off.

At LinkedIn's scale (Kafka's birthplace), this design routinely moves over a trillion messages a day — the throughput comes from the append-only log structure (sequential disk writes are fast) and partitioning (parallelism), not from any single clever trick.

2.9Reliability

  • High availability (HA) — designing so the system keeps serving requests despite individual component failures, usually measured in "nines" (99.9% = ~8.7 hours of downtime/year; 99.99% = ~52 minutes/year; 99.999% = ~5 minutes/year).
  • Fault vs. failure — a fault is a component deviating from expected behavior (a slow disk, a buggy branch of code); a failure is the user-visible consequence when faults aren't contained (the whole service goes down). Good design stops faults from cascading into failures.
  • Redundancy — no single instance of anything critical; N+1 (or more) of every critical component.
  • Failover — automatically switching to a standby/replica when the primary fails; requires health checks and (usually) the consensus/leader-election machinery from Section 2.7.
  • Disaster recovery — the plan for a large-scale failure (a whole region going down): defined by RPO (Recovery Point Objective — how much data you can afford to lose, driving your replication/backup frequency) and RTO (Recovery Time Objective — how fast you must be back up, driving whether you need a hot standby region or can tolerate restoring from backup).
  • Backups — regular, tested, and ideally stored in a geographically separate location and encrypted at rest; untested backups are a false sense of security, not a real safety net.
  • Graceful degradation — when a dependency fails, serve a reduced experience instead of an error (cached/stale recommendations instead of none, a "comments temporarily unavailable" banner instead of a broken page) — directly enabled by the Circuit Breaker pattern from Section 2.1.
  • Load shedding — under extreme load, deliberately reject a portion of incoming requests early and cheaply (before they consume real resources) so the requests that are accepted can still be served well, rather than letting every request in and having all of them time out together.
  • Chaos engineering — deliberately injecting failure into a production or production-like system (killing instances, adding latency, dropping network connections) to verify that redundancy and graceful degradation actually work, rather than assuming they do until a real outage proves otherwise. Netflix's Chaos Monkey (Part III, Group D) is the best-known example, but the practice generalizes well beyond Netflix: the point is that resilience you haven't tested is a hypothesis, not a property.

2.10Performance

  • Latency — time for a single operation to complete. Report it as a distribution, not an average: p50 (median, typical experience), p95/p99 (tail latency — the requests that are actually painful), because averages hide exactly the outliers users complain about. SLAs are almost always written in percentile terms for this reason.
  • Throughput — work completed per unit time (requests/sec, messages/sec). Latency and throughput trade off against each other under load — batching improves throughput at the cost of per-item latency.
  • Caching — see 2.5; the highest-leverage performance lever in most designs, precisely because of the latency table in Part 0 (memory vs. disk vs. cross-region network).
  • Database optimization — indexing the right columns (see below), denormalizing read-heavy tables, using read replicas to offload reads from the writer, and connection pooling.
  • Asynchronous processing — move anything not required for the immediate response (sending a confirmation email, generating a thumbnail, updating analytics) off the request path and onto a queue, so the user-facing latency reflects only the work that actually needs to happen synchronously.
  • Connection pooling — reuse a small set of already-open database/network connections instead of paying TCP + TLS handshake cost per request; almost every production ORM/driver does this by default, but it's worth naming explicitly as a cost you're avoiding.

Database indexing — an index is an auxiliary data structure mapping column values to row locations, so a lookup doesn't require scanning every row.

Concept What it means
B-Tree / B+Tree The default index structure in most relational databases — balanced, so lookups are logarithmic in row count; B+Trees additionally chain all data at the leaf level, making range scans (WHERE x BETWEEN a AND b) efficient
Clustered index The table's rows are physically stored in index order (usually on the primary key) — a table can have at most one
Non-clustered (secondary) index A separate structure pointing back to the row's actual location — a table can have many
Composite index An index over multiple columns together — column order matters; it only helps queries that filter on a left-anchored prefix of the indexed columns
Hash index The simplest index worth knowing by name (the mechanism behind Bitcask/Riak's storage engine): an in-memory hash map from key to the byte offset of that key's most recent value in an on-disk, append-only log. Writes are a single sequential append plus a hash-map update — about as fast as writing gets — but the entire key set must fit in memory, and range queries ("all keys between X and Y") aren't supported at all, only exact-match lookups
LSM Tree (Log-Structured Merge-Tree) The write-optimized alternative to a B-Tree, and the default storage engine in Cassandra, RocksDB, and LevelDB. Writes never modify data in place: they're appended to an in-memory memtable (backed by a write-ahead log for crash recovery), and once the memtable fills up it's flushed to disk as an immutable, sorted SSTable. A background compaction process periodically merges SSTables, discarding overwritten/deleted values. This makes writes extremely fast (always sequential, never a random-access update) at the cost of read amplification — a read may need to check the memtable and several SSTables before finding the latest value, which is why LSM-based stores lean heavily on Bloom filters per SSTable to skip the ones that definitely don't contain a given key
Inverted index Term → list of documents containing it — the structure behind full-text search (Elasticsearch/Lucene), unrelated to B-Trees entirely
Geospatial index R-tree, Geohash, or a hierarchical grid (Quadtree/S2/H3) — see the Design Uber case study (Part III) for the full comparison

Trade-off to say out loud: indexes make reads faster but make writes slower (every insert/update/delete has to update every index on that table too) and cost extra storage — so you index columns that are frequently filtered/sorted/joined on, not every column reflexively. At a higher level, B-Tree vs. LSM-Tree is itself a read/write trade-off: pick a B-Tree-based engine (Postgres, MySQL/InnoDB) when reads dominate and you need efficient range scans; pick an LSM-based engine when you're write-heavy and can tolerate slightly slower, Bloom-filter-assisted reads.

2.11Consistency & Data Patterns

  • Strong consistency (linearizability) — every read reflects the latest committed write, everywhere, immediately, and all operations appear to happen in one single, real-time-ordered sequence. Simple to reason about, expensive to provide across regions.
  • Eventual consistency — replicas converge to the same value eventually, with no fixed bound on when. Cheaper and more available, but requires the application (or the user) to tolerate a window of staleness.
  • BASE (the NoSQL-world counterpart to ACID) — Basically Available, Soft state, Eventual consistency — a deliberate naming joke, and a genuinely different design philosophy: favor availability and accept that the system's state may be "soft" (in flux) between writes.

Between those two extremes sits a spectrum of session guarantees worth naming precisely, because they let you promise a user something meaningfully stronger than plain eventual consistency without paying for full linearizability everywhere:

Model The guarantee Typical use
Causal consistency Operations that are causally related (a reply to a comment) are seen by everyone in the same order; unrelated operations can be seen in any order Comment threads, chat — a reply should never appear before the message it's replying to
Read-your-writes A user always sees their own writes immediately, even if other users see them later A user editing their own profile shouldn't see their old data reappear on refresh
Monotonic reads Once a user has seen a value, they never see an older value on a subsequent read (even if routed to a different, lagging replica) Prevents a refresh from appearing to "undo" data the user already saw
Bounded staleness Data is guaranteed to be no more than some fixed time (or number of writes) out of date Dashboards and analytics views that don't need real-time freshness but do need an upper bound on lag

A common, practical technique for read-your-writes specifically: route a user's own reads to the leader (or a replica confirmed caught-up) for a short window right after they write, and fall back to any replica afterward.

CQRS (Command Query Responsibility Segregation) — split the model that handles writes (commands) from the model that handles reads (queries), letting each be optimized independently — a write model normalized for correctness, a read model denormalized/indexed purely for fast retrieval, kept in sync asynchronously. Read and write load are rarely equal (most systems are far more read-heavy), so scaling them together under one model wastes effort. Cost: the read side lags the write side by some small window (eventual consistency again), and you now have two models to keep conceptually aligned.

Event sourcing — instead of storing only the current state, store the full sequence of state-changing events; current state is derived by replaying them. A bank balance isn't a stored number — it's the sum of every deposit and withdrawal event, replayed. Benefits: a complete audit trail for free, the ability to reconstruct state as of any point in time (invaluable for debugging and compliance), and natural pairing with CQRS (the event log is the write model; a projection built from it is the read model). Cost: replaying a long event history to reconstruct current state gets expensive without periodic snapshots, and the storage grows unboundedly without an archiving strategy.

How event sourcing differs from plain CRUD, concretely: in CRUD, reducing an inventory count from 10 to 7 overwrites the 10 — the fact that a reduction of 3 happened is gone. In event sourcing, you store stock: 10 then decremented by 3, so the why and when survive, not just the what.

2.12Security

Authentication (who are you) vs. authorization (what can you do) — a distinction worth stating explicitly in an interview, because the mechanisms differ.

Access-control models:

Model How permissions are decided
ACL (Access Control List) An explicit list of who can do what to a specific resource
RBAC (Role-Based Access Control) Permissions attach to roles; users are assigned roles
ABAC (Attribute-Based Access Control) Decisions consider dynamic attributes — user department, resource sensitivity, time of day, location

JWT (JSON Web Token) — a common way to carry authentication/authorization claims statelessly. Three base64-encoded parts: header (algorithm + token type), payload (claims — sub subject, exp expiration, custom claims), signature (HMAC or RSA over the header+payload, so tampering is detectable). Because a valid signature proves the claims weren't altered, a server can trust a JWT without a database lookup on every request — the trade-off is that a JWT can't be easily "revoked" before its expiry without extra infrastructure (a blocklist, or short expiries plus a refresh-token flow).

OAuth 2.0 and OIDC — OAuth 2.0 is an authorization framework (letting an app act on a user's behalf without seeing their password — "log in with Google"); OpenID Connect (OIDC) is a thin identity layer built on top of OAuth 2.0 that adds actual authentication (an ID token asserting who the user is), which is why "OAuth 2.0 / OIDC" is usually named together.

Encryption:

  • At rest — data encrypted on disk, so a stolen drive or improperly-accessed backup isn't readable.
  • In transit — TLS on every network hop, including internal service-to-service traffic in a zero-trust design, not just the public-facing edge.
  • Key management — the practical hard part: secure generation, storage (a dedicated secrets manager, never hardcoded or committed to source), rotation, and access-scoping of encryption keys.
  • Password hashing specifically is worth calling out by name rather than lumping into "encryption" generally, since it's a distinct, frequently-checked detail: passwords are never encrypted (reversible) or hashed with a fast, general-purpose algorithm like MD5/SHA-1/SHA-256 (crackable via rainbow tables and brute-force at GPU speed). The correct answer is a slow, purpose-built password hash with per-user salting — bcrypt or Argon2 (the more modern, memory-hard choice, and the current recommended default) — deliberately expensive to compute so brute-forcing every possible password is impractical even after a database leak.
  • Tokenization, specifically in payment contexts (Part III, Group F): replace a sensitive value (a card number) with a non-sensitive token at the earliest possible point, so the real value never touches your own database or logs at all — the token is meaningless to anyone who steals it, and only the tokenization provider (or a narrowly-scoped vault) can map it back. This is the concrete mechanism behind "PCI-DSS scope reduction" — if your systems never store the real card number, most of PCI-DSS's strictest requirements simply don't apply to them.

Network security layers: firewalls (rule-based traffic filtering at the network boundary), WAF (Web Application Firewall) (inspects application-layer HTTP traffic for attack patterns — SQLi, XSS, the categories the OWASP Top 10 catalogs as the most common real-world web-application vulnerabilities, worth naming as the standard reference if a design question drifts toward application-level security specifically), IDPS (Intrusion Detection/Prevention System) (monitors for anomalous behavior in real time), VPN (encrypted tunnel for remote/internal access).

Zero Trust — the modern default mental model, replacing "trusted internal network, untrusted outside": never trust, always verify, regardless of whether a request originates inside or outside the network perimeter. Every request is authenticated and authorized on its own merits, service-to-service included — which is exactly why a service mesh's mTLS-everywhere approach (Section 2.15) has become standard rather than optional.

Compliance — GDPR (EU data protection and privacy), HIPAA (US health data), PCI-DSS (payment card data). These aren't just legal checkboxes to mention — they impose concrete design constraints (data residency, right-to-erasure implying you need to actually be able to delete a user's data across every system that touched it, audit logging of who accessed what).

2.13Observability

The three pillars, and what each one is actually good for:

Pillar Answers Tooling examples
Logs "What exactly happened, in detail, for this one request/event?" ELK/Elasticsearch, structured JSON logs
Metrics "How is the system behaving in aggregate, over time?" (CPU, latency percentiles, error rate, queue depth) Prometheus + Grafana, Datadog
Traces "As this one request crossed 12 microservices, where did the time go, and where did it fail?" Jaeger, Zipkin

OpenTelemetry has become the vendor-neutral standard for instrumenting all three, so an application emits data in a common format regardless of which backend (Datadog, Grafana, Honeycomb) ultimately visualizes it — worth naming specifically, since it replaced a fragmented, vendor-specific landscape.

Alerts are built on metrics, but the design decision that matters is what to alert on: alert on symptoms your users would notice (error rate, latency percentile breach) rather than every possible internal cause, or you'll train your on-call rotation to ignore alerts.

Two dashboard conventions worth naming rather than reinventing per-project: the RED method (Rate, Errors, Duration) for services — how many requests, how many failed, how long did they take; and the USE method (Utilization, Saturation, Errors) for resources — how busy is this CPU/disk/queue, is work queuing up waiting for it, and is it throwing errors. Reaching for one of these by name is a fast way to structure "what should this dashboard show" without designing a monitoring taxonomy from scratch.

SLI / SLO / SLA — a chain of increasingly formal commitments: an SLI (Service Level Indicator) is the actual measured metric (e.g., "p99 latency"); an SLO (Objective) is your internal target for that metric ("p99 < 300ms for 99.9% of the month"); an SLA (Agreement) is the externally-facing, often contractual promise to customers, usually with a looser target than the internal SLO so you have margin to fix problems before breaching the customer-facing promise. The error budget is just 100% − SLO, expressed as an allowance rather than a target — a 99.9% SLO means a 0.1% error budget, and teams commonly use "how much of this month's error budget is left" as the actual, numeric input to a real decision (keep shipping features, or freeze releases and spend the week on reliability work).

2.14Infrastructure

  • Containers — package an application with its dependencies into a portable, isolated unit (Docker being the dominant runtime); solves "works on my machine" by shipping the machine's relevant slice along with the code.
  • Kubernetes (K8s) — orchestrates containers across a cluster of machines: scheduling (deciding which node runs which container), self-healing (restarting crashed containers, rescheduling off failed nodes), scaling (horizontal pod autoscaling based on load), service discovery, and rolling updates.
  • Cloud platforms (AWS, Azure, GCP) — provide the managed building blocks (compute, storage, databases, queues, load balancers) most of this guide's components map directly onto, so you rarely build them from scratch in practice.
  • Infrastructure as Code (IaC) — define infrastructure in version-controlled config (Terraform, Pulumi, CloudFormation) rather than clicking through a console, so environments are reproducible and changes are reviewable.
  • CI/CD — automated build/test/deploy pipelines; enables the frequent small releases that make rollback (rather than heroic debugging) the default response to a bad deploy. Deployment strategies worth naming: blue-green (two full environments, switch traffic atomically, instant rollback), canary (roll out to a small traffic slice first, watch metrics, expand gradually), rolling update (replace instances gradually, old and new versions coexist briefly). Feature flags solve a related but distinct problem — they decouple deploying code from releasing a feature, letting new code ship to production dark (inactive) and then be switched on gradually, per-user or per-percentage, without a second deployment; this is what makes it possible to roll a feature back instantly (flip the flag) even when rolling back the deployment itself would be slower or riskier.
  • Service mesh — a dedicated infrastructure layer (typically sidecar-based, Section 2.1) handling service-to-service traffic concerns — mTLS, retries, circuit breaking, observability — outside application code. Istio (built on the Envoy proxy) is the canonical example; it's what "sidecar pattern, applied consistently across every service in a cluster" looks like in production.

2.15Modern AI Systems

This is the fastest-moving section in system design prep, and the one most likely to be missing from courses even a year or two old. Every major 2026 course update (Grokking, Hello Interview, and the standalone GenAI-focused guides) now treats it as a core topic, not an appendix — interviewers at AI-forward companies increasingly ask "design a customer-support bot" or "design ChatGPT" alongside the classic case studies.

RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) — grounds an LLM's answers in your own data by retrieving relevant context and injecting it into the prompt before generation, rather than relying purely on what the model memorized during training. The pipeline, end to end:

  1. Offline (indexing) path — documents are split into chunks (sized to balance context relevance against noise — bigger chunks carry more context but dilute relevance), each chunk is converted to an embedding (a dense vector capturing semantic meaning) by an embedding model, and stored in a vector database.
  2. Online (query) path — the user's query is embedded the same way, the vector database returns the most semantically similar chunks (via approximate nearest-neighbor search), an optional re-ranker reorders candidates for relevance, and the top results are inserted into the prompt sent to the LLM alongside the user's question.

Vector databases — purpose-built for high-dimensional similarity search at scale (Pinecone, Weaviate, Milvus, or pgvector as a Postgres extension for teams who'd rather not run a new datastore). The core operation is approximate nearest-neighbor (ANN) search, trading a small amount of recall for large speedups over exact search, since exact nearest-neighbor search doesn't scale past a fairly small corpus.

Hybrid search — combining dense (embedding-based/semantic) retrieval with sparse (traditional keyword, e.g., BM25) retrieval, because pure semantic search can miss exact-match needs (product SKUs, error codes, proper nouns) that keyword search catches trivially.

AI agents — an LLM given tools (function calls, code execution, web/API access) and a loop that lets it plan, act, observe the result, and decide the next step — as opposed to a single prompt-in/answer-out call. Design considerations that come up repeatedly: a tool sandbox (agents executing code or hitting APIs need containment so a bad plan can't do real damage), a step/iteration cap (to bound cost and prevent infinite loops), and human-escalation paths for low-confidence or high-stakes actions.

Model serving — deploying a trained model to handle production inference traffic: batching requests to use GPU capacity efficiently, model quantization (trading some precision for lower latency/cost), routing (a fast small model for easy queries, a larger model only when needed), and semantic caching (cache by embedding-similarity to a prior query, not just exact string match, since two differently-phrased questions can have the same answer).

AI-specific observability: track token usage per user/session (it's the direct cost driver), p50/p95/p99 generation latency, vector-retrieval hit rate, and a golden dataset of representative Q&A pairs for offline regression testing whenever the underlying model, prompt, or retrieval pipeline changes — the AI-era equivalent of a unit-test suite, since LLM outputs aren't deterministic enough to unit-test in the traditional sense.

Evaluation — because you can't just "assert equals" an LLM's output, evaluation typically combines automated metrics (RAGAS-style scores for retrieval relevance and answer faithfulness) with a fixed golden dataset run on every change, plus online signals (thumbs up/down, escalation rate) once shipped.

Guardrails and safety — input/output filtering for the specific risks generative systems introduce: leaking system prompts or secrets, producing unsafe or off-brand content, and prompt injection (malicious instructions smuggled into retrieved documents or tool outputs, attempting to hijack the agent's behavior) — worth naming explicitly if your design retrieves or acts on untrusted external content.

2.16Design Principles

A closing set of engineering values that don't map to one component, but should visibly inform every choice you make in the rest of the interview:

Principle In practice
KISS (Keep It Simple) Prefer the boring, well-understood solution unless the requirements genuinely demand more — over-engineering for hypothetical future scale is a common interview mistake
DRY (Don't Repeat Yourself) Shared logic lives in one place — at the architecture level, this is part of the case for extracting a common service rather than copy-pasting logic into three microservices
Loose coupling Components interact through stable interfaces/contracts, not by reaching into each other's internals — the property that makes independent deployment and independent scaling possible at all
Separation of concerns Each component/module owns one concern — this is Single Responsibility (Section 1.3) applied at the system level, not just the class level
Stateless services Any instance can handle any request, since no request-specific state is held in server memory between calls — the property that makes horizontal scaling and load balancing simple; session state, if needed, goes in a shared store (cache/DB), not in-process
Defense in depth Layer multiple independent security controls (network, application, data) so a single control's failure doesn't mean total compromise
Graceful degradation See Section 2.9 — a reduced experience beats an error page
Design for failure Assume every component will fail eventually, and design so that failure is contained and recoverable rather than surprising — the mindset underlying redundancy, circuit breakers, and retries throughout this entire Part II
The end-to-end argument A correctness guarantee (data wasn't corrupted, a message wasn't duplicated) is only ever as strong as the check made by the two parties who actually care about it — the application at each end — because any guarantee provided only by a layer in the middle (the network, a single hop's retry logic) can't see what happens before or after its own boundary. TCP's checksums don't mean an application can skip its own integrity checks; a broker's at-least-once delivery doesn't mean a consumer can skip idempotency (Section 2.7) — the middle can help, but the endpoints are where correctness is actually enforced

2.17Data Processing at Scale

A category of its own, distinct from the request-serving path the rest of Part II focuses on — this is about moving and transforming large volumes of data between systems, usually off the user-facing critical path entirely. It comes up whenever a design needs analytics, ML features, search-index freshness, or any "recompute this over everything we have" workload.

  • Batch processing — process a large, bounded, already-accumulated dataset on a schedule (hourly, nightly). MapReduce is the foundational concept (map: transform each record independently and in parallel; reduce: aggregate the mapped results by key); Spark generalizes it with in-memory processing (far faster than MapReduce's disk-based shuffle for iterative workloads) and a richer set of operations.
  • Stream processing — process events continuously, as they arrive, with sub-second to few-second latency instead of waiting for a batch window. Flink and Kafka Streams are the standard engines; both are built around event time (when something actually happened) vs. processing time (when your pipeline saw it) — a distinction that matters the moment events can arrive late or out of order (a mobile client offline for an hour, then reconnecting and flushing a backlog). Windowing (tumbling — fixed, non-overlapping; sliding — overlapping; session — grouped by gaps in activity) defines how a continuous stream gets bucketed into finite pieces to aggregate over, and watermarks are the mechanism a stream processor uses to decide "I've probably seen all the events for this window now, even accounting for some lateness" — trading a bit of latency for the ability to eventually close a window and emit a result at all.
  • Lambda vs. Kappa architecture — two competing answers to "how do I get both fast approximate results and eventually-correct exact results from the same data." Lambda architecture runs two parallel pipelines against the same raw data: a batch layer (slow, exact, reprocesses everything) and a speed layer (fast, approximate, covers the most recent data not yet processed by the batch layer); a serving layer merges both views. The well-known cost is maintaining two independent codebases that need to agree. Kappa architecture is the simplification: treat everything, including historical data, as a replayable stream (Kafka's log retention makes this practical), and use one stream-processing pipeline for both real-time and reprocessing — rerunning history is just replaying the log from an earlier offset. Kappa is the more common answer in new designs specifically because it avoids the two-codebases problem; Lambda still comes up when a genuinely different algorithm is needed for the exact, offline pass than is feasible in real time.
  • Data lakes vs. data warehouses vs. lakehouses — a data lake (raw files, often Parquet, on object storage) stores data in its original form cheaply, schema-on-read; a data warehouse (Redshift, BigQuery, Snowflake) stores structured, transformed data optimized for fast SQL analytics, schema-on-write; a lakehouse (Delta Lake, Apache Iceberg) is the newer attempt to get the warehouse's transactional/query guarantees directly on top of lake-style cheap object storage, avoiding the duplicate "lake for raw, warehouse for query" pipeline.
  • ETL vs. ELT — Extract-Transform-Load transforms data before it lands in the destination (traditional, when the destination is expensive/limited); Extract-Load-Transform loads raw data first and transforms it afterward, inside the destination itself — practical now that warehouses/lakehouses are cheap and powerful enough to do the transformation step themselves, and increasingly the default for new pipelines.
  • CDC as the batch/stream bridge — Section 2.1's Change Data Capture is the most common source feeding both these worlds: a CDC stream can feed a real-time pipeline directly, and/or land continuously in a lake/warehouse as an always-current replacement for a nightly extract job.
  • Orchestration — batch and stream pipelines both need something to schedule, sequence, and retry multi-step jobs with dependencies between them (Airflow, Dagster, Temporal) — worth naming as its own layer rather than assuming pipelines run themselves.

2.18Transactions & Concurrency Control

Section 2.5 defined ACID at a high level. This section is the part most HLD-only prep skips and most backend-heavy interviews (and almost any "we had a data corruption bug in production" conversation) actually probe: what a database transaction promises is a spectrum, not a single guarantee, and knowing exactly which point on that spectrum you're standing on is what separates "I called BEGIN TRANSACTION" from actually understanding what can still go wrong.

Why isolation is a spectrum at all: true serializability — transactions behaving as if they ran one at a time, in some order, with zero concurrency — is expensive to implement efficiently, so almost every production database offers weaker isolation levels that allow more concurrency at the cost of specific, well-catalogued anomalies. The whole game is knowing which anomalies each level still permits, because "I set isolation level X" is meaningless without knowing what X actually stops.

The anomalies, defined precisely (these are the vocabulary the levels below are graded against):

Anomaly What happens
Dirty read Transaction A reads a row that transaction B has written but not yet committed — if B then rolls back, A read a value that never really existed
Dirty write Transaction A overwrites a value that transaction B wrote but hasn't committed yet — can interleave unrelated fields from two transactions into one corrupted record
Read skew (non-repeatable read) Transaction A reads the same row twice and gets two different values, because transaction B committed a change in between — a problem specifically for anything that reads multiple related things and expects them to be mutually consistent (e.g., reading two account balances that should sum to a constant)
Lost update Transactions A and B both read the same value, both compute a new value based on it, and both write back — whichever writes second silently overwrites the first's update, and the first update is gone with no error raised
Write skew Two transactions both read an overlapping set of rows, then each writes to a different row based on what they read — individually each write looks valid, but the combination violates an invariant neither transaction could see on its own
Phantom read Transaction A runs a query with a WHERE condition; transaction B inserts a new row that would have matched that condition and commits; if A re-runs the same query, a row appears that wasn't there before — a special case of write skew where the conflict is over rows that don't exist yet

The isolation levels, and exactly what each one stops:

Level Prevents Still allows Typical mechanism
Read Committed Dirty reads, dirty writes Read skew, lost updates, write skew, phantoms Row-level locks held only for the duration of a write; reads simply never see uncommitted data
Snapshot Isolation (often called Repeatable Read) Dirty reads/writes, read skew Lost updates by default (needs an explicit fix — below), write skew, phantoms MVCC (Multi-Version Concurrency Control) — each transaction reads from a consistent snapshot of the database as it was at the moment the transaction started, so it never sees concurrent commits at all, rather than being blocked by them
Serializable Everything, including write skew and phantoms Nothing — transactions behave as if executed one at a time Actual serial execution (run all transactions on a single thread — viable now that RAM is large enough to hold entire working sets, as used by early single-threaded Redis and VoltDB), Two-Phase Locking (2PL) (transactions take shared/exclusive locks and hold all of them until commit — strong but kills concurrency and is prone to deadlock), or Serializable Snapshot Isolation (SSI) (start from cheap MVCC snapshots, optimistically allow transactions to proceed, and abort-and-retry only the ones that a runtime check detects would have violated serializability — the modern default, since it gets serializable correctness at close to snapshot-isolation performance for most workloads)

MVCC, mechanically: rather than locking rows for reads, the database keeps multiple versions of each row (tagged with the transaction ID that created them) and gives every transaction a consistent view of "every row as committed before I started." This is why snapshot isolation is so much more concurrency-friendly than lock-based read committed — readers never block writers and writers never block readers, since they're simply looking at different, independently-valid versions of the data.

Preventing lost updates specifically (since snapshot isolation alone doesn't stop them): use an atomic compare-and-set / conditional update (UPDATE ... WHERE version = ?, the same primitive from the Ticket Booking and E-commerce case studies in Part III), an explicit SELECT ... FOR UPDATE row lock, or let the database's automatic conflict detection abort one of the two transactions and have the application retry it.

Write skew is the one that surprises people, because no single row was written twice — the classic shape is two transactions each independently checking "is this invariant still satisfied?" (is at least one doctor still on call, is this seat still available across two different seats a user is trying to book together) and both proceeding because the check passed for them individually, when checked concurrently. The general fix is to make the invariant an actual, explicit constraint the database can enforce (a unique constraint, a check constraint) rather than something only the application logic verifies via a read-then-write — or use SELECT ... FOR UPDATE to lock the rows the invariant depends on, not just the row being written.

What to say in an interview when a design needs multi-row consistency: name the specific anomaly the design is vulnerable to (usually lost update or write skew, since dirty reads/writes are solved by even the weakest usable isolation level), then name the concrete fix (conditional update, explicit row lock, or a real constraint) — reciting "I'll use a transaction" without naming which isolation level and which anomaly it addresses reads as memorized rather than understood.

2.19Data Encoding & Schema Evolution

Every system that outlives its first deployment eventually needs to change its data's shape while old and new code run side by side — a mobile client on an old app version talking to a just-upgraded backend, or a message written to a queue last week being read by a consumer deployed today. This is a distinct concern from anything else in this guide, and it's commonly under-prepared for because it doesn't show up as its own "design X" case study — it shows up as a follow-up question inside almost any other one ("now the schema needs a new field — walk me through the rollout").

Encoding formats, broadly:

Format Shape Trade-off
JSON / XML Text-based, self-describing (field names travel with every record) Human-readable and universally supported, at the cost of size (repeating every field name in every record) and ambiguity (no built-in distinction between an integer and a float, weak support for binary data)
Language-specific (e.g., Java Serializable, Python pickle) Native to one language's object model Fast to use within a single codebase, but locks you into that language and often that exact class definition — a poor choice for anything crossing a service boundary or a long time horizon
Schema-based binary (Protocol Buffers, Thrift, Avro) A separate schema defines field names and types once; encoded records carry only field tags (numbers) or, in Avro's case, no tags at all Compact and fast, and — the real point — the schema itself becomes the tool for managing change safely

Protocol Buffers / Thrift, mechanically: every field in the schema gets a number (its tag), and only that number (not the field name) is written into the encoded bytes. This single design choice is what makes safe evolution possible: you can rename a field freely (the tag, not the name, is what's encoded), and the rule for compatibility becomes simple and mechanical — never reuse an old tag number for a new field, and any new field must be optional or have a default (so old code, which has never heard of it, can simply skip it).

Avro takes a different approach — no tags at all in the encoded data, just values packed tightly back-to-back in schema-defined order. This makes Avro even more compact, but means a reader must have the exact writer's schema to parse the bytes at all; Avro's actual trick for evolution is that a reader is handed both the schema the data was written with and the schema it wants to read as, and a resolution process matches fields by name between the two — which is what lets a large, frequently-changing dataset (Avro's classic use case) evolve without needing to update every consumer in lockstep.

Backward and forward compatibility, defined precisely (a distinction worth stating exactly, since the two directions fail differently and get confused constantly):

  • Backward compatibility — newer code can read data written by older code. This is the direction you need for your own rolling deploys: today's consumer must still understand yesterday's messages sitting in a queue.
  • Forward compatibility — older code can read data written by newer code. This is the direction you need when a producer is upgraded before all its consumers are — a newer service writing a new field that an old, not-yet-upgraded consumer will simply ignore, rather than crash on.

A safe rollout of any schema change is really a rollout of both directions at once across however many services touch that data, and the two safe operations that never break either direction are: adding a new optional field with a default, and removing a field that was already optional — the classic "you'll shoot yourself in the foot" moves are making a field required (breaks backward compatibility for older writers) or repurposing a tag/field name for something else entirely (breaks both directions silently, since old and new code both parse it, just as different, wrong things).

Where this shows up as three distinct "dataflow modes," each with a different evolution constraint:

  1. Through a database — a row written years ago by old code must still be read correctly by today's code (and vice versa, if you ever roll back a deploy) — this is backward AND forward compatibility, indefinitely, since old rows don't get rewritten just because the schema changed.
  2. Through services (REST/RPC) — a client and server are upgraded independently, so an API contract needs the same two-directional discipline as above; this is the concrete justification for the "additive, versioned changes" API-design advice in Section 2.2, not an unrelated best practice.
  3. Through a message queue/stream — a message can sit in a durable log (Section 2.8) for a long time before being consumed, so the producer that wrote it and the consumer that eventually reads it may be running code from very different points in time — arguably the mode where schema discipline matters most, since there's no way to "just query the current version" the way a request/response API allows.

Part III — Case Studies

Every case study below follows the same shape on purpose — requirements, core components, the one or two decisions an interviewer will actually probe, and real numbers where they sharpen the design — because that consistency is the whole point: once you've internalized the shape, you can apply it to any "design X" prompt you haven't seen before, which is the actual skill being tested.

Group A — Foundational Building Blocks

These are the problems every other case study quietly depends on — expect at least one of these, or a close variant, in almost any HLD loop.

Design a Rate Limiter

Why this problem, specifically: it looks like a five-minute problem and isn't — the naive answer (a counter in a database) is obviously wrong under load, the correct answer has a genuine race condition, and there are five legitimate algorithms with real trade-offs between them. Interviewers use it to see whether you reach for the right primitive (atomicity) reflexively, and how far you can go when pushed past the single-node case.

Clarifying questions to open with:

  • Rate limit what, exactly — per user ID, per IP, per API key, per endpoint, or some combination? (Assume per-API-key as the primary axis, with per-endpoint as a secondary dimension, unless told otherwise.)
  • Where is it enforced — client SDK (advisory only, easily bypassed), API gateway (the usual answer), or inside each service (for business-specific limits)?
  • What happens to a rejected request — hard reject with 429, or queued/delayed?
  • Fail-open or fail-closed if the rate-limiting store itself is unreachable? (This single question is worth asking out loud — it's a real product decision, not an implementation detail.)
  • Single region or global — does a user's limit need to be enforced consistently across data centers?

Functional requirements: enforce N requests per time window per key; return a clear signal (status code + headers) telling the client how much quota remains and when it resets; support different limits for different tiers/endpoints.

Non-functional requirements: the check itself must add negligible latency (single-digit milliseconds) to every request it gates; must work correctly across many stateless gateway nodes, not just one; must not become a single point of failure for the entire API.

API contract, concretely — this is worth stating explicitly rather than leaving implicit:

HTTP/1.1 429 Too Many Requests
Retry-After: 30
X-RateLimit-Limit: 1000
X-RateLimit-Remaining: 0
X-RateLimit-Reset: 1735689600

A well-behaved client throttles itself using the headers on every response (200s included), so it rarely has to actually hit a 429 at all — worth mentioning as the difference between a rate limiter that merely enforces and one that helps clients cooperate.

Back-of-envelope framing: the interesting number here isn't throughput of the protected API — it's the throughput of the rate limiter's own check. At 100K API requests/sec, the limiter's shared store needs to sustain 100K reads+writes/sec just for bookkeeping, on top of whatever the actual API does — which is exactly why the store has to be something like Redis (Section 2.19's case study — hundreds of thousands of ops/sec on a single node) and not a row in the primary relational database.

Architecture:

flowchart LR

The algorithms, in real depth (Section 2.4 has the summary table — this is the mechanism behind each row):

  • Token bucket, worked through: a bucket holds up to B tokens, refilling at R tokens/second. Each request attempts to remove one token; if the bucket is empty, reject. The clever part is not running a background refill timer per key (wildly wasteful across millions of keys) — instead, store (tokens, last_refill_timestamp) and compute the refill lazily on each check: tokens = min(B, tokens + (now - last_refill_timestamp) * R). This makes the whole algorithm a single atomic read-modify-write, no background process needed.
  • Sliding window counter, worked through: blend the current fixed window's count with a weighted fraction of the previous window's count: estimated_count = current_window_count + previous_window_count * (1 - elapsed_fraction_of_current_window). This approximates a true sliding window (which would need to store every timestamp — expensive) using just two integers per key, which is why it's the practical default for high-scale public APIs rather than the more precise but memory-heavy sliding-window-log.

The atomicity problem, worked through in actual Redis commands — this is the part interviewers probe hardest, because the naive version has a check-then-act race identical in shape to the parking-spot and seat-locking races from Part I:

-- executed atomically as a single Lua script via EVAL, so no other
-- client's script can interleave between the read and the write
local key = KEYS[1]
local limit = tonumber(ARGV[1])
local window = tonumber(ARGV[2])
local current = redis.call("INCR", key)
if current == 1 then
    redis.call("EXPIRE", key, window)
end
if current > limit then
    return 0  -- reject
else
    return 1  -- allow
end

The reason this needs to be a Lua script rather than two separate Redis calls (GET then INCR) from the application: Redis executes a single command (and a single Lua script) atomically, so wrapping check-and-increment inside one script closes the race entirely, whereas two round-trips from the application always leave a window where two concurrent requests both read "9 of 10 used" and both proceed, silently allowing 11.

Multi-region — the hardest real follow-up: enforcing one global limit for a user across data centers means every gateway node worldwide needs a consistent view of that user's count, which conflicts directly with wanting low-latency local checks. The two honest answers: (1) accept eventual global accuracy — each region keeps a local counter and asynchronously syncs a rough count to the others, so the true global limit can briefly overshoot by however stale the sync is (acceptable for abuse prevention, not for anything billing-critical); or (2) route a given user consistently to one "home" region for rate-limit purposes specifically (even if their actual traffic is served locally), trading a network hop for exactness. Name both, and say which one fits the stated use case — this is the single best way to show depth on this problem.

Failure modes:

  • The Redis store itself becomes unreachable — this is exactly the fail-open/fail-closed question from the clarifying questions above, now with a concrete answer to give: fail-open (let requests through unlimited) for most consumer APIs, since an unenforced rate limit for a few minutes is far less damaging than rejecting all legitimate traffic; fail-closed only when the limiter is protecting something that fails catastrophically without it (a downstream payment processor with its own hard rate limits).
  • A hot key — one extremely high-volume API key hammering a single Redis key. This is the hot-key problem from Section 2.5 in miniature; the fix is the same toolkit (local, coarse pre-filtering on each gateway node before ever hitting Redis, so Redis only sees the traffic that survived the cheap local check).

Interviewer curveballs, with the shape of a strong answer:

  • "Rate limit by cost, not just request count" (some endpoints are 10x more expensive than others) — token bucket generalizes trivially: instead of removing 1 token per request, remove cost(endpoint) tokens; everything else is unchanged.
  • "Rate limit a WebSocket connection, which isn't a series of discrete requests" — shift from request-count to a message-rate or bandwidth limit checked periodically on the open connection, using the same token-bucket accounting but sampled every N seconds rather than per-HTTP-request.
  • "How would you let a client know its limit before it gets rejected?" — the X-RateLimit-* headers above, returned on every response, not just 429s — this is the cooperative-throttling point made concrete.

Design Consistent Hashing

(as a from-scratch design problem, not just the concept from Section 2.6 — expect this specifically when an interviewer wants to probe raw data-structure and distributed-systems fundamentals without any product framing at all.)

Why this problem, specifically: it's the purest test of whether you actually understand why naive hashing fails at scale, not just that consistent hashing exists. A shockingly large fraction of candidates can name "consistent hashing" but can't explain what specifically breaks with hash(key) % N, which is the actual question being tested.

The problem, stated precisely first: with N servers and server = hash(key) % N, adding or removing a single server changes N for every key's computation, which reassigns the overwhelming majority of keys to a different server — not just the keys that "belong" to the changed server. For a cache, this is a near-total cache wipe on every scaling event; for a sharded database, this is a full data-migration event triggered by adding one box.

Functional requirements: map an arbitrary key to one of N servers; adding or removing a server should remap as few keys as possible; the mapping should be reasonably load-balanced even as servers come and go.

The ring, built step by step:

  1. Pick a hash function producing a fixed-width output (commonly treated as points on a circle from 0 to 2^32 - 1 or 2^160 - 1 for SHA-1).
  2. Hash each server (by ID/address) onto the ring — each server now occupies one point.
  3. To place a key, hash it the same way, then walk clockwise from that point to find the first server encountered — that server owns the key.
  4. Adding a new server: it lands at some new point on the ring, and takes ownership of exactly the keys between it and its counter-clockwise neighbor (previously owned by that neighbor) — every other server's assignments are untouched.
  5. Removing a server: its keys transfer entirely to its clockwise neighbor — again, nothing else on the ring moves.

Data structure, precisely (worth naming unprompted): the ring is implemented as a sorted map/balanced tree (a Java TreeMap, a Python SortedList, or a plain sorted array with binary search) from ring-position → server. "Find the first server clockwise from point P" is then a single ceiling()/upper_bound() lookup — O(log N) — not a linear scan around the ring, which candidates who've only memorized the concept frequently get wrong when asked to actually implement get_server(key).

class ConsistentHashRing:
    ring = SortedMap<int, Server>()   # position -> server

    function addServer(server):
        for i in range(VIRTUAL_NODES):           # see below
            position = hash(server.id + ":" + i)
            ring[position] = server

    function removeServer(server):
        for i in range(VIRTUAL_NODES):
            position = hash(server.id + ":" + i)
            ring.remove(position)

    function getServer(key):
        position = hash(key)
        entry = ring.ceilingEntry(position)       # first position >= key's position
        if entry == null:
            entry = ring.firstEntry()              # wrap around the ring
        return entry.value

Virtual nodes — the detail that separates a textbook answer from a working one. With only a handful of physical servers hashed to single points, the arcs between them are wildly uneven by chance alone (imagine 4 random points on a circle — some gaps will be much larger than others), so some servers end up owning far more keys than others. The fix: hash each physical server to many points on the ring (100–200 is typical, shown as VIRTUAL_NODES above), so its total ownership is the sum of many small, randomly-sized arcs — which by the law of large numbers averages out close to an even share. Virtual nodes solve a second problem too: when a server fails, its load doesn't dump entirely onto one clockwise neighbor — it's spread across the many different servers that happen to be clockwise-adjacent to its many virtual points, distributing the failover load instead of creating a new hot spot.

Follow-up Q&A:

  • "How do clients/gateway nodes learn the current ring membership?" — a coordination service (ZooKeeper/etcd) holding the authoritative membership list, watched by every node for changes, or a gossip protocol (Section 2.7) where membership changes propagate peer-to-peer without a central coordinator — name both, and note gossip trades a small propagation delay for not needing a separate coordination system.
  • "What if servers have different capacities (one box is 4x the others)?" — give the bigger server proportionally more virtual nodes (4x as many), rather than treating virtual-node count as fixed — this is precisely how real deployments handle heterogeneous hardware.
  • "Walk me through adding a server live, without downtime." — the new server's virtual nodes are added to the ring; the only keys affected are the ones that now fall in its arcs; for a cache, those simply become cold misses in the new server's newly-owned space (self-healing on next read); for a database shard, this requires an explicit, monitored data-migration step to physically copy the reassigned key ranges before the ring change is safe to make live — the distinction between "cache, self-heals" and "database, needs orchestrated migration" is exactly the kind of nuance worth stating without being asked.

301 vs. 302 — state this trade-off explicitly, it's a favorite one-liner follow-up: a 302 Found (temporary redirect) means the browser asks your server on every single click, so you see every redirect (necessary for click analytics) at the cost of that server load; a 301 Moved Permanently gets cached by the browser after the first visit, so subsequent clicks never even hit your server — far less load, but you lose visibility into repeat clicks entirely.   • "Detect and block malicious/phishing URLs." — an async check (URL-reputation API or an internal blocklist) run after the redirect record is created but before it's served publicly, or checked synchronously on creation if the product requires zero window of exposure — a real trade-off between creation latency and safety worth naming.

Design a Unique ID Generator (Distributed)

Why this problem, specifically: it looks like a small, self-contained algorithm question, and interviewers use it to probe one very specific thing — do you understand why a naive approach (auto-increment, or plain random UUIDs) fails at distributed scale, and can you reason correctly about the one genuinely nasty edge case (clock skew)?

Requirements: generate IDs that are unique across many concurrent machines with zero coordination on the hot path; ideally roughly time-sortable (so they behave well as primary keys / B-Tree index values, Section 2.10); very high throughput per machine.

The bit layout, precisely (Twitter Snowflake's original scheme, still the reference every course cites):

Bits Field Purpose
1 unused (sign bit) kept 0 so the ID is always a positive 64-bit integer
41 timestamp (ms since a custom epoch) gives ≈ 69 years of range from whatever epoch you pick; provides the rough time-ordering
10 machine/datacenter ID up to 1,024 concurrently-running generator instances with zero risk of collision between them
12 sequence number up to 4,096 distinct IDs from one machine within the same millisecond

Throughput this actually yields: 4,096 IDs/ms/machine = ~4.096 million IDs/sec per machine, and linearly more with every additional machine — the entire point of moving the uniqueness guarantee into the ID's own structure rather than a central counter.

Generation algorithm, worked through — including the edge case that's the actual point of the question:

function nextId():
    now = currentTimeMillis()
    if now < lastTimestamp:
        # THE edge case: system clock moved backward (NTP correction, VM migration).
        # Do NOT generate an ID with an earlier timestamp than one already issued --
        # that would break the sortability guarantee and could even collide with a
        # sequence number already used at that same earlier millisecond.
        throw ClockMovedBackwardsException()   # or: block until now >= lastTimestamp

    if now == lastTimestamp:
        sequence = (sequence + 1) mod 4096
        if sequence == 0:
            now = waitUntilNextMillis(lastTimestamp)   # this machine's 4096-per-ms budget is exhausted
    else:
        sequence = 0

    lastTimestamp = now
    return (now << 22) | (machineId << 12) | sequence

The clock-moved-backward branch is worth narrating unprompted — it's precisely the "process pauses" and "distributed clocks" material from Section 2.7 and Section 2.18 showing up concretely: a generator has no way to know why its clock jumped backward, only that trusting it blindly could silently issue a duplicate or out-of-order ID, so the safe move is to refuse (or block) rather than proceed on a clock it can no longer trust.

Machine-ID assignment — the coordination this design otherwise avoids entirely: each of the up-to-1,024 running instances needs a distinct machine ID, assigned once at startup, not per-request — typically via a lease from ZooKeeper/etcd (Section 2.7's coordination-service pattern) that the instance holds for its lifetime, or a simpler scheme deriving it from a known, pre-assigned hostname/pod-index in a container orchestrator. This is the only coordination in the whole design, and it happens at process startup, never on the request hot path — worth stating explicitly, since it's what makes the throughput number above achievable.

Failure modes: a machine ID lease expires unexpectedly while the process is still running (e.g., a coordination-service hiccup) — the process should treat this as fatal and stop issuing IDs rather than risk silently colliding with whatever process picks up that same machine ID next, rather than trying to "recover" gracefully in place.

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "Why not just use a random UUID (v4)?" — uniqueness is fine (collision probability is astronomically low), but a v4 UUID is uniformly random across its entire 128-bit space, meaning consecutive inserts land in unpredictable, scattered positions in a B-Tree index (Section 2.10) — causing expensive page splits and poor cache locality, versus a Snowflake ID's mostly-increasing values which append cleanly to the right edge of the tree.
  • "What about UUIDv7?" — the modern middle ground (Section 2.15): a standard 128-bit UUID that embeds a millisecond timestamp in its leading bits, giving most of Snowflake's index-friendliness as a drop-in UUID with zero custom machine-ID infrastructure to run — the right answer when "roughly time-ordered and unique" is enough and you'd rather not operate a dedicated ID service at all.
  • "Multi-datacenter, and a machine ID collision across two DCs that don't coordinate?" — split the 10-bit machine-ID field into a datacenter-ID sub-field and a per-DC machine-ID sub-field (e.g., 5 bits + 5 bits), so uniqueness holds globally without the two DCs' coordination services ever needing to talk to each other.

Design a Key-Value Store

(the "Dynamo-style" classic)

Why this problem, specifically: more than any other case study in this guide, this is the one built specifically to test whether you can reason live about CAP/quorums rather than recite them — every follow-up is some variant of "what happens to this specific read/write during this specific failure," and there's nowhere to hide behind memorized vocabulary.

Clarifying questions: what's the value size (small, like a session token, vs. large, like a file)? Is this the primary store, or a cache in front of something else? What consistency does the product actually need — can two clients briefly disagree about the latest value?

Functional requirements: put(key, value) and get(key), at very high scale, remaining available even when individual nodes fail.

Non-functional requirements: high write and read availability (favor AP under partition, Section 2.7, unless told otherwise — this is Dynamo's own original design choice and the canonical answer for this problem); tunable consistency per operation; horizontal scalability with no single coordinator on the hot path.

Architecture:

flowchart TD
    Client --> Coord["Coordinator node
(any node can serve this role)"]
    Coord -->|consistent hash of key| N1["Node A"]
    Coord --> N2["Node B"]
    Coord --> N3["Node C"]
    N1 <-.->|gossip: membership + health| N2
    N2 <-.-> N3
    N1 <-.->|anti-entropy: Merkle tree sync| N3

Any node can act as the coordinator for a given request — there's deliberately no fixed leader, which is the leaderless-replication design (Section 2.6) that makes this whole architecture tolerant of any single node failing without an election/failover pause.

Partitioning and replication, concretely: the keyspace is partitioned via consistent hashing (the from-scratch problem above) across all nodes; each key is additionally replicated to the N nodes immediately clockwise of its ring position (so N=3 means 3 physical copies of every key, on 3 different nodes, chosen automatically by the ring layout).

Quorums, worked through with actual numbers — this is the core of the interview: with N=3 replicas, W (write quorum) and R (read quorum) are chosen per the guarantee needed, subject to W + R > N for strong-ish consistency:

Configuration Write cost Read cost Behavior
W=3, R=1 Wait for all 3 replicas Read 1, always fresh if writes truly reached all 3 Slowest writes, fastest/most available reads
W=1, R=3 Fast, only 1 replica must ack Must read all 3 and reconcile Fastest writes, slowest reads
W=2, R=2 (N=3) Wait for 2 of 3 Read 2 of 3 The standard "sweet spot" — W+R=4 > N=3 guarantees any read quorum and any write quorum share at least one common replica, so a read is guaranteed to see the latest acknowledged write

Walk through an actual partition, since this is exactly what gets asked: with W=2, R=2, N=3, a network partition splits the cluster so one replica of a key is isolated alone on one side. A write can still succeed (2 of the remaining 2-node side ack it) and a read can still succeed (same). If instead the partition isolates two replicas together and leaves only one reachable, neither a W=2 write nor an R=2 read can be satisfied on the single-node side — that side becomes unavailable for that key until the partition heals, which is the system correctly choosing not to serve a request it can't back with quorum, rather than silently serving stale or unconfirmed data.

Availability under failure — sloppy quorum and hinted handoff: a strict quorum (always the same N "home" nodes for a key) means a write can be rejected outright if one home node is temporarily down, even though plenty of other healthy nodes exist. Sloppy quorum relaxes this: a write is accepted by the first N healthy nodes on the ring, whether or not they're a key's usual home. When a temporarily-down home node comes back, the node that stood in for it delivers the writes it collected on its behalf — a hinted handoff. This trades a small, temporary consistency risk (a home node's own local copy can lag briefly) for meaningfully higher write availability, and is exactly the kind of nuance that separates "I know quorums" from "I know how Dynamo actually stays available during a real node failure."

Conflict resolution, concretely: two clients write to the same key on two different "healthy replica" sets during a partition — both writes succeed locally, and the system now has two divergent versions. Vector clocks (a per-key map of {replica_id: write_count}) let the system detect this is a genuine concurrent conflict (neither version is derived from the other) rather than a simple overwrite, and hand both versions back to the client/application to reconcile (a shopping cart merges both sets of items; a last-write-wins policy picks the higher timestamp) — worth stating explicitly that the database only detects the conflict, it's the application's job to decide what "correct" reconciliation means for that specific data.

Anti-entropy: replicas that drifted apart (from hinted handoff, or a node down longer than a handoff window covers) are reconciled in the background using Merkle trees — compare a hash tree of each replica's data top-down, and only the branches whose hashes differ need to actually be compared and synced key-by-key, rather than diffing the entire dataset.

Storage engine, one level down: each node's local storage is typically an LSM-tree (Section 2.10) — write-heavy, sequential-append-friendly, exactly matching this system's write-optimized access pattern, with Bloom filters in front of each SSTable so a get() for a key that node doesn't have skips straight past irrelevant files.

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "Make this strongly consistent instead." — set W=N (every replica must ack every write) — correctness at the direct cost of availability (any single node down blocks all writes), the CP side of the CAP choice made concrete, and worth explicitly contrasting against the AP default above.
  • "A node is permanently gone, not just temporarily down — how does re-replication happen?" — once the coordination layer (gossip/ZooKeeper) confirms the node is truly gone rather than transiently partitioned, the ring's virtual nodes (Consistent Hashing case study above) redistribute its key ranges to its ring neighbors, who then need to be re-replicated up to N copies again via bulk data transfer.
  • "How is this different from the Distributed Cache case study?" — genuinely worth answering directly: this system is designed as a system of record (durable, quorum-consistent, no data loss on a node restart), where the Distributed Cache case study explicitly accepts data loss on a full outage in exchange for far higher per-node throughput and simplicity — same underlying primitives (consistent hashing, replication), different point on the durability/performance trade-off.

Design a Distributed Cache (build Redis)

Why this problem, specifically: unlike the Dynamo-style KV store, this problem explicitly trades away durability for raw speed — the interesting interview signal is whether you can articulate that trade-off clearly and design accordingly, rather than accidentally re-deriving Dynamo's answer to a differently-optimized problem.

Clarifying questions: is this purely a cache (source of truth lives elsewhere, so data loss is a performance blip, not a correctness incident), or does the product want it to double as lightweight durable storage? What's the expected value size distribution — tiny (counters, session flags) or larger (serialized objects)?

Functional requirements: GET/SET/DEL with optional TTL; support for the richer data structures (lists, sets, sorted sets) many real use cases actually need beyond plain key-value.

Non-functional requirements: sub-millisecond latency at very high per-node throughput; graceful degradation (not data loss catastrophe) on a node crash; horizontal scalability once one node's memory or throughput ceiling is reached.

Core engine: an in-memory hash table with lazy expiry (a key's TTL is checked on access — if expired, delete and treat as a miss) backed by an active expiry cycle (a background sweep that also probabilistically samples and removes expired keys nobody's accessed recently, so memory isn't held forever by dead keys nobody ever triggers a lazy check on). Under memory pressure, eviction uses an approximated LRU/LFU — Redis specifically samples a small random set of keys (default 5) and evicts the oldest/least-used among just that sample, rather than tracking exact global recency/frequency across the entire keyspace, because exact tracking would itself cost more memory than the data it's protecting — a deliberate accuracy-for-memory trade-off worth naming as intentional, not a shortcut.

Replication and failover, worked through: asynchronous leader-follower replication (Section 2.6), using a partial resynchronization (PSYNC) protocol — a follower that briefly disconnects and reconnects sends the leader the offset it last received, and the leader replays just the missed backlog from its in-memory replication buffer, rather than the follower re-copying the entire dataset from scratch (a full resync, which only happens if the backlog has already rolled past what the follower needs). Failover — promoting a follower when the leader dies — is handled by Sentinel processes in a simpler deployment (a small quorum of Sentinels monitor leader health via heartbeats and vote on which follower to promote, the same leader-election shape as Section 2.7's consensus discussion, just purpose-built rather than general-purpose Raft/Paxos) or built directly into Redis Cluster for a sharded deployment.

Persistence, despite being "just a cache" — worth explaining why this exists at all: even a pure cache benefits from surviving a planned restart (a deploy, a maintenance reboot) without a full cold-cache stampede against the backing store. RDB takes periodic point-in-time snapshots (compact, fast to reload on restart, but loses everything written since the last snapshot on an unplanned crash); AOF (Append-Only File) logs every write operation and replays them on restart, with a tunable fsync policy — always (fsync every write — safest, slowest), everysec (fsync once a second — the common compromise, losing at most ~1 second of writes), or no (let the OS decide when to flush — fastest, riskiest). Most production deployments run AOF with everysec alongside periodic RDB snapshots, getting AOF's low data-loss window for normal restarts and RDB's fast, compact full-reload for disaster recovery.

Scaling past one node — Redis Cluster, precisely: the keyspace is partitioned into a fixed 16,384 hash slots (slot = CRC16(key) mod 16384), distributed across however many nodes are in the cluster. A client computes a key's slot directly and can route straight to the owning node without an intermediary lookup; a node that receives a request for a key it doesn't currently own returns a MOVED redirect (the slot has permanently moved to another node — update your local slot map) or an ASK redirect (the slot is mid-migration — retry this one request at the new node, but don't update your permanent slot map yet, since the migration isn't finalized). Fixing the slot count at 16,384 rather than "one slot per node" is deliberate — it decouples the logical partitioning scheme from the physical node count, so adding or removing nodes only means reassigning a subset of slots (and the keys in them), not recomputing a hash function across the whole keyspace, similar in spirit to why consistent hashing's ring uses many virtual points rather than one point per node.

Data structures worth naming if asked "why Redis and not just a hash map": Lists, Sets, Sorted Sets (the backbone of the Leaderboard case study below and feed-precompute patterns in Part III's social case studies), Streams (an append-only log, close in spirit to a lightweight Kafka partition — Section 2.8), HyperLogLog (Section 2.19-adjacent — approximate cardinality in near-constant memory), and Geospatial indexes (the Uber case study, Part III) — the breadth of built-in structures is a large part of why Redis shows up as the answer to so many different sub-problems across this guide, not just one, and is worth mentioning as evidence you understand it as a data-structure server, not merely a key-value cache.

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "What happens on a full cluster outage and restart?" — cold cache means every read is now a miss hitting the backing database simultaneously — the thundering herd / cache avalanche scenario from Section 2.5, and the same fixes apply: staggered/jittered warm-up, or a brief period of degraded (slower, DB-direct) service while the cache refills naturally.
  • "How would you cache a value too large to fit comfortably as one key?" — split it into chunks under related keys (or store the large blob in object storage and cache only a signed reference/URL to it), since a cache is optimized for many small, fast operations, not a handful of huge ones.
  • "Compare this to Memcached." — Memcached is deliberately simpler (pure key-value, multi-threaded, no built-in replication or persistence — you build those yourself or don't have them), which is a legitimate choice when you want a dumb, extremely fast cache with nothing else, versus Redis's richer data structures and built-in HA story when you want the cache to do more.

Design an API Gateway

Why this problem, specifically: this is one of the few HLD problems that's really an infrastructure-design question in disguise — the interviewer is testing whether you can separate a hot-path concern from a control-path concern cleanly, which is a skill that generalizes far beyond this one component.

Clarifying questions: how many backend services sit behind this gateway, and are they all internal, or does it also front third-party/partner traffic? Does it need to support gRPC/WebSocket passthrough, or is it HTTP-only? Is TLS termination happening here, or upstream at a load balancer?

Functional requirements: route each incoming request to the correct backend service; authenticate and authorize before forwarding; enforce rate limits (the case study above, reused as a building block); transform requests/responses where clients and backends disagree on shape.

Non-functional requirements: the gateway sits in front of every request to every service, so its own latency budget must be a small, near-constant number of milliseconds — any inefficiency here is multiplied across the entire system's traffic; it must not become a single point of failure (run many stateless gateway instances behind a load balancer, Section 2.4).

Architecture — the data-plane/control-plane split, which is the entire interview:

flowchart TD

(Envoy-style)"]

The data plane is the hot path every single request flows through: routing by path/host/header, authentication (validating a JWT or session token — Section 2.12), rate limiting (the previous case study, applied here as one gateway-level concern among several), request/response transformation, and TLS termination — built on a performance-focused proxy (Envoy is the standard real-world answer) precisely because its latency is added to every request the whole system serves. The control plane is everything that configures the data plane — route definitions, certificate rotation, canary traffic percentages — and can afford to be slower and heavier, since it's entirely off the request path; it pushes configuration down to data-plane nodes rather than data-plane nodes pulling on every request, so a control-plane slowdown never directly adds latency to user traffic.

Retry budgets, worked through — the decision that matters most here: a gateway retrying failed requests to a backend seems safely defensive in isolation, but if every one of a gateway's many stateless nodes independently retries against an already-struggling backend, the retries themselves are exactly what can tip a slow backend into a full outage — retry amplification. The fix is a global cap: the gateway tracks what fraction of total outbound traffic to a given backend is currently retries (not original requests), and stops issuing new retries once that fraction crosses a threshold (a common one: retries capped at 10% of the request volume), favoring fast, honest failures for the excess over piling more load onto an already-struggling backend. This connects directly to Section 2.1's timeout-cascade and circuit-breaker material — a retry budget is really a circuit breaker scoped to "retry traffic" specifically rather than all traffic to a dependency.

Also expected, at this depth: active and passive health checks against every backend instance (routing around unhealthy ones automatically, and pulling a newly-recovered instance back into rotation only after it passes checks, not the instant it comes back up); canary/weighted routing (send a small, configurable percentage of traffic to a new backend version, watch its error rate, then ramp — Section 2.14's deployment-strategy material, implemented as a gateway routing rule rather than a separate system); request/response transformation (translating a public REST contract into an internal gRPC call, so backend services can evolve their internal protocols without breaking public API consumers).

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "The gateway itself needs to scale to handle 1M req/sec — how?" — it's stateless by design (Section 2.16), so this is purely a horizontal-scaling and load-balancing problem (Section 2.4) — many identical gateway nodes behind an L4/L7 load balancer, with the shared rate-limiting state (the genuinely stateful part) pushed out to Redis exactly as in the Rate Limiter case study above.
  • "How do you roll out a route-config change to every gateway node without downtime or inconsistency?" — the control plane pushes new config to data-plane nodes via a streaming update (Envoy's xDS protocol is the real-world mechanism), with nodes applying it atomically — never serving a request with half-old, half-new routing rules — and a control-plane failure should never be able to take down already-running data-plane nodes, since they continue serving their last-known-good config.
  • "What's the difference between this and a plain load balancer?" — a load balancer (Section 2.4) purely distributes traffic across healthy backends; a gateway does that plus the cross-cutting application-aware concerns above (auth, rate limiting, transformation) — in practice the line blurs (Envoy does both), but naming the conceptual distinction is the point of the question.

Design a Web Crawler

Why this problem, specifically: it's really a distributed-systems politeness-and-scale problem wearing a "crawl the web" costume — the interesting parts are queueing discipline and dedup at scale, not HTML parsing, and an interviewer steering this toward "now build a search engine on top" is testing whether the scatter-gather pattern generalizes for you.

Clarifying questions: crawl scope — the whole open web, or a bounded set of domains? Is freshness (re-crawling changed pages) required, or is this a one-time/periodic full crawl? What content types matter (HTML only, or PDFs/images too)?

Functional requirements: given a set of seed URLs, discover and download reachable pages; avoid re-fetching the same URL redundantly; respect each site's stated crawl policy.

Non-functional requirements: massive horizontal scalability (billions of pages); politeness — never overwhelm any single host with concurrent requests, regardless of overall crawler throughput; resilience to malformed pages, dead links, and deliberately infinite URL spaces.

Architecture:

The URL Frontier, precisely — this is the component that encodes "politeness": not a single global queue, but logically partitioned by host, so a fetcher can only ever have one (or a small, capped number of) requests in flight to any given domain at once, no matter how many total fetchers the crawler is running — this is what prevents the crawler from accidentally DDoSing a small site just because it happens to have thousands of pages. Within that constraint, URLs are prioritized (a priority queue, not FIFO) by a mix of estimated importance (PageRank-like signal, if re-crawling an existing index) and freshness need (how long since this URL was last fetched).

Deduplication at scale, worked through precisely: "has this exact URL been seen before" needs to answer against a set that can reach billions of entries — a plain hash set doesn't fit in memory at that scale. A Bloom filter (Section 2.5-adjacent) answers "definitely not seen" or "probably seen" using a fixed, small-per-entry memory footprint (a handful of bits per URL, not the URL's own bytes) — false positives (wrongly skipping a genuinely-new URL) are rare and acceptable (you just miss crawling one page you should have), and false negatives are mathematically impossible (a probably seen never means definitely new), which is exactly the asymmetry a crawler can tolerate. Content-level dedup (two different URLs serving identical content — very common with tracking parameters) is a separate check: hash the fetched page's normalized content and compare against previously-seen content hashes.

Politeness mechanics, concretely: fetch and cache each host's robots.txt once (don't re-fetch it per page); respect its Disallow rules and any stated Crawl-delay; even without an explicit Crawl-delay, self-impose a minimum delay between requests to the same host (commonly a few seconds), enforced by the per-host partitioning of the Frontier above rather than trusted to each fetcher's individual behavior.

Crawler traps, and the concrete defenses: an infinite URL space (a calendar page that always generates a "next month" link, session-ID parameters appended to every link creating infinite URL variants of the same page) can trap a naive crawler forever on one domain. Defenses: a hard max crawl depth per domain; a max total pages per domain budget; URL normalization (strip session/tracking query parameters, resolve to a canonical form) before the dedup check, so ?session=abc123 and ?session=xyz789 on an otherwise-identical page are recognized as the same URL rather than two "new" ones.

Extension — turning the crawler into a search engine, worked through, since this is where the conversation usually goes next: indexing takes crawled pages and builds an inverted index (term → list of documents containing it, Section 2.17 — typically built via a MapReduce/Spark batch job at this scale, not built incrementally per-page). Ranking blends offline signals (PageRank, computed once over the entire link graph by treating links as votes, iteratively — a page's rank is a function of the rank of pages linking to it) with query-time relevance scoring (BM25/TF-IDF, weighing how often and how prominently a query term appears in a given document) and freshness. Serving a query against an index too large for one machine means sharding the index (by term range or by document range — the same local-vs-global secondary-index partitioning trade-off from Section 2.6, applied to search specifically) and doing a scatter-gather: fan the query out to every shard, each returns its local top-K matches, and a merger combines them into one final top-K — this scatter-gather shape recurs anywhere a single logical index outgrows one machine, and recognizing it as the same pattern as the Leaderboard case study below (not a search-specific trick) is worth saying explicitly.

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "How do you prioritize re-crawling a page that changes often (news) vs. one that never changes (an archived PDF)?" — track each URL's observed change frequency over time and use it to set that URL's re-crawl priority adaptively, rather than a single fixed re-crawl interval for the whole corpus.
  • "A fetcher crashes mid-crawl — how do you avoid losing or duplicating work?" — URLs are only removed from the Frontier once a fetch is confirmed complete (persisted, not in-memory-only), so a crashed fetcher's in-flight URLs simply remain claimed-but-incomplete and get reclaimed by a lease-timeout mechanism — structurally the same at-least-once-with-idempotent-retry pattern as the Distributed Scheduler case study (Part III, Group G).

Design a Leaderboard

Why this problem, specifically: it's deliberately one of the smaller problems in this guide, and the interview signal is exactly that — can you recognize when the simple, one-data-structure answer is correct and resist the urge to over-architect a problem that doesn't need it (Section 0.6's checklist, tested directly).

Clarifying questions: how many distinct leaderboards (global, per-region, per-friend-group, time-windowed)? What's the read pattern — mostly "top 10," mostly "my rank," or both equally? How fast do score updates need to be reflected?

Functional requirements: update a user's score; retrieve the top-K users; retrieve a specific user's current rank (even if they're far outside the top-K).

Core design, at moderate scale — genuinely this simple: a Redis sorted set (ZSET) solves this completely on its own. ZADD leaderboard <score> <user_id> to update a score (O(log N)); ZREVRANGE leaderboard 0 9 for the top 10 (O(log N + 10)); ZREVRANK leaderboard <user_id> for a specific user's rank (O(log N)) — a sorted set is internally a skip list plus a hash map, giving log-time insert/update and log-time rank lookup from the same structure, which is precisely why it fits this problem so well rather than being an arbitrary tool choice.

Time-windowed boards, worked through: daily/weekly/all-time boards are naturally just separate keys (leaderboard:daily:2026-09-23, leaderboard:weekly:2026-W38, leaderboard:alltime), each updated on the same score-change event, with old daily/weekly keys simply expiring via TTL (Section 2.5) once they're no longer needed — no separate cleanup job required.

The decision that matters — scaling past one node: once a single board is too large or too hot for one Redis node (a genuinely global, all-time leaderboard for a huge game), shard it by hashing the user ID across multiple ZSETs, and merge the top-K from each shard on read — the identical scatter-gather shape named in the Web Crawler/search-engine extension above: fan out to every shard, each returns its local top-K, a merger combines and re-sorts into the true global top-K. "My rank" gets more expensive under this scheme (you now need to sum how many users across all shards outrank you, not just query one structure) — worth naming as the real cost of sharding this particular problem, since it's the one operation that doesn't shard as cleanly as top-K does.

Trap — how do you stop cheating? score updates must be computed and written server-side only, from server-observed gameplay events, never accepted as a client-submitted number — a client saying "I scored 999999" is not a data source, it's an input to validate or ignore entirely. This is worth stating even though it sounds obvious, because it's exactly the kind of requirement a candidate focused purely on the ZSET mechanics forgets to mention.

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "Show a friends-only leaderboard, not global." — don't maintain a separate ZSET per user's friend group (combinatorial explosion); instead, fetch the relevant slice from the single global/regional ZSET for the user's friend-ID list specifically (ZSCORE per friend, or ZRANK per friend), computed on read rather than precomputed per user.
  • "Real-time updates pushed to viewers watching the board live?" — pair the ZSET with a pub/sub channel (Section 2.8, or Redis's own built-in Pub/Sub) that publishes score-change events to connected clients via WebSocket/SSE (Section 2.2), rather than clients polling the ZSET repeatedly.

Design Twitter's Timeline / X

Why this problem, specifically: the fan-out trade-off is the single most-recycled algorithmic idea in HLD interviews — it reappears under different names in News Feed, Notification System, Slack, and Leaderboard — so this is the case study where getting genuinely fluent pays off across half the rest of this guide.

Clarifying questions: is the timeline strictly reverse-chronological, or algorithmically ranked (assume chronological first — it's the harder infrastructure problem; ranking is a layer on top, covered in the News Feed case study below)? Do retweets and replies belong in the timeline? What's the follower distribution — are we told to expect celebrity accounts with tens of millions of followers, or is this a smaller social network where that's not a concern?

Functional requirements: post a tweet (280 chars + optional media); follow/unfollow a user; view a timeline of tweets from followed accounts, newest first; like/retweet a tweet.

Non-functional requirements: timeline reads must be very low latency (this is the core, constantly-used product surface); the system must tolerate wildly skewed follower counts (from 0 to 100M+) without a single account's activity degrading service for everyone else; eventual consistency for timeline delivery is acceptable (a few seconds' delay before a tweet appears in a follower's feed is invisible to users).

The fan-out trade-off, worked through with the numbers that make it concrete:

  • Fan-out on write (push): when a user tweets, a fan-out worker immediately inserts the tweet ID into every follower's precomputed feed (a Redis sorted set, feed:{userId}, scored by tweet timestamp/Snowflake ID). Reading a timeline is then a single ZREVRANGE — cheap, fast, O(log N + page size). The failure mode is entirely on the write side: an account with 50M followers triggers 50 million individual writes for one tweet — at even a modest fan-out rate this alone can saturate the entire write pipeline.
  • Fan-out on read (pull): tweets are stored once; assembling a timeline means fetching the most recent tweets from every account a user follows and merging them at read time (a K-way merge of sorted lists, one per followed account). Writing a tweet is now O(1) regardless of follower count, but a user following 500 accounts pays for a 500-way merge on every single timeline load — and this is paid by every reader, on every refresh, which at 70K peak reads/sec is a much larger aggregate cost than it sounds.
  • The hybrid, precisely (the actual answer Twitter has documented publicly): set a follower-count threshold (in the tens-of-thousands range); accounts below it get pushed (the vast majority of accounts and the vast majority of tweets — normal users have hundreds of followers, so their fan-out cost is trivial); accounts above it are never pushed at all — their tweets are fetched and merged in at read time, exactly like the pure-pull model, but only for this small set of high-follower accounts. A reader's timeline load becomes: read the precomputed feed (covers everyone they follow who isn't a celebrity) plus a small, bounded merge against the handful of celebrities they follow (bounded because almost nobody follows more than a few dozen mega-accounts) — getting the O(1) read of push for the common case and paying the O(followed accounts) cost of pull only for the rare celebrity case, where it's cheap because there are so few of them.

Feed storage, precisely: feed:{userId} is a Redis sorted set, score = a Snowflake-style ID or timestamp (Section 2.15), member = tweet ID. Only the most recent ~800 tweet IDs are kept per feed (not the user's entire follow history) — old enough tweets simply fall off the precomputed feed, and a user scrolling back further than that triggers a (rare, acceptable) fallback query against the tweet store directly. The feed stores tweet IDs, not full tweet content — the timeline service does a batch fetch of the actual tweet bodies (from a cache-in-front-of-DB, Section 2.5) for whatever page of IDs it's currently rendering, keeping the hot sorted-set small and cheap to maintain.

Backfill on new follow, worked through: a freshly-created follow relationship doesn't retroactively appear in the follower's precomputed feed — the fan-out mechanism only pushes future tweets. So the follow action itself triggers a one-time backfill: fetch the newly-followed account's recent tweets (the same read path pull uses) and merge them directly into the follower's feed at follow-time — a small, one-off pull operation layered on top of an otherwise push-based system.

Failure modes:

  • A fan-out worker crashes mid-fan-out for a very popular (but sub-celebrity-threshold) tweet — the fan-out job needs to be resumable/idempotent (Section 2.7): track progress (which followers have been written so far) so a retry doesn't have to restart from zero, and writing the same tweet ID into a sorted set twice is naturally idempotent anyway (a ZADD with an existing member just updates its score, it doesn't duplicate).
  • A newly-crossed celebrity threshold — an account that grows past the threshold needs a one-time transition: stop pushing new tweets, and its existing fanned-out tweets simply age out of followers' feeds naturally over time (no need to retroactively purge them).

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "How do you delete a tweet?" — don't attempt to purge it from every follower's fanned-out feed (an expensive, unbounded fan-out-in-reverse); instead, mark it deleted (a tombstone flag) in the tweet store, and have the timeline service filter out tombstoned tweets when it fetches full content for a page of IDs — the ID can linger harmlessly in a feed's sorted set since it's never actually rendered.
  • "How would retweets work?" — a retweet fans out like an original tweet (a new entry in the follower's feed pointing at the original tweet's content, not duplicating it), which is also why the tweet store is content-addressed by tweet ID rather than embedding content directly in each feed entry.
  • "What about a ranked (not chronological) timeline?" — this is precisely the News Feed case study below, layered on top of the same fan-out/candidate-generation infrastructure — worth naming as "the next problem" rather than trying to solve ranking here too.

Design Reddit

Why this problem, specifically: unlike Twitter, the interesting problem here isn't fan-out at all (Reddit's content is organized around communities, not personal follower graphs) — it's ranking under a time-decay function and efficiently storing/reading deeply nested trees, two genuinely different technical problems bundled into one case study.

Clarifying questions: is ranking in scope, or just storage/retrieval (assume ranking is in scope — it's the more interesting half)? How deep can comment nesting realistically go, and does the UI collapse deep threads? Is vote count shown exactly, or approximated at high volume?

Functional requirements: create/join communities (subreddits); post within a community; nested/threaded commenting; upvote/downvote posts and comments, affecting their visible rank.

The ranking algorithm, worked through precisely — Reddit's actual publicly-documented "hot" formula, which is worth being able to derive the intuition for, not just recite:

score = upvotes - downvotes
order = log10(max(|score|, 1)) + sign(score) * (post_created_at_seconds - epoch) / 45000

Unpacked: log10(|score|) means going from 1 vote to 10 votes matters far more to rank than going from 1,000 to 1,010 — early votes carry outsized weight, which is precisely what keeps genuinely new, resonating content able to surface quickly rather than requiring an already-huge score to compete. The (created_at) / 45000 term is a linear time boost (45,000 seconds ≈ 12.5 hours per "point" of equivalent score) — it means every post is continuously losing ground to newer posts as clock time passes, independent of whether it's still getting votes, which is what keeps the front page from being permanently dominated by whatever got the first burst of attention hours or days ago. The combination — logarithmic in votes, linear in time — is a deliberately tuned balance: pure vote-count ranking would let old, heavily-voted posts entrench forever; pure recency would drown good content in a flood of mediocre new posts. Being able to explain why each term is shaped the way it is (not just that the formula exists) is the actual signal being tested.

Nested comments, storage worked through — three real options, compared:

Approach How it works Read cost Write cost
Adjacency list (comment.parent_id) Each comment stores only its direct parent Fetching a whole thread means N queries (or one recursive CTE, if the DB supports it) to walk down the tree O(1) — a single insert
Materialized path (path = "1.4.9.2") Each comment stores its full ancestor chain as a string Fetch an entire subtree with one LIKE 'path.%' query — no recursion needed O(1) insert, but the path must be computed from the parent's path
Nested set (left/right bounds) Each comment stores numeric bounds encoding its position in a tree traversal Extremely fast subtree reads (WHERE left BETWEEN ? AND ?) Expensive — inserting anywhere requires renumbering many other nodes' bounds

The right answer to give, and why: materialized path for most real deployments — adjacency list's read cost is too high for deeply-threaded discussions (exactly Reddit's actual shape), and nested set's write cost is disqualifying for a system with Reddit's comment volume (constant high-frequency inserts, vs. the rare-insert workloads nested sets are actually good for). Worth stating this comparison unprompted — naming only one option without the trade-off against the alternatives is a weaker answer even if it happens to be the "right" one.

Vote-count consistency, worked through: a naive UPDATE posts SET score = score + 1 on every single vote creates write contention on hot posts (thousands of concurrent voters on a front-page post hitting the same row) — the same hot-row problem named generically in Section 2.5. The practical fix: buffer votes in a fast counter (an in-memory or Redis increment) and periodically (every few seconds) flush the aggregated delta to the durable posts row, trading a few seconds of read staleness on vote count for removing per-vote write contention from the critical path entirely.

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "How do you detect vote manipulation/brigading?" — this becomes a stream-processing / anomaly-detection problem (Section 2.17): flag statistically unusual voting patterns (a burst of votes from newly-created accounts, votes arriving faster than human interaction plausibly allows) asynchronously, and either discount suspicious votes from the public score or flag the post/comment for review — not something the core voting path itself needs to solve synchronously.
  • "Support sorting comments by 'best,' 'top,' 'new,' and 'controversial' simultaneously?" — each sort is a different scoring formula (or a different simple field) applied at read time over the same underlying comment data — worth naming "controversial" specifically, since it usually means high total votes with a near-even up/down split, a genuinely different signal than either raw score or hot-ranking.

Design a News Feed (generic, e.g. Facebook)

Why this problem, specifically: it's deliberately positioned as "Twitter's fan-out problem, plus a ranking layer" — the interview signal is whether you keep those two concerns cleanly separated (retrieval vs. ranking) rather than conflating them into one tangled service, since that separation is precisely what lets each half scale and evolve independently.

Core design — two distinct services, on purpose: a candidate generation / retrieval step assembles what could possibly appear (recent posts from followed friends/pages/groups — reusing the fan-out infrastructure from the Twitter case study directly), followed by a separate ranking step that scores and reorders that candidate set for this specific user, right now. Keeping these as two services (not one) means the retrieval infrastructure (fan-out, feed storage) never needs to change when the ranking model changes, and vice versa — exactly the kind of loose coupling named as a general principle in Section 2.16, made concrete here.

Ranking factors, worked through (the classic "EdgeRank"-style shape, still the right mental model even though modern systems use full ML models instead of a hand-tuned formula):

  • Affinity — how much does this user historically engage with content from this specific friend/page? (a learned or heuristic score per user-pair)
  • Weight — different content types carry different inherent engagement value (a video historically drives more engagement than a plain text post, in most feeds)
  • Time decay — newer content is boosted, structurally identical in spirit to Reddit's time term above, just usually implemented as a smoother decay curve rather than one hard linear term.

Where the ranking model actually runs — this connects directly to Section 2.15's AI infrastructure material: candidate generation happens first and cheaply (a few hundred posts, from a precomputed feed or a lightweight query); the ranking model only has to score that few-hundred-item shortlist, not the entire universe of content that exists — the same two-phase "cheap broad filter, then expensive precise scoring" shape as the Recommendation System case study (Group J) and the AI-serving material in Part II.

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "Avoid showing 10 posts from the same person in a row." — a diversity/deduplication pass applied after ranking, re-ordering or interleaving the ranked list to enforce a spacing constraint — a separate, explicit post-processing step, not something baked into the ranking score itself, since it's a presentation constraint rather than a relevance signal.
  • "Insert sponsored posts into the feed." — ads are injected at fixed intervals (every Nth position) into the already-ranked organic list, from a completely separate ad-selection pipeline (Group J's Ad-Serving case study) — worth naming that ad insertion and organic ranking are deliberately two different systems with different latency budgets and business logic, merged only at the final rendering step.

Batching, worked through — the detail that separates a good answer from a naive one: a naive design sends one push notification per event, which means a popular post getting 40 likes in a minute sends its owner 40 separate "X liked your post" pushes — a genuinely bad experience, not just an efficiency problem. The fix: a short batching/dedup window (collect events for the same (user, notification-type) pair over a few seconds-to-minutes) that coalesces them into one notification ("40 people liked your post") sent once the window closes or a max-wait timeout is hit — a small, purpose-built version of the micro-batching idea from Section 2.17's stream-processing material.

Provider failover and delivery tracking, worked through: each channel sender treats its underlying provider (APNs, FCM, an email/SMS gateway) as an external dependency subject to Section 2.1's circuit-breaker and retry-with-backoff discipline — a provider outage shouldn't take down the whole notification pipeline, only degrade that one channel, ideally with a fallback (retry via a secondary email provider, or fall back to in-app-only if push fails repeatedly). Delivery status flows back through a separate receipt queue — sent → delivered → failed — updating a status store that the product can query ("was this actually delivered?"), decoupled from the send path so a slow status update never blocks the next notification from going out.

Idempotency, connected explicitly: every notification carries an idempotency key (Section 2.7) — typically a hash of (user, event, notification-type, time-bucket) — so a retried event (the queue redelivering after a consumer crash, Section 2.8) can't double-notify a user; the consumer checks the key against a short-lived dedup store before sending.

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "Respect quiet hours (no notifications 10pm–8am) without losing the notification entirely." — queue it (don't drop it) and deliver at the start of the user's next allowed window, re-running it through the batching logic above so quiet-hours-deferred notifications from the same night still coalesce sensibly rather than arriving as a flood at 8:00am sharp.
  • "A push token is invalid (the user uninstalled the app) — what happens?" — the provider's send call returns an explicit invalid-token error; the sender should mark that token dead in the user's device-token store on the first such error (not retry it) so the system stops wasting send attempts on a channel that will never succeed again.
  • "Prioritize a security alert ('new login from unknown device') over a 'someone liked your photo' notification." — separate priority queues (Section 2.8), with security/transactional notifications on a high-priority queue that bypasses batching entirely and is consumed by dedicated, unshared worker capacity, so a backlog of low-priority social notifications can never delay a security-critical one.

Group C — Communication & Collaboration

Design WhatsApp / a Chat System

Why this problem, specifically: it's the case study that most directly tests persistent-connection infrastructure — every other problem in this guide is fundamentally request/response, but a chat system's defining constraint is millions of long-lived, stateful WebSocket connections that a stateless-services mindset (Section 2.16) doesn't directly solve, forcing a genuinely different architecture.

Clarifying questions: 1:1 only, or groups too (assume both)? Is end-to-end encryption in scope? Multi-device (same account, phone + desktop simultaneously)? Media messages (photos/video), or text only for the core design?

Functional requirements: send/receive messages in real time; deliver messages to offline users once they reconnect; group messaging; delivery and read receipts; message history synced across a user's devices.

Non-functional requirements: very low latency for online-to-online delivery; no message loss, ever, even across server crashes and reconnects; ordering preserved within a conversation; massive number of concurrent persistent connections (billions of users, a meaningful fraction simultaneously connected).

Back-of-envelope estimation:

Metric Assumption Result
DAU 500M
Messages/day 40 per user 20B messages/day ≈ 230K messages/sec average (peak much higher)
Concurrent connections ~150M at peak each WebSocket costs ~10KB server-side → ~1.5TB just to hold connection state, spread across thousands of connection servers

That connection-state number is the whole architectural justification for the next section — it's far too much for any single machine, and it's state a load balancer can't just shard by request the way it would stateless HTTP traffic.

Connection routing, worked through — the piece that makes the whole thing work: a message from A to B has to reach whichever specific connection server currently holds B's live socket, out of thousands of servers. A fast, shared routing table (Redis: userId → connectionServerId) is updated every time a client connects/disconnects/reconnects; the server handling A's outgoing message looks up B's current connection server and forwards the message there for final delivery — this lookup-then-forward hop is the one piece of real coordination in an otherwise horizontally-scaled fleet of connection servers.

Message ordering, worked through: with many connection servers involved, two messages in the same conversation could theoretically be processed slightly out of order if nothing enforces sequencing. The fix: a per-conversation sequence number, assigned by whichever single component owns the write for that conversation (commonly: the message queue partition for that conversation_id, since Section 2.8 guarantees ordering within a partition) — clients use this sequence number to detect and correct any out-of-order delivery on their end, rather than trusting network delivery order.

Delivery and read receipts, worked through as a genuinely separate concern: don't bolt delivery-state onto the message record itself — model it as its own small event stream (sent → delivered → read), each transition triggering a lightweight event back to the sender's device. This separation matters because receipts have different semantics than messages (they can be lost/late without the conversation being wrong, just briefly stale — unlike a dropped message, which is unacceptable) and different privacy rules (a user can disable read receipts without disabling message delivery).

Offline delivery, worked through: if B's routing-table lookup finds no active connection, the message is still durably persisted (never dropped) and a push notification (APNs/FCM) is sent as a wake-up signal; the actual message content syncs when B's client next connects and requests everything since its last-known sequence number per conversation — the push notification is a nudge, never the actual delivery mechanism, an important distinction since push delivery itself is best-effort and unreliable.

Group chat fan-out — the same trade-off as Twitter, at a different scale: a small group (a handful to a few dozen members) simply pushes the message to every member's connection server directly — cheap, since the fan-out factor is tiny. A very large group (WhatsApp caps groups at 1,024 members partly for exactly this reason) starts to resemble the celebrity fan-out problem (Group B) — worth naming the parallel explicitly even though group chat rarely needs the full push/pull hybrid, since the group-size cap is itself a simpler, product-level solution to the same underlying fan-out cost problem.

Multi-device sync, worked through: each device maintains its own per-device cursor (the last sequence number it's synced, per conversation) rather than the account having one global read position — sending a message writes to a durable, per-user message log; each of a user's devices independently pulls everything newer than its own cursor, so a phone and a laptop can be at different points in the same conversation's history without conflicting.

End-to-end encryption, at a high level (worth naming, not implementing): the Signal Protocol's core idea is a "double ratchet" — every message uses a new derived key, so compromising one message's key doesn't expose past or future messages (forward secrecy) — with initial key exchange happening once per device pair. The practical system-design consequence: the server cannot read message content (it only ever handles encrypted blobs), so anything the server needs to do with message content — search, spam detection — either can't be done server-side at all, or must happen client-side before encryption, which is exactly the kind of trade-off worth surfacing if a design needs both E2E encryption and, say, search.

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "A connection server crashes — what happens to its connected users?" — every affected client's WebSocket drops, each reconnects (typically to a different, healthy server via the load balancer), and the routing table is updated on reconnect — no message loss, since messages were already durably persisted before this crash, only a brief reconnection delay.
  • "How does 'last seen' / presence work at this scale?" — a lightweight, separate presence service (Redis with short TTLs refreshed by periodic heartbeats from each connected client) — deliberately not routed through the same durable message-persistence path, since presence is inherently ephemeral, high-frequency, and tolerant of being briefly stale.
  • "Support message editing and deletion." — both are new events referencing the original message ID (an "edit" event, a "tombstone" event) appended to the same ordered stream, rather than mutating the original stored message in place — preserving the append-only, replayable structure the rest of the design already depends on.

Design Slack

Why this problem, specifically: it's deliberately framed as "the chat system above, plus the two things that are genuinely new" — search and very-large-channel fan-out — so a strong answer explicitly reuses the WhatsApp architecture rather than re-deriving connection management from scratch, then spends its time on what's actually different.

Clarifying questions: are threads (replies nested under a specific message) in scope? Is cross-workspace search needed, or always scoped to one workspace? File sharing and its size limits?

What's reused wholesale from the Chat System case study above: WebSocket connection servers, the routing table, per-conversation (here, per-channel) sequence numbers, offline delivery via push — none of this changes moving from 1:1/small-group chat to channel-based team chat; restating it would be wasted interview time better spent on the two genuinely new problems below.

Search, worked through — a first-class requirement, not an afterthought: every message is indexed into a text-search engine (Elasticsearch, Section 2.5) as it's sent, typically via the same async pipeline pattern used elsewhere in this guide (write the message, then asynchronously index it — never block the send path on indexing latency). Search is scoped per-workspace (an index per workspace, or a single index with a mandatory workspace_id filter on every query) both for access-control correctness (a user must never see another workspace's messages, even accidentally) and for query performance (searching within one workspace's messages, not the entire platform's).

Channel fan-out at scale, worked through with the actual numbers that make it a real problem: a channel with 10 members pushes trivially to 10 WebSocket connections. A channel with 50,000 members (common in large organizations) pushing one message means fanning out to 50,000 live connections if they're all online simultaneously — structurally identical to the celebrity-account fan-out problem from Twitter (Group B), just triggered by channel size instead of follower count. The same hybrid instinct applies: for very large channels, consider whether every member genuinely needs an instant push, or whether some can tolerate a slightly-pulled model (fetch-on-open for members who aren't actively viewing the channel right now) — worth naming as the same underlying trade-off wearing a different costume, which is exactly the kind of pattern-recognition interviewers are listening for.

Threads, worked through as a small, contained modeling decision: a threaded reply is a message with a parent_message_id, displayed nested under its parent but not re-triggering a full-channel notification the way a top-level message does (only thread participants and anyone who explicitly follows the thread get notified) — a much shallower structure than Reddit's arbitrarily-deep nesting (Group B), since Slack threads are exactly one level deep by design, which simplifies both storage and the notification-fan-out logic considerably.

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "Slash commands and bot integrations?" — modeled as a webhook (Section 2.2): a slash command triggers an outbound HTTP call to a registered external endpoint, which responds (synchronously or via a follow-up callback) with content to post back into the channel — the same request/response-plus-async-callback shape used for third-party payment integrations elsewhere in this guide.
  • "A user mutes a channel — how does notification behavior change without changing the message-delivery path itself?" — muting is purely a notification-service-layer preference (Section 2.15's Notification System), checked when deciding whether to push an alert — it never touches message delivery/persistence, which keeps the two concerns (deliver the message vs. notify about it) cleanly separated exactly as the News Feed case study's retrieval/ranking split argued for generally.

Design Google Docs (real-time collaborative editing)

Why this problem, specifically: OT vs. CRDT is one of the most conceptually rich topics in all of system design — it's a genuine algorithms problem wearing a systems-design costume, and interviewers use it to see whether you can reason precisely about why naive approaches (last-write-wins) silently corrupt data, not just that a fancier algorithm exists.

Clarifying questions: how many concurrent editors realistically, on one document (a handful, or thousands)? Does offline editing need to be supported, with merge-on-reconnect? Is this rich text (formatting, images) or plain text for the core design?

Why "last write wins" is wrong — worked through concretely, since this is worth explicitly rejecting out loud before presenting the real answer: if user A types "hello" and user B, concurrently, types "world" at the same cursor position, a naive server that just applies whichever write arrives last silently drops one user's entire edit — not a conflict warning, just data loss with no error. Both real solutions exist specifically to make concurrent edits compose correctly instead of overwrite each other.

Operational Transformation (OT), worked through with an actual example: every edit is expressed as an operation (insert(position, text), delete(position, length)). When two operations happen concurrently, each is transformed against the other before being applied, adjusting position offsets so the end result reflects both edits' intent. Concretely: document is "AC"; user 1 inserts "B" at position 1 (intending "ABC"); concurrently, user 2 deletes the character at position 1 ("C", intending "A"). Applied naively in arrival order, these two operations can corrupt each other (the delete might remove the just-inserted "B" instead of the "C" it meant to, depending on arrival order) — OT's transform function adjusts user 2's delete-at-position-1 into delete-at-position-2 once it knows user 1's insert landed first, so the delete still removes the originally-intended "C" regardless of arrival order. This transform logic must run centrally (this is why Google Docs' real implementation uses a single ordering authority) — the server sees every operation, establishes one true order, and transforms accordingly, which is a genuinely complex piece of code to get right (real-world OT implementations are notorious for subtle bugs in edge cases).

CRDTs (Conflict-free Replicated Data Types), worked through as the modern alternative: instead of transforming operations against each other, structure the data itself so that concurrent updates always converge to the same result no matter what order they're applied in, with no central coordinator required at all. For collaborative text specifically, a common approach (RGA — Replicated Growable Array, or fractional-indexing-based sequence CRDTs) gives every character a unique, globally-ordered position identifier assigned at insert time (not just a numeric index, which shifts under concurrent edits) — two users inserting at "the same visual position" concurrently get different, deterministically-ordered position identifiers, so both insertions survive and end up in a stable, consistent (if not always intuitively "correct") relative order on every replica, with zero coordination needed at write time. This is why CRDTs are the more common choice in newer systems (Figma, Linear, most modern collaborative tools): they work correctly even fully offline/peer-to-peer, since convergence is a property of the data structure itself, not of a central server being reachable.

OT vs. CRDT, the actual trade-off to state: OT produces smaller, simpler operations and has decades of production hardening (Google Docs), but requires a central transformation authority and is notoriously hard to implement correctly for rich, nested document structures. CRDTs need no central coordinator and handle offline/peer-to-peer editing naturally, at the cost of larger metadata overhead per character/element (each needs a globally-unique, sortable identifier, not just an index) and a design space that's still maturing for complex rich-text structures. Naming both, and picking one with a stated reason ("CRDT, because offline editing was an explicit requirement" or "OT, because Google Docs' own production history is the strongest evidence it scales") reads far stronger than presenting only one as if it were the only option.

Persistence, worked through — this is event sourcing (Section 2.11) applied directly: the document's true state is the full operation log, not a single mutable row — exactly the event-sourcing pattern from Part II, here with the operation log as the write model. Reconstructing a document means replaying its operation log from the start, which gets slow for a long-lived, heavily-edited document — so periodic snapshots are taken (a materialized full-document state at a given point), and loading a document means "latest snapshot, plus replaying only the operations since it," not the entire history from time zero.

Presence and cursors, worked through: other users' live cursor positions are broadcast over the same WebSocket channel but are never persisted — they're pure ephemeral state (Redis or in-memory on the document server, gone the instant a user disconnects), since a cursor position has no meaning once its owner isn't actively looking at the document, unlike the durable operation log.

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "What if the document server for a given document dies mid-session?" — every connected client's socket drops and reconnects, routed to a new document server (or a replica) that loads the latest snapshot plus subsequent ops from the durable log — exactly the same crash-recovery shape as the Chat System's connection-server failure above, since both rely on "durable log + reconnect + resync," not on the serving process itself surviving.
  • "Support offline editing with merge on reconnect." — this is precisely where CRDTs earn their keep over OT: a client edits its local CRDT-structured copy while offline, and on reconnect simply sends its accumulated local operations to merge — the CRDT's convergence guarantee handles the merge correctly without needing to replay a live transformation sequence against operations it never saw while offline.
  • "How would you handle access control — some users can view, others can edit?" — a permissions check at the WebSocket connection/authorization layer (Section 2.12), gating which operations a given connection is allowed to emit (a viewer's client can still receive the live op stream to render the document, but the document server rejects any edit operation from a connection not authorized to write) — kept as a layer in front of the OT/CRDT machinery, not tangled into it.

Design an Email Service (Gmail-style)

Why this problem, specifically: it's one of the few case studies genuinely rooted in decades-old, standardized internet protocols rather than a system you'd design from scratch — the interview signal is whether you know when to use the existing protocol (SMTP) versus where the interesting, novel engineering actually is (storage at mailbox scale, and search).

Clarifying questions: sending only, or also receiving and long-term storage (assume both)? Is spam/phishing filtering in scope? Attachments — inline, or referenced from separate storage?

Functional requirements: send email to any address on the open internet; receive and durably store incoming email; search a mailbox with years of accumulated history; organize via labels/folders.

Sending, worked through — protocol-driven, not something to redesign: outbound mail uses SMTP, relayed through the sending provider's outbound mail servers. Deliverability (whether the receiving provider trusts the message enough to put it in the inbox rather than spam) rests on three DNS-based verification mechanisms worth naming precisely: SPF (a DNS record listing which mail servers are authorized to send on behalf of a domain), DKIM (a cryptographic signature over the message, verifiable against a public key published in DNS, proving the message wasn't altered in transit), and DMARC (a policy telling receiving servers what to do if SPF/DKIM checks fail — reject, quarantine, or allow). A message missing all three is, correctly, routinely dropped into spam by every major provider — this is worth stating as a real, functioning distributed-trust system running on top of DNS, not an obscure detail.

Storage, worked through — the part that's genuinely a hard systems problem: mailboxes are sharded by user ID (Section 2.6) — a natural partition key, since almost every access pattern (read your inbox, search your mail) is scoped to exactly one user. A mailbox can accumulate a decade or more of mail, so storage is tiered: recent mail on fast, readily-available storage; older mail moved to cheaper, higher-latency object storage (Section 2.16), fetched transparently on the rare access — the same hot/cold tiering principle as the Logging Infrastructure case study (Group G), applied to personal mailboxes instead of operational logs.

Search, worked through: a per-user (or per-shard) inverted index (Section 2.5) — structurally the same tool as a general web search engine (Group A's Web Crawler extension), just scoped to one mailbox's documents instead of the entire web, which conveniently also keeps each individual index small enough to stay fast without needing search's usual sharded scatter-gather.

Spam filtering, worked through as an async, best-effort-blocking gate: filtering has to happen at ingestion, before a message is shown to the recipient, combining fast heuristics/blocklist checks (near-instant) with a heavier ML classifier — and critically, this check has to be async-tolerant: holding up mail delivery waiting on a slow classifier undermines the core promise that email is reliable and prompt, so a common real pattern is a fast synchronous coarse check (obvious blocklist hits rejected immediately) plus an async, slightly-delayed fine-grained classification that can still move a message to spam moments after it briefly appeared in the inbox.

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "Handle large attachments." — never store attachments inline in the message record; store them in object storage (Section 2.16) and reference them by ID/URL from the message metadata — identical reasoning to Instagram's presigned-upload pattern (Group B), avoiding ever proxying large binary content through the mail-processing path itself.
  • "Show a threaded conversation view, not just a flat inbox." — group messages by a stable thread identifier, derived from email's own standard References/In-Reply-To headers (a mechanism that already exists in the SMTP standard specifically for this) rather than inventing new thread-detection logic — another example of using the existing protocol instead of redesigning around it.
  • "Labels that let one email appear in multiple views (not exclusive folders)?" — a many-to-many message_id ↔ label_id mapping table, rather than a single folder column on the message — a small but important modeling choice, since a single-folder model can't represent "this email is both Starred and in Receipts" at all.

Group D — Media & Streaming

Design YouTube

Why this problem, specifically: it's the canonical bandwidth-bound system in this entire guide — almost every other case study is bottlenecked on compute, storage IOPS, or coordination, while this one is dominated by the sheer number of bytes moving to viewers, which changes which parts of the design actually matter.

Clarifying questions: live streaming in scope, or upload-then-watch only (assume the latter as the core problem, live as an extension)? Recommendations in scope, or purely upload/transcode/playback? Any constraint on max video length/file size?

Functional requirements: upload a video; the platform makes it available for streaming at multiple quality levels; viewers can play, seek, and have playback adapt to their current bandwidth; view counts and comments.

Non-functional requirements: playback must start quickly and never stall for a well-connected viewer; the system must serve an enormous, highly skewed read volume (a small fraction of videos account for a huge fraction of views) cost-effectively; upload and transcoding can tolerate minutes of latency — playback cannot tolerate more than a second or two.

Back-of-envelope estimation — the numbers that justify every architectural choice below:

Metric Assumption Result
Uploads/day 500K videos, avg 10 min, ~50MB/min raw ≈ 25PB/day arriving at the transcoding pipeline
Views/day 5B ≈ 58K playback starts/sec average, far higher at peak
Egress bandwidth 5B views × ~150MB average streamed multiple exabytes/month of egress — this single number is why a CDN isn't optional infrastructure here, it's the majority of the whole system's cost and engineering effort

Architecture:

    Up --> Raw[("Raw video

    Q --> W1["Transcode worker

    Variants --> Manifest["Manifest generator

Upload, worked through: resumable, chunked upload directly to object storage (the same presigned-URL pattern as Instagram, Group B) — critical here specifically because upload files are large (potentially gigabytes) and mobile/unreliable connections make "restart the whole upload on any hiccup" unacceptable; chunking means only the failed chunk needs retrying.

The transcoding pipeline, worked through in real depth — this is the heart of the interview: raw upload triggers an async job (never block the upload response on transcoding, which can take many minutes). The job is parallelized by splitting the source video into short chunks (a few seconds each) that can be transcoded concurrently across many workers, then reassembled — this is precisely why a multi-hour source video doesn't take multi-hours to become available; it's a batch/parallel-processing problem (Section 2.17's MapReduce-style shape) applied to video. Output is multiple resolution/bitrate variants (240p through 4K) in multiple codecs (for device/browser compatibility), each itself split into short segments (2–10 seconds) — this segmentation is what makes adaptive bitrate streaming possible at all.

Adaptive bitrate streaming, worked through mechanically — the specific technique that makes "smooth playback on any connection" work: the transcoding pipeline produces a manifest file (HLS's .m3u8 or DASH's .mpd) listing every available resolution variant and the URLs of its individual segments. The video player continuously measures its own recent download throughput and, before requesting each new segment, picks whichever variant's bitrate its current bandwidth estimate can sustain — meaning quality can step up or down mid-playback, segment by segment, without ever interrupting playback to rebuffer from scratch. This client-driven decision (not server-pushed) is deliberate: the server doesn't need to know or guess a viewer's network conditions at all, it just needs to have already produced every variant the manifest advertises.

CDN strategy, applied concretely here (Section 2.4's general mechanics, at YouTube's actual scale): the overwhelming majority of bytes are served from edge caches, never touching origin storage per-request — an origin shield layer absorbs the cache-miss traffic from many edge PoPs so a newly-viral video causes at most one fetch per region from origin, not one fetch per edge node. Cold, rarely-watched long-tail videos rely more on pull-CDN dynamics (cached on first request); hot, predictably-popular content can be proactively pushed/pre-warmed into edge caches ahead of expected demand (a scheduled premiere, for instance).

View counts at scale, worked through: a synchronous UPDATE videos SET views = views + 1 per playback start would create brutal write contention on popular videos (Section 2.5's hot-row problem, again) — the practical fix is the same one used in Reddit's vote counting (Group B): buffer increments (in-memory counters, or a Redis INCR) and periodically flush an aggregated delta to durable storage, trading a few seconds of read staleness for removing per-view write contention from the hot path entirely. At truly massive scale, exact counts are sometimes deliberately relaxed to approximate counts (HyperLogLog, Section 2.19) when "exactly how many" matters less than "roughly how many, cheaply."

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "Live streaming, not just upload-then-watch?" — fundamentally different latency requirements: low-latency HLS/DASH variants (shorter segments, more aggressive client buffering trade-offs) or WebRTC (sub-second latency, at the cost of much higher server-side complexity and cost) for true real-time interaction — worth naming that "live" isn't just "upload with less delay," it's a different point on the latency/complexity curve requiring different protocols.
  • "Detect copyrighted content in uploads (ContentID-style)." — an async pipeline (never blocks the upload) that computes a perceptual fingerprint of the video/audio and matches it against a database of known copyrighted fingerprints — conceptually similar to the content-hash deduplication idea from a file-sync system, applied to detecting near-duplicates (a re-encoded, cropped, or slightly edited copy) rather than exact-duplicates.
  • "Why not just serve everything from one giant origin datacenter?" — walk back to the egress-bandwidth number in the estimation table: at exabyte-per-month egress, both the network cost and the physical distance-driven latency make a CDN not an optimization but a structural requirement — say the number, don't just assert the conclusion.

Design Spotify

Why this problem, specifically: it deliberately reuses YouTube's storage/CDN backbone (audio has the same "upload → process → serve via CDN" shape) so the interview can spend its time on what's genuinely different — personalization at the core of the product, collaborative data structures, and offline access with licensing constraints.

Clarifying questions: are collaborative/shared playlists in scope? Offline downloads (and therefore DRM) in scope? Is recommendation-quality itself in scope, or just the serving architecture around it?

What's reused wholesale from YouTube: object storage for audio files, a (much lighter-weight, since audio files are orders of magnitude smaller than video) transcoding step for multiple bitrate variants, and CDN delivery — restating this would waste interview time; naming that it's reused, and why the bandwidth math is fundamentally different (audio egress is a small fraction of video egress at the same user count), is a stronger use of the time.

Recommendations, worked through — the actual center of gravity for this product: the same two-phase shape as the News Feed and generic Recommendation System case studies (Group B, Group J): a cheap candidate generation step (collaborative filtering — "users with similar listening history also played X" — computed largely offline/batch over the full listening-history dataset, Section 2.17) narrows millions of tracks down to a manageable shortlist, followed by a ranking step (a model scoring that shortlist for this specific user, this specific context — time of day, recent listening session) run online. A feature specifically worth naming — "Discover Weekly"-style personalized playlists — is this exact pipeline run as a scheduled batch job rather than computed live: since the output only needs to refresh weekly, there's no reason to pay online-serving latency/cost for it, an explicit example of matching computation timing to the actual freshness requirement rather than defaulting to "real-time" reflexively.

Playlists, worked through as a data-modeling problem: a playlist is an ordered list of track references, not a copy of track data — storing an explicit position/rank per (playlist, track) pair (an integer position, or a fractional-indexing scheme identical in spirit to the CRDT sequence-ordering idea from Google Docs, Group C) so reordering or inserting a track doesn't require renumbering the whole list. Collaborative playlists (multiple users editing the same playlist concurrently) are a smaller-scale version of the exact same concurrent-editing problem Google Docs solves — worth naming the parallel explicitly rather than re-deriving a conflict-resolution scheme from scratch: two users adding tracks concurrently should both survive (an append is naturally conflict-free), while two users reordering concurrently benefit from the same fractional-indexing trick that avoids needing a central lock on the whole list.

Offline downloads and DRM, worked through: a downloaded track isn't a plain cached file — it's encrypted with a license tied to the user's active subscription, checked periodically (the app must "phone home" within some window, commonly a few weeks, to confirm the subscription is still active, or downloaded content stops playing) — this is a licensing/business-rule requirement as much as a technical one, worth naming as the reason offline support is meaningfully more complex than "just cache the file locally."

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "How would you calculate royalty payouts per stream, at this scale?" — a batch aggregation job (Section 2.17) over the raw play-event stream, not a live running counter — royalty calculation tolerates being computed daily/monthly in bulk, which is a far simpler and cheaper problem than trying to maintain it as a real-time counter the way view counts above are handled.
  • "A user's music taste shifts (they start listening to a new genre) — how quickly does the recommendation system adapt?" — this is a cold-start-adjacent problem (Group J's Recommendation System case study) even for an existing user: blend the slow-moving batch-computed long-term taste profile with a fast, session-level online signal (what they've played in just the last hour) so recommendations can react to a listening-session shift without waiting for the next full batch recompute.

Design Netflix

Why this problem, specifically: unlike the other two streaming case studies, this one is less about a single hard technical sub-problem and more about organizational and resilience architecture at scale — it's the case study to reach for when an interviewer wants to talk about microservices, chaos engineering, and multi-region failure as first-class design topics rather than footnotes.

Clarifying questions: is the interview focused on the streaming/playback path (closer to the YouTube case study), the recommendation/personalization layer, or the resilience/infrastructure story (assume resilience/infrastructure, since that's what makes this case study distinct from YouTube)?

Why microservices, concretely — the canonical real-world justification, not just the general Section 2.1 argument: Netflix is the textbook example precisely because their public engineering writing documents why they moved off a monolith — a single monolithic failure could (and, notoriously, once did) take down the entire service; splitting into independently deployable, independently scalable services means a failure in, say, the recommendations service degrades recommendations, not playback. This is Section 2.1's fault-isolation argument for microservices made concrete with a real, publicly-documented incident-driven motivation, which is a stronger answer than reciting the generic trade-off table.

Open Connect, worked through — a more aggressive CDN strategy than the generic pattern in Section 2.4: rather than relying purely on third-party CDN edge nodes, Netflix places its own caching appliances directly inside ISP networks and internet exchange points — physically as close to end users as infrastructure allows, often literally inside the ISP's own facilities. This matters because it minimizes not just latency but inter-network transit cost — video traffic that would otherwise cross expensive peering links between Netflix's infrastructure and every ISP's network instead terminates almost entirely within the ISP's own network. Worth stating as a deliberate cost-and-latency optimization enabled by Netflix's traffic volume being large enough to justify ISPs hosting dedicated hardware for one company — not a strategy every system at every scale can adopt.

Chaos Engineering, worked through as a discipline, not just "Chaos Monkey": the Chaos Monkey family is a progression of deliberately-induced failures at increasing scope — Chaos Monkey itself randomly terminates individual production instances (does the system tolerate losing one box?); Latency Monkey injects artificial delay into service calls (does a circuit breaker, Section 2.1, actually trip when it should?); Chaos Kong simulates the loss of an entire AWS region (does multi-region failover actually work, or only in theory?). The underlying philosophy, worth stating explicitly: resilience claims that have never been tested under real, deliberately-injected failure are hypotheses, not properties — Section 2.9's "design for failure" principle, operationalized as an ongoing engineering practice rather than a one-time design review.

Multi-region resilience, worked through: an active-active deployment (Section 2.9) across multiple regions means any single region's failure — even a full Chaos-Kong-style outage — leaves the service still fully operational from surviving regions, at the cost of needing to handle cross-region data consistency for anything that isn't purely regional (a user's "continue watching" position needs to be visible regardless of which region now serves their traffic, which pushes back toward the replication and consistency trade-offs from Section 2.6/2.18).

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "Personalized artwork — different users see different thumbnail images for the same title." — this is A/B-tested content selection at the recommendation layer (Group J), not a video-encoding concern: multiple thumbnail images are pre-generated and stored per title, and the serving layer picks which one to show a given user based on their inferred preferences (this specific mechanism is real and publicly documented by Netflix).
  • "Per-title encoding optimization — why not use one fixed bitrate ladder for every video?" — a fast-motion action movie and a static talking-head video have very different complexity profiles; encoding every title against the same fixed resolution/bitrate ladder wastes bits on simple content and under-serves complex content — per-title optimized encoding analyzes each video's actual complexity and picks a tailored ladder, trading more upfront encoding compute for meaningfully better quality-per-byte at playback, directly reducing the CDN egress cost from the YouTube case study's back-of-envelope math.

Group E — Location, Marketplace & Real-Time Coordination

Design Uber (rider-driver matching)

Why this problem, specifically: it's the best vehicle in this entire guide for demonstrating that you can run two different consistency models side by side in one system — location data is deliberately AP (a slightly stale driver position is harmless), while trip and payment state is deliberately CP (an inconsistent trip status is a real financial/safety problem) — and knowing why each half gets its own treatment is the actual interview signal.

Clarifying questions: is routing/ETA computation in scope, or just matching (assume matching is the core, ETA as an extension — it's effectively the Google Maps case study's routing problem, reused)? Is dynamic pricing in scope? Scheduled (book-ahead) rides?

Functional requirements: a rider requests a trip and is matched to a nearby available driver; both parties see live location during the trip; fare is calculated and charged on completion.

Non-functional requirements: matching latency must be very low (seconds, not tens of seconds); the system must handle a huge volume of frequent, low-stakes location updates efficiently; trip and payment state must never be lost or inconsistent, even if location tracking briefly is.

Geospatial indexing, in real depth — the three real options, compared, not just named:

Structure Mechanics Trade-off
Geohash Encode (lat, lng) into a base-32 string; shared prefixes mean geographic proximity Simple, but cells are rectangular and distort badly near the poles; a "nearby" search near a cell boundary requires checking neighboring cells explicitly
Quadtree Recursively subdivide the map into four quadrants until each leaf cell holds a small, bounded number of points Naturally adapts cell size to point density — dense cities get finer cells, sparse rural areas get coarser ones — at the cost of a tree structure that's more complex to maintain under constant point movement
H3 (Uber's own, hexagonal) Divides the Earth into a hierarchical grid of hexagonal cells; each cell is a compact 64-bit ID; a cell has exactly one parent and subdivides into 7 children for multi-resolution queries Hexagons have uniform distance to all six neighbors — a square grid's diagonal neighbor is measurably farther than its edge neighbor, which distorts any distance-based aggregation (exactly the kind Uber does constantly for matching and surge pricing)

Why hexagons, precisely — worth being able to state the actual geometric reason, not just "Uber uses H3": on a square grid, "nearest neighbor" is ambiguous — 4 edge-adjacent cells are closer than the 4 diagonal-adjacent ones, so any algorithm treating all 8 as equally "adjacent" is systematically biased. A hexagonal grid has exactly 6 neighbors, all equidistant from the center cell, which removes that bias entirely — genuinely important when the whole point of the structure is fast, accurate proximity search.

The matching algorithm, worked through — not just "find nearby drivers," the actual dispatch logic: query the rider's H3 cell and expand outward to neighboring cells until enough candidate drivers are found (typically a handful of rings outward is sufficient in any reasonably dense area). Candidates are then ranked not by straight-line distance but by estimated ETA (via the routing engine, since a driver 500m away on the wrong side of a highway can have a worse ETA than one 1.5km away with a direct route) — combined with secondary factors like driver rating and fairness (avoiding always sending requests to the same nearest driver while others nearby go unmatched). Real production systems (per Uber's own publicly documented approach) often run this as batched, not purely greedy, matching — collecting open ride requests and available drivers over a short window (a second or two) and solving a small bipartite-matching optimization across all of them at once, which produces measurably better fleet-wide outcomes than matching each request the instant it arrives, at the cost of a small, deliberate delay.

Location update pipeline, worked through — why 250K/sec doesn't hit a database directly: each ping updates the driver's position in the in-memory geospatial index directly (Redis or a custom in-process structure) with a short TTL — there is no durability requirement for a single stale ping, since it's superseded within seconds by the next one. Durable storage only captures a sampled or aggregated trail (useful for analytics, ETA-model training, trip-route reconstruction after the fact), never every raw ping — this is the same batching/decimation instinct as the view-count and vote-count problems elsewhere in this guide, applied to location instead of a counter.

Surge pricing, worked through: aggregate supply (available drivers) vs. demand (open, unmatched requests) per H3 cell, computed continuously via a stream-processing job (Section 2.17 — Flink-style windowed aggregation), and translate the resulting supply/demand ratio into a price multiplier for that cell, updated on a short interval (not instantaneously per-request, which would make pricing feel erratic to riders) — the same geospatial cell structure built for matching is directly reused here, which is worth stating as evidence the two problems share infrastructure rather than needing separate systems.

The CP half — trip and payment state, worked through: once a match is made, the trip transitions through an explicit state machine (requested → accepted → arrived → in_progress → completed), each transition a durable, strongly-consistent write (Section 2.18) — this is exactly where the design deliberately leaves the AP world of location pings behind, because an inconsistent or lost trip-state transition is a real correctness and billing problem, not a cosmetic one. Payment on completion goes through the same idempotency-key discipline (Section 2.19) as the dedicated Payment System case study, since a network retry on trip completion must never double-charge a rider.

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "A driver's GPS is inaccurate or goes briefly offline mid-trip." — the location index treats a driver as unavailable once their last ping exceeds a TTL (the same expiry mechanism as Redis's own key TTLs, Group A) — the trip itself isn't affected (trip state lives in the strongly-consistent Trip Service, independent of live location), only the rider's live-map view degrades gracefully to "last known position" until pings resume.
  • "Support scheduled, book-ahead rides." — a separate scheduling component (structurally close to the Distributed Scheduler case study, Group G) that triggers the normal real-time matching flow at the scheduled time, rather than trying to reserve a specific driver hours in advance (driver availability that far out isn't a meaningful guarantee anyway).
  • "Multiple stops on one trip." — the Trip Service's state machine gains intermediate waypoint states between in_progress and completed; the routing engine recomputes ETA at each stop — a data-model and state-machine extension, not a change to the matching/dispatch logic that got the driver assigned in the first place.

Design Airbnb

Why this problem, specifically: it combines two problems this guide has already built real muscle for — geospatial + faceted search, and a concurrency-correctness problem — but the concurrency problem here is genuinely harder than the ones seen so far, because it's a date-range overlap, not a simple equality check, and that distinction is exactly what interviewers probe.

Clarifying questions: instant-book, or does every booking need host approval first (changes the concurrency story significantly, below)? Is dynamic/smart pricing in scope? Multi-night stays only, or also flexible date-range search ("any 3 nights this month")?

Functional requirements: hosts list properties with photos, amenities, a nightly price, and an availability calendar; guests search by location, date range, and filters; guests book specific, available dates.

Search, worked through: combines geospatial filtering (the identical H3/geohash toolbox from the Uber case study — "listings within this map viewport") with structured facets (price range, amenities, guest count) — a combination that makes a plain relational query increasingly impractical as filter combinations multiply, which is why this is backed by a dedicated search engine (Elasticsearch, Section 2.5) rather than SQL WHERE clauses stacked on an indexed table.

The booking-correctness problem, worked through precisely — this is the harder version of the Ticket-Booking case study's seat lock: the Movie-Ticket and Ticket-Booking case studies (Part I, Part III Group F) prevent double-booking a single discrete seat with an equality check (WHERE status = AVAILABLE). Here, two bookings conflict if their date ranges overlap at all — booking Mar 10–15 and booking Mar 13–18 conflict even though neither request matches the other's exact dates, which a simple equality-based conditional update cannot express. The real-world answer: a database-level exclusion constraint over the date range itself (PostgreSQL's EXCLUDE USING gist (listing_id WITH =, date_range WITH &&) is the concrete mechanism — it rejects any insert whose date range overlaps an existing row for the same listing, at the database layer, atomically) — this is the range-query generalization of the atomic conditional update pattern used everywhere else in this guide, and naming the specific mechanism (not just "check for overlap in application code," which reintroduces exactly the check-then-act race this whole guide has been warning about since Part I) is what separates a strong answer here.

Instant-book vs. host-approval, worked through as a real branch in the flow: instant-book follows the pattern above directly — the exclusion constraint enforces correctness at write time. Host-approval is different: a booking request holds the dates in a pending state (structurally similar to the seat-hold TTL pattern, but with a longer, host-response-driven expiry rather than a fixed few minutes) — and here's the interesting edge case worth naming unprompted: two guests can both have a pending, unconfirmed request for overlapping dates simultaneously (since neither has been confirmed yet, only one hold), and the host approving one must atomically reject/expire the other's hold — the same conditional-update discipline, just triggered by a host action instead of a payment success.

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "Support flexible-date search ('any 3-night stay in the next month')?" — rather than searching for exact date-range availability per listing, precompute and index each listing's available date ranges (as intervals, not per-night rows) so a flexible query can match against interval overlaps directly in the search index — an extension of the same range-based thinking as the booking constraint above, moved into the read/search path.
  • "Cancellation policies with different refund tiers based on how close to check-in the cancellation happens?" — a policy lookup (a Strategy-shaped decision, Part I) evaluated against (cancellation_time, check_in_time) at cancellation time — a business-rule computation layered cleanly on top of the booking state machine, not entangled with the overlap-prevention logic itself.
  • "Hosts syncing availability from other platforms (iCal import/export)?" — external calendars are pulled/parsed periodically and materialized into the same availability structure the exclusion constraint protects — the correctness guarantee doesn't change just because the source of a blocked date is external rather than a direct booking on this platform.

Design Google Maps / "Nearby Places"

Why this problem, specifically: it's really two unrelated hard problems bundled under one product name — proximity search (which by now should feel like a solved, reusable toolbox) and route-finding (a genuinely different graph-algorithms problem this guide hasn't touched yet) — and the interview signal is recognizing that split immediately rather than trying to force one technique to solve both.

"Nearby places," worked through — explicitly reused, not re-derived: this is the identical geospatial-indexing toolbox as Uber and Airbnb above (Geohash/Quadtree/H3) — index points of interest into the same kind of cell structure, query the user's viewport/cell and expand outward. Stating this reuse explicitly, rather than re-explaining geospatial indexing from scratch a third time in one interview loop, is itself a signal of efficient thinking.

Routing — the genuinely new problem, worked through in depth: model the road network as a graph (intersections as nodes, road segments as weighted edges — weight commonly a function of distance and speed limit). Dijkstra's algorithm finds the shortest path correctly, but is far too slow to run online at road-network scale (a continental road network has hundreds of millions of edges — a live per-request Dijkstra traversal would take seconds to minutes, not the sub-second response users expect). Production routers instead use contraction hierarchies: an expensive offline preprocessing pass that ranks nodes by "importance" and inserts shortcut edges bypassing less-important nodes (if the shortest path between two important junctions always passes through the same sequence of minor roads, a single shortcut edge can represent that whole sequence) — then an online query only needs to search a small subgraph of shortcuts between important nodes, plus short local searches at the start and end near the actual origin/destination, turning what would be a slow full-graph search into a fast, mostly-precomputed lookup. The trade-off to state plainly: a large, periodically-refreshed offline computation in exchange for near-instant online queries — the same "pay the cost ahead of time, not per-request" shape as Autocomplete's offline top-K precomputation (an earlier case study), applied to graph search instead of ranking.

Live traffic, worked through: traffic conditions are folded in as dynamically updated edge weights layered on top of the precomputed contraction-hierarchy structure, not by re-running the entire offline preprocessing pass in real time (which would defeat the purpose) — a segment with heavy current traffic simply gets a temporarily higher weight, and the online query naturally routes around it using the existing shortcut structure. Worth naming where the traffic data itself comes from: crowd-sourced from the location pings of phones actively running the app/maps client — aggregated speed on a road segment, inferred from how fast many anonymous devices are currently moving along it, the same kind of high-volume, loss-tolerant location-ping pipeline as the Uber case study's driver tracking, repurposed as a sensor network.

Map tile serving, worth naming as a separate, much simpler concern: the visual map imagery itself is served as pre-rendered tiles per zoom level — static, highly cacheable content, ideal for CDN delivery (Section 2.4) exactly like any other static asset — worth distinguishing explicitly from the routing/search problems above, since it's a completely different (and far simpler) piece of the overall system that's easy to accidentally conflate with the harder graph and indexing problems.

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "How accurate is the ETA, and how would you improve it?" — raw graph distance/speed-limit-based ETA is a starting point; production systems layer a learned model on top, trained on historical actual-travel-time data for similar segments/times/conditions, correcting for systematic biases (speed limits are a ceiling, not an average) — worth naming that this is now a Modern-AI-Systems-adjacent problem (Section 2.15), not purely a graph-algorithms one.
  • "Re-route live when a driver deviates from the suggested path." — the client periodically checks its actual position against the active route; a deviation beyond a threshold triggers a fresh routing query from the driver's new position — not a fundamentally different algorithm, just the same online query re-triggered, worth stating plainly rather than implying some more exotic re-routing-specific mechanism exists.

Group F — Commerce & Finance

Design a Payment System

Why this problem, specifically: more than any other case study in this guide, the bar here isn't cleverness — it's paranoia, applied systematically. Every design decision below exists because someone, somewhere, lost real money to the failure mode it prevents; the interview signal is whether you reach for these safeguards because you understand why money-moving systems are different, not because you memorized a checklist.

Clarifying questions: direct merchant payments, or a marketplace where money must ultimately reach multiple sellers (changes the ledger structure significantly, below)? Which payment methods — card, bank transfer, wallet? Are refunds and disputes/chargebacks in scope?

Functional requirements: charge a customer; move funds to the appropriate destination account(s); support refunds; maintain a complete, auditable history of every money movement.

Non-functional requirements: zero tolerance for double-charging or lost charges — this single requirement shapes almost every decision below; full auditability (every balance must be reconstructable and explainable from history); regulatory compliance (PCI-DSS at minimum).

Idempotency keys, worked through with an actual failure scenario — the most-tested mechanic in this whole case study: a client calls charge($100); the network times out before the client receives a response, but the charge may well have already succeeded on the processor's side — the client genuinely cannot tell. The client retries the identical request, this time including the same idempotency key (a UUID it generated once, client-side, for this one logical charge attempt). The Payment Service checks the idempotency store first: if this key has been seen before, it returns the stored result of the original attempt without processing anything a second time — the retry is safe by construction, regardless of how many times the client repeats it. This is worth stating precisely: idempotency doesn't prevent the network failure, it makes retrying after one safe, which is the actual achievable goal (Section 2.7's "effectively-once" framing, applied to the highest-stakes case in this guide).

The double-entry ledger, worked through with an actual worked example — the mechanism that makes the system self-auditing: never UPDATE balance. Instead, every transaction writes a matched pair of entries — a debit and a credit of equal magnitude, to two different accounts. A $100 customer charge, moving money into the platform pending payout to a seller, might write:

Entry 1: DEBIT  customer_receivable   $100
Entry 2: CREDIT platform_revenue      $100

A "balance" is never stored directly — it's the sum of all entries for a given account, computed (or maintained as a cached, derived value) from the immutable entry log. This is event sourcing (Section 2.11) applied to money specifically, and it buys two things worth naming explicitly: self-auditing correctness (the sum of every debit across the entire ledger must always equal the sum of every credit — a single global invariant that, if it ever breaks, immediately proves a bug exists somewhere, rather than silently drifting) and a complete, replayable history (every historical balance at any point in time is reconstructable by replaying entries up to that moment, valuable for both debugging and regulatory audit).

The payment state machine, worked through with failure handling at each step: created → authorized → captured → settled (or failed/refunded off any state). Each transition is its own durable, atomic write — and critically, the transition into "confirmed" success is never assumed from a request having been sent, only from a confirmed response or an async webhook (below) actually arriving.

The "unknown state" problem, worked through — arguably the single most important idea in this case study: when a call to the external processor times out, the system does not know whether the charge succeeded, failed, or is still processing on the processor's side. The one universally wrong move is to blindly retry the charge as if it definitely failed — that's exactly how double-charges happen. The correct handling: mark the payment pending/unknown, and either (a) poll the processor's status endpoint using the original request's idempotency key (most processors expose exactly this, precisely to solve this problem) until a definitive answer arrives, or (b) wait for the processor's own async webhook confirming the outcome — never re-issue a fresh charge attempt without the same idempotency key protecting it.

Async confirmation via webhook, worked through: many processors confirm success asynchronously (Section 2.2) rather than in the original response — meaning the system must model "payment initiated" and "payment confirmed" as genuinely separate, durable states, not assume completion the instant the initiating request returns. Webhook handling itself needs the same idempotency discipline in reverse (Section 2.19's Inbox pattern) — a processor may redeliver the same webhook multiple times, and the handler must recognize and skip an already-processed one rather than recording the same settlement twice.

Reconciliation, worked through as the system's ultimate safety net: a nightly batch job (Section 2.17) compares the internal ledger against the processor's own settlement statements, line by line — catching anything that slipped through (a webhook that never arrived, a race the idempotency layer somehow missed) and routing discrepancies to a manual-review queue. This is worth naming as a deliberate, designed-in second line of defense, not a sign the primary mechanisms are untrusted — even a system engineered as carefully as this one still reconciles against ground truth, because money is the one domain where "we're pretty sure it's correct" isn't an acceptable final answer.

Security, applied concretely here (Section 2.14's general mechanism): card numbers are tokenized at the earliest possible point (often client-side, via the processor's own SDK, so the raw number never even reaches this system's servers) — the system only ever handles an opaque token, which is what narrows the PCI-DSS compliance scope down to a manageable surface rather than the entire application.

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "Marketplace payments — money needs to reach multiple sellers, minus a platform fee, not just one merchant." — the double-entry model generalizes directly: one incoming charge writes debit/credit pairs splitting the amount across a platform-fee account and each seller's payable account — the ledger structure doesn't change, only the number of entries per transaction.
  • "Handle a chargeback/dispute." — a chargeback is itself just another ledger transaction (a reversing debit/credit pair against the original), triggered by an external event from the card network days or weeks after the original charge — worth naming that this is why the ledger is append-only and never mutates history: a dispute doesn't erase the original charge, it adds a new, linked, offsetting entry.
  • "Multi-currency support?" — each ledger entry carries its own currency and an exchange rate captured at transaction time (never recomputed later, since rates move) — cross-currency reporting sums are computed at read time by converting at each entry's own recorded rate, not by forcing a single global currency into the ledger itself.

Design a Stock Exchange

Why this problem, specifically: it's the one case study in this entire guide where the answer looks almost nothing like a typical web backend — no horizontal scaling of the core logic, no eventual consistency, no distributed locking — and interviewers use it specifically to see whether you can reason about an extreme, microsecond-latency, single-threaded-by-design system instead of reflexively reaching for the usual toolbox.

Clarifying questions: simple equities matching, or also options/derivatives (assume equities — options add contract-complexity, not architectural complexity)? Is the focus the exchange's matching core, or the surrounding client-facing trading platform (assume the matching core — it's the distinctive, hard part)?

Functional requirements: accept buy/sell orders (market, limit, and stop-loss at minimum); match compatible orders and execute trades; publish real-time market data (the current best bid/ask, recent trades); produce a complete, replayable, auditable trade history.

Non-functional requirements: matching latency in microseconds, not milliseconds — genuinely the tightest latency budget in this entire guide; zero tolerance for lost or duplicated orders; deterministic, exactly-reproducible behavior (a regulator must be able to replay the day's events and get identical results).

The order book, worked through with an actual matching example: each tradable symbol has its own order book — two sorted structures, bids (buy orders, sorted descending by price — the highest bidder is first) and asks (sell orders, sorted ascending by price — the lowest seller is first). A new order matches under price-time priority: it's compared against the best opposing price, and if compatible (a buy at or above the best ask, or a sell at or below the best bid), a trade executes immediately at the resting order's price; if multiple resting orders sit at the same price, the one that arrived first executes first (pure FIFO within a price level). Concretely: the book shows asks starting at $50.02 (100 shares), $50.05 (200 shares); a market buy for 150 shares consumes the entire $50.02 level (100 shares) and 50 shares from the $50.05 level, executing as two separate trades at two different prices — worth walking through an example like this explicitly, since "price-time priority" as an abstract phrase undersells how mechanical and precise the actual matching logic is.

Order types, worked through — each hits the book differently:

Type Behavior
Market Execute immediately against the best available opposing price(s), regardless of price, until fully filled or the book is exhausted
Limit Execute only at the specified price or better; if it can't fully fill immediately, the remainder rests in the book, becoming part of the bid/ask structure future orders match against
Stop-loss Dormant until the market price crosses a trigger threshold, at which point it converts into a market (or limit) order and enters the book normally
Fill-or-Kill (FOK) Must execute completely, immediately, or not at all — no partial fill, no resting

Why single-threaded, in-memory, per symbol — the decision that matters most, and the actual engineering reasoning behind it: real exchanges (and the widely-cited LMAX Disruptor pattern) run each symbol's matching logic on one dedicated thread, against an in-memory order book, deliberately avoiding locks on the hot matching path entirely. The reasoning, stated precisely: locks introduce both latency (contention) and non-determinism (the exact interleaving of concurrent access varies run to run) — and non-determinism is disqualifying for a system that must be exactly replayable for audit and recovery purposes. A single thread per symbol sidesteps both problems at once: there's no concurrent access to the book to coordinate at all, so there's nothing to lock, and the sequence of operations is trivially deterministic (whatever order they were processed in, which is the same order every time you replay the log).

Durability without sacrificing speed — the event-sourcing mechanism that makes this work: every incoming order is first written to a durable, sequenced write-ahead log before the matching engine touches it — and critically, this write is a cheap, purely sequential append (Section 2.10's LSM-tree-style write pattern), not a random-access database write, so it doesn't meaningfully compete with the matching thread's speed requirement. If the matching engine crashes, recovery means deterministically replaying the log from the last snapshot — since matching is a pure, deterministic function of (current book state, next order), replaying the identical sequence of orders against the identical starting state always reconstructs identical book state, with zero ambiguity about what the "correct" recovered state should be.

Market data dissemination, worked through: every book change (a new best bid/ask, a trade execution) is published to subscribers via a multicast/fan-out feed, structurally the pub/sub pattern from Section 2.8, but at a latency budget tight enough that real exchanges often use raw multicast networking rather than a general-purpose message broker — worth naming as an example of a problem so latency-sensitive it justifies stepping outside this guide's usual default tools.

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "What's a 'circuit breaker' in exchange terminology — same as Section 2.1's software pattern?" — explicitly not the same concept, and worth distinguishing precisely if asked: a trading circuit breaker is a regulatory/market mechanism that halts trading entirely on a symbol (or the whole exchange) when price moves too far too fast, to let panic subside and prevent a cascading crash — a business-rule safety mechanism, not a software resilience pattern, even though both borrow the same "trip and stop" metaphor.
  • "Prevent a trader from matching against their own resting order (self-trade)." — the matching engine checks the counterparty account on every candidate match and skips (or cancels) a match against the same account/trader, a rule enforced directly inside the core matching loop since it must happen before a trade is considered final, not as an after-the-fact check.
  • "Market maker vs. taker — does it affect matching?" — it doesn't change matching logic at all (price-time priority is identical for everyone) — it's a fee-structure distinction layered on top (liquidity providers who rest orders are often paid a rebate; takers who consume resting liquidity pay a fee) — worth naming that not every exchange concept changes the engineering, some are purely economic/business-layer decisions riding on top of an unchanged core.

Design an E-commerce Platform (Amazon-style)

Why this problem, specifically: it's the broadest, most "put it all together" case study in this guide — a strong answer explicitly names which earlier building blocks it's reusing (inventory correctness from Ticket Booking, saga orchestration from Section 2.1, CQRS from Section 2.11) rather than re-deriving each from scratch, which is itself the signal being tested at this point in the guide.

Clarifying questions: single-seller or marketplace (many third-party sellers, changing the payment-splitting story to match the Payment System case study's marketplace extension)? Is search/catalog browsing in scope, or just the checkout flow (assume both)?

Service decomposition, worked through by bounded context, not arbitrarily: catalog, cart, order, payment, inventory, notification — each an independently scalable service (Section 2.1), specifically because their load profiles are wildly different: catalog browsing traffic vastly outweighs checkout traffic (browsing is read-heavy and forgiving of slight staleness; checkout is comparatively low-volume but must be exactly correct) — splitting them means the catalog service can be scaled and cached aggressively without that decision having any bearing on how the payment service is built.

Inventory correctness under concurrent checkouts, worked through as the sharp edge of this entire case study: two customers racing to buy the last unit of a low-stock item is the exact check-then-act race this guide has solved repeatedly — an atomic conditional decrement (UPDATE inventory SET stock = stock - 1 WHERE product_id = ? AND stock > 0, checking affected-row count) is the same primitive as the movie-ticket seat lock and the ticket-booking hold, applied to a quantity instead of a boolean availability flag. Reserving inventory at "add to cart" time vs. only at "place order" time is a real product trade-off worth naming: reserving early (with a short TTL, the seat-hold pattern again) gives customers confidence their cart won't be emptied from under them, at the cost of holding stock hostage from other buyers during abandoned-cart windows; reserving only at checkout maximizes fairness/throughput at the cost of a customer occasionally losing a race at the final step.

The checkout flow as an explicit saga, worked through step by step — this is where the interesting engineering actually lives: checkout touches inventory, payment, and order-confirmation across (in a microservices decomposition) three separate services and three separate local transactions — precisely the shape the Saga pattern (Section 2.1) exists for:

  1. Reserve inventory (the conditional decrement above) — compensating action if a later step fails: release the reservation back to available stock.
  2. Charge payment (delegating to the Payment System case study's idempotency-key discipline in full) — compensating action if a later step fails: refund.
  3. Confirm the order and emit a confirmation event (via the Outbox pattern, Section 2.1, so the event is guaranteed to fire if and only if the order was actually durably confirmed) — triggering downstream notification and fulfillment.

If step 2 (payment) fails, step 1's compensating action (release inventory) runs automatically; if step 3 somehow fails after a successful charge, the compensating action is a refund — walking through this failure-and-compensation chain explicitly, not just naming "saga" as a buzzword, is what turns this into a strong answer.

Cart, worked through as its own small data-modeling decision: a shopping cart is typically not stored in the same durable, transactional database as confirmed orders — it lives in a fast key-value store (Section 2.15's Distributed Cache case study, or a lightweight session store), since a cart is mutated frequently, tolerates being lost far more gracefully than a placed order, and doesn't need ACID guarantees the way inventory decrements and payment records do.

CQRS, applied concretely here (Section 2.11's general pattern): the read-heavy catalog/browse path (denormalized, search-index-backed, Section 2.5, optimized purely for fast retrieval) and the write-heavy order/payment path (normalized, transactional, optimized for correctness) genuinely have almost nothing in common — forcing them through one shared model would compromise both, which is exactly the justification CQRS's general definition gives, made concrete with a real example.

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "A flash sale — thousands of customers hitting 'buy' on one item within seconds." — this is the Ticket-Booking case study's virtual-waiting-room pattern, reused directly: rather than letting an instant flood hit the inventory-decrement layer directly, admit purchase attempts at a controlled rate through a queueing gate — worth naming the reuse explicitly rather than inventing a new mechanism.
  • "A product's price changes while it's sitting in a customer's cart." — snapshot the price at add-to-cart time (or explicitly re-validate and surface the change at checkout) rather than silently charging whatever the current catalog price happens to be at purchase time — a small but real product-correctness decision worth naming unprompted.
  • "Order tracking / shipment status updates?" — an event-driven pattern (Section 2.1): the fulfillment/shipping subsystem publishes state-change events (shipped, out_for_delivery, delivered) that the order service and notification service both independently consume — not a synchronous call chain, so a slow shipping-carrier integration can never block the rest of the order pipeline.

Design a Ticket-Booking System (BookMyShow/Ticketmaster-style)

Why this problem, specifically: it's explicitly the HLD-scale generalization of the LLD Movie-Ticket case study (Part I) — the seat-locking correctness mechanism carries over unchanged, so the entire interview should be spent on what's genuinely new at this scale: an extreme, short-duration traffic spike that the LLD version never had to consider.

Clarifying questions: is this a single high-demand on-sale (a popular concert) or steady-state booking across many ordinary events (assume both need to work, but design specifically for the extreme case, since it's strictly harder)? Are dynamic/surge pricing and resale/exchanges in scope?

What's reused wholesale from the LLD Movie-Ticket case study (Part I): the short-TTL seat hold (Redis, typically a few minutes, held from "seat selected" through payment confirmation) backed by a database-level unique constraint on (event_id, seat_id) as the final correctness guarantee — this mechanism doesn't change moving from one theater to a global ticketing platform; restating it here would waste interview time better spent on the traffic-spike problem below.

The virtual waiting room, worked through mechanically — this is the entire new problem: for a high-demand on-sale, letting every interested user hit the seat-holding layer simultaneously the instant tickets go live would thrash it under a load spike orders of magnitude above steady-state. Instead: users arriving before or during the rush join a fair queue — concretely, a structure ordered by arrival time (a Kafka partition consumed in arrival order, or a Redis sorted set scored by join timestamp) — and are admitted into the actual booking flow at a controlled rate the backend can sustain, each receiving a short-lived admission token granting temporary access to the real seat-selection/hold flow. Users not yet admitted see a "you're in line, position #4,281" holding page, polling for their turn rather than hammering the booking API directly.

Why this specifically protects the seat-locking layer, stated precisely: the seat-hold mechanism (an atomic conditional update per seat) is correct under any concurrency level — it will never double-book a seat no matter how many simultaneous requests arrive. The waiting room isn't fixing a correctness problem, it's fixing a capacity problem: without it, a flood of simultaneous conditional-update attempts (the overwhelming majority of which are doomed to fail, since there are far more interested buyers than seats) can still saturate the database/cache layer's throughput just from the sheer volume of attempts, degrading the experience even for the users who would have succeeded. Worth stating this distinction explicitly — a candidate who conflates "prevents double-booking" with "prevents overload" hasn't fully understood why both mechanisms are independently necessary.

Inventory sharding by event, worked through: each event's seat map is entirely independent of every other event's, which makes event_id a natural, clean shard key (Section 2.6) — a hot on-sale for one concert saturating its shard has no effect on a different, ordinary event's booking traffic being served from a different shard, isolating the blast radius of exactly the extreme-load scenario this whole case study is about.

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "Anti-bot / scalper protection?" — CAPTCHA at waiting-room entry, per-account purchase-quantity limits enforced at the same layer as the seat hold, and queue-position integrity checks (detecting many queue-joins from the same device/IP faster than human interaction plausibly allows) — layered checks, not one silver-bullet mechanism.
  • "Dynamic pricing for extremely high-demand events?" — a pricing-strategy lookup (the same Strategy-shaped decision as the LLD case study's fee calculation, Part I) evaluated against real-time demand signals — structurally identical to Uber's surge-pricing mechanism (Group E), just triggered by ticket-queue depth instead of a geospatial supply/demand ratio.
  • "Support partial refunds or seat exchanges after purchase?" — modeled as a new saga (Section 2.1): release the original seat hold's now-permanent booking record into a compensating "cancelled" state, refund via the Payment System case study's mechanics, and — for an exchange — atomically attempt a new hold on the desired seat before finalizing the cancellation of the old one, so a customer never ends up holding neither seat if the exchange target turns out to be unavailable.

Design an Object Store (AWS S3-style)

Why this problem, specifically: it's deceptively described as "just a key-value store for big files," and the interview signal is whether you recognize that the metadata/index layer, not the blob storage itself, is the harder engineering problem at trillions-of-objects scale.

Clarifying questions: is strong or eventual read-after-write consistency required (historically a real, evolving design decision — S3 itself moved from eventual to strong consistency for this exact reason)? Multi-tenant (many customers' buckets on shared infrastructure) — does that change anything about isolation?

Functional requirements: PUT/GET/DELETE an object by key; list objects by key prefix; support very large objects (multi-GB) via multipart upload.

Non-functional requirements: extremely high durability (the "eleven nines" figure below) and effectively unlimited horizontal scalability, both far exceeding what a normal database is built for.

Architecture:

Not a filesystem — worked through precisely: conceptually a flat key-value store; there's no real directory hierarchy, just keys that happen to contain slashes that a UI renders as if they were folders. This matters because it explains why "list objects under this prefix" is a genuinely different (and more expensive) operation than a filesystem's directory listing — it's a range scan over a sorted keyspace, not a pointer dereference into a directory structure.

Erasure coding, worked through with the actual math: rather than storing 3–4 full copies of every object (200–300% storage overhead), an object is split into data shards plus parity shards — a common scheme is 5 data shards + 4 parity shards, from which the original can be reconstructed from any 5 of the 9 total shards — surviving the loss of any 4 shards entirely, at only 80% storage overhead (9 shards to represent 5 shards' worth of data) instead of 200%+. Shards are placed across independent fault domains (separate Availability Zones/data centers), so a whole-datacenter event still can't destroy enough shards to lose the object.

The write path, worked through — durability is enforced before acknowledgment, not after: a PUT is not acknowledged to the client until the erasure-coded shards are confirmed durable across their fault domains — durability is a property of what "success" means on the write path itself, not a background replication step that happens to catch up eventually.

The keymap/index — the actually-hard part, worth stating explicitly: a separate, strongly-consistent layer tracks which physical shards, on which nodes, back which key — at a scale of trillions of keys, keeping this layer correct, available, and fast is arguably harder than the blob-storage mechanism itself, since every single read and write depends on it being right.

The number worth knowing, and why it's not a marketing figure: S3's publicly stated target is "eleven nines" of durability (99.999999999%) — framed as: store 10 million objects, expect to lose one roughly once every 10,000 years. This number is a direct, calculable consequence of the erasure-coding scheme and fault-domain placement above (the probability that enough independent shards fail simultaneously to lose reconstructability), not an arbitrary promise.

Contrast worth naming — object storage vs. a distributed file system: a distributed file system (Google's GFS, and its open-source descendant HDFS) instead preserves real directory/file semantics, optimized for large sequential reads by batch jobs rather than random small-object access: a single name node holds all metadata in memory for speed, while many data nodes store fixed-size blocks (64–128MB), replicated (commonly 3×) with rack-aware placement so one rack failure can't take out every copy of a block. The name node's centrality is the classic weak point — GFS/HDFS deployments invest heavily in name-node HA specifically because everything else depends on it.

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "Support object versioning (keep every past version of an overwritten object)." — the keymap stores multiple shard-location entries per key, ordered by version/timestamp, rather than overwriting the current entry — structurally event-sourcing again (Section 2.11), applied to blob metadata.
  • "Lifecycle policies — auto-delete or auto-tier objects after N days." — a background batch job (Section 2.17) scans the keymap for objects matching a bucket's policy and either deletes them or moves their shards to a cheaper, higher-latency storage tier, updating the keymap's location pointer accordingly — the object's key/identity never changes, only where its shards physically live.
  • "Multi-tenant isolation — one customer's traffic spike shouldn't degrade another's." — per-bucket/per-customer rate limiting at the API layer (Section 2.4), and physically spreading different tenants' shards across the underlying fleet so no single tenant's data density creates a hot node others depend on.

Design a Distributed Message Queue (build "Kafka")

Why this problem, specifically: interviewers ask this to see whether you understand why each of Kafka's specific mechanisms exists — not just that "partitions," "replication," and "consumer groups" are the right vocabulary, but the actual problem each one independently solves.

Clarifying questions: ordering guarantees needed — global, or just per-key (assume per-key/per-partition, the realistic answer)? At-least-once or exactly-once processing semantics? Expected retention — hours, or a durable long-term log?

Functional requirements: producers publish messages to named topics; multiple independent consumers can read the same topic; messages persist for a configurable retention period, replayable from any earlier point.

Architecture, building it up piece by piece — this is the entire interview:

  • Partitions — for parallelism, and the reason ordering is scoped the way it is: a topic is split into partitions so many brokers can share the write/read load; a message is assigned to a partition by hashing its key (so all messages for the same key — e.g., the same user, or the same order — always land in the same partition). Ordering is only guaranteed within a partition, never across an entire topic — worth stating precisely, since "Kafka guarantees ordering" is a common but imprecise claim: it guarantees ordering only for messages that share a partition.
  • Replication and the ISR set, worked through: each partition has a configurable number of replicas across brokers — one leader (handles all reads/writes for that partition) and the rest followers. The set of replicas fully caught up with the leader is the In-Sync Replica (ISR) set; a message is only considered "committed" (acknowledged to the producer) once every member of the ISR has it — this is what lets Kafka survive a broker failure without losing acknowledged data, since any remaining ISR member is guaranteed to have every committed message.
  • Consumer groups, worked through — why this is the parallelism mechanism on the read side: a consumer group splits a topic's partitions among its members, so each partition is read by exactly one consumer in the group at a time — this is how Kafka parallelizes consumption while still preserving per-partition order (only one consumer touches a given partition, so there's no cross-consumer race to worry about within it). Maximum useful parallelism for one consumer group is therefore bounded by the partition count — adding an 11th consumer to a group reading a 10-partition topic leaves that 11th consumer permanently idle, a concrete number worth stating rather than leaving abstract.
  • Consumer group rebalancing, worked through: when a consumer joins or leaves a group (deploy, crash), partitions are reassigned among the remaining/new members — a brief pause in consumption for the affected partitions while reassignment completes, which is why rebalancing frequency/speed is itself a real operational concern at scale, not just a background implementation detail.
  • Offsets, worked through: each consumer group tracks its own position (offset) in each partition it reads, stored durably (in a special internal Kafka topic, in Kafka's own real implementation) — this is precisely what lets a consumer crash and restart, resuming exactly where it left off rather than reprocessing the entire topic or losing its place.

Exactly-once semantics, worked through — worth being precise about what this actually means: true end-to-end exactly-once is a genuinely hard guarantee; Kafka's own idempotent producer (each message tagged with a producer ID + sequence number, so the broker can detect and drop an exact duplicate from a producer retry) combined with transactions (atomically writing to multiple partitions, or writing plus committing a consumer offset, as one all-or-nothing unit) gets close to exactly-once within the Kafka ecosystem — but the moment a message triggers a side effect outside Kafka (an email send, an external API call), you're back to Section 2.7's honest answer: at-least-once delivery plus an idempotent consumer.

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "A broker holding a partition's leader dies — what happens?" — the controller (itself elected via a consensus mechanism, Section 2.7) promotes one of the ISR followers to be the new leader; since ISR members are guaranteed caught-up, no committed message is lost, though in-flight, not-yet-acknowledged writes at the moment of failure may need the producer to retry.
  • "Log compaction — keep only the latest value per key, not every historical message?" — a background process that rewrites a partition's log, discarding all but the most recent message for each key, turning the topic into more of a durable, replayable key-value snapshot than a pure event stream — useful for topics representing current state (like a changelog feed) rather than a sequence of discrete events.
  • "Store years of history cheaply without keeping it all on expensive local disks?" — tiered storage (a newer real Kafka capability): older log segments are moved to cheap object storage (Section 2.16) while brokers keep only recent segments on fast local disk, extending retention dramatically without a proportional cost increase.

Design a Serverless Function Platform (AWS Lambda-style)

Why this problem, specifically: it's the case study most directly about isolation and startup-latency engineering — the interesting content is entirely in how the platform safely and quickly gets untrusted, arbitrary customer code running, not in any request-handling logic.

Clarifying questions: are long-running functions (minutes, not seconds) in scope, or strictly short-lived? Is stateful workflow orchestration (chaining multiple functions with conditional logic) in scope?

Functional requirements: run arbitrary user code in response to triggers (HTTP request, queue message, schedule); scale invocation concurrency up and down automatically, including to zero when idle.

Architecture:

Execution isolation, worked through: each invocation runs inside a strongly isolated environment — historically a full VM, now more commonly a lightweight microVM (AWS's own Firecracker is the concrete, publicly-documented example) that boots in milliseconds rather than the seconds-to-minutes a traditional VM takes, while still providing hardware-level isolation between different customers' code running on shared physical hosts — a stronger isolation boundary than a plain container, which matters enormously given this platform runs arbitrary, mutually-untrusted code from many different customers on the same fleet.

Cold starts, worked through as the central engineering trade-off: the first invocation after a period of no traffic (or the first invocation ever, for a newly-deployed function) requires provisioning a fresh execution environment and loading the function's code into it — this "cold start" adds real, user-visible latency compared to reusing an already-warm environment. Mitigations, worth naming as trade-offs, not a free lunch: keeping a small pool of pre-initialized warm environments ready for likely-to-be-invoked functions (at the cost of paying for idle capacity); provisioned concurrency (a paid tier guaranteeing a fixed number of always-warm environments for latency-sensitive functions, trading serverless's cost-elasticity for predictable latency); and language/runtime choice mattering a great deal (a runtime with a heavy startup cost, like loading a large framework, pays a bigger cold-start penalty than a lightweight one).

Scaling to zero and to burst, worked through: with no warm environments and no traffic, the platform runs (and bills) nothing at all — the defining economic property of serverless. A sudden burst of concurrent invocations triggers rapid parallel provisioning of many fresh environments simultaneously (bounded by a per-account concurrency limit, both to protect the platform's own capacity and to cap a runaway customer's blast radius) — this burst-provisioning behavior is precisely where cold starts are most visible in aggregate, since a burst after idle time means many simultaneous cold starts, not just one.

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "Support long-running tasks (minutes, not seconds)?" — most serverless platforms impose a hard execution-time cap specifically because the model is optimized for short, bursty, stateless work — a genuinely long-running task is a signal to use a different compute model (a container/VM-based worker) rather than fighting the platform's core assumptions.
  • "Chain multiple functions into a stateful workflow with conditional branching?" — a separate orchestration layer (AWS Step Functions-style) that tracks workflow state externally (Section 2.11 — this is CQRS/event-sourcing again, at the workflow level) rather than trying to hold state inside the inherently stateless, ephemeral functions themselves.
  • "A function fails — retry behavior?" — configurable retry policy plus a dead-letter queue (Section 2.8) for invocations that exhaust their retries, so a poison-pill event doesn't retry forever and silently vanishes instead into a queue an operator can inspect.

Design a Distributed Scheduler (cron at scale)

Why this problem, specifically: "just run this job at this time" sounds trivial until pushed to millions of jobs across many machines, at which point the honest, hard-won admission that exactly-once triggering is impossible becomes the entire point of the question.

Clarifying questions: do jobs have dependencies on each other (a DAG, not independent jobs)? Is catch-up/backfill needed if the scheduler itself was down when a job should have fired?

Functional requirements: fire each scheduled job at its configured time; survive individual dispatcher/worker failures without ever double-firing or silently dropping a job; support both one-off and recurring schedules.

Architecture:

The dispatch mechanism, worked through precisely: a job store (a table indexed on next_run_at) is scanned by multiple dispatcher instances, each responsible for a shard of jobs (hashed by job ID, Section 2.6's consistent hashing — instance churn only reshuffles a fraction of jobs, not all of them). At trigger time, a dispatcher doesn't execute the job directly — it inserts an execution record with a unique constraint on (job_id, scheduled_time), letting a database constraint violation be the actual mechanism that prevents a double-fire if two dispatchers somehow raced on the same job (the same atomic-conditional-write discipline as every concurrency-correctness problem in this guide, applied here to "has this job already been triggered" instead of "is this seat/spot/inventory-unit taken").

Worker claiming, worked through: workers claim pending execution records via an atomic, leased UPDATE ... WHERE status='pending' AND lease_expires < now(), execute the job, and mark the record done; a watchdog process re-queues any lease that expires without completing — the worker that held it presumably crashed mid-execution.

The decision that matters, stated as plainly as possible: exactly-once execution is not actually achievable, and saying so directly is a stronger answer than pretending otherwise. The honest, achievable contract is at-most-once triggering (via the unique-constraint mechanism above, which genuinely prevents a double dispatch) combined with an expectation that jobs themselves are idempotent — so the rare case of a lease timing out on a job that actually did complete (just slowly) results in a harmless redundant execution, not a catastrophic one.

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "Jobs with dependencies — job B should only run after job A succeeds." — this becomes a DAG-based orchestration problem (Airflow/Dagster-style, Section 2.17) layered on top of the same triggering mechanism — a job's next_run_at eligibility is gated on its dependencies' completion status, not purely on wall-clock time.
  • "The scheduler was down for an hour — do missed jobs run when it recovers?" — a policy decision, not purely technical: catch-up/backfill re-runs every missed occurrence (correct for "send a daily report," wrong for "check inventory every 5 minutes," where running 12 stale checks back-to-back is pointless) versus skip-to-latest (only the most recent missed occurrence runs) — worth naming that the right default genuinely depends on the job's semantics, and asking rather than assuming.

Design a Metrics & Monitoring Platform (Datadog-style)

Why this problem, specifically: the interesting engineering isn't ingestion or storage in isolation — it's designing a system whose own cardinality (not raw volume) is the thing most likely to make it fall over, which is a genuinely different failure mode than most systems in this guide worry about.

Clarifying questions: are distributed traces in scope alongside metrics, or metrics/alerting only (assume metrics/alerting as the core)? What's the acceptable alert-evaluation latency — seconds, or is a minute of lag tolerable?

Functional requirements: ingest metrics from every host/service continuously; power live dashboards; evaluate alerting rules against recent data and notify on breach; retain history for long-term trend analysis.

Why Kafka sits in front of everything, worked through — deliberate, not incidental: it absorbs traffic spikes so a downstream slowdown (a struggling storage backend, a buggy consumer) doesn't translate into metric loss — and its replay capability means a fixed consumer can be re-run over recently-ingested data, recovering from a bug rather than having silently and permanently dropped data during the time it was broken.

Two storage paths with genuinely different jobs, worked through: a hot path (recent hours-to-days, high resolution, in memory/SSD) serves live dashboards and alert evaluation, where staleness of even a few seconds is unacceptable. A cold path progressively downsamples (raw → 1-minute → 1-hour → 1-day rollups) into cheaper long-term storage for trend queries that have no need for per-second precision — querying "CPU usage over the last year" against raw per-second data would be both absurdly expensive and pointless, since no dashboard renders that much resolution meaningfully anyway.

Cardinality control — the decision that matters most, worked through with a concrete failure: a metric like request_duration{status=200} has a handful of possible label combinations and is cheap to store. A metric like request_duration{user_id=...} explodes into one entire time series per distinct user — at real scale, this single modeling mistake can be the difference between a monitoring system that works and one that falls over under its own metadata, since the underlying time-series storage has to track and index every distinct label combination separately. The discipline: keep high-cardinality fields (user IDs, request IDs, session tokens) out of metric labels entirely, and route that kind of fine-grained, per-entity detail to logs or traces instead, which are architecturally built for high-cardinality lookup in a way metrics storage is not.

Alert evaluation, worked through mechanically: alerting rules are continuously evaluated as streaming windowed queries (Section 2.17) directly against the hot path — "has error rate exceeded 5% over the trailing 5 minutes" is a sliding-window aggregation, not a query re-run from scratch on a timer. Alert deduplication/grouping (Alertmanager-style) collapses many simultaneously-firing rules that share a root cause into one notification, rather than paging an on-call engineer with fifty separate alerts for one underlying outage.

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "Correlate a metric spike with the specific requests that caused it." — this is precisely why distributed tracing exists as a separate pillar (Section 2.13) alongside metrics — a trace ID threading through every service a request touched lets you jump from "error rate spiked at 2:14pm" to the actual slow/failing requests, which aggregate metrics alone can never show you.
  • "Alert on 'burn rate' against an SLO instead of a raw threshold?" — a burn-rate alert (Section 2.13's error-budget framing) fires based on how fast the error budget is being consumed relative to the remaining time in the period, catching both sudden severe issues and slow sustained degradation that a fixed threshold alone might miss or fire on too late/too often.

Design a Logging Infrastructure

Why this problem, specifically: structurally the sibling of the metrics pipeline above, but the interview signal here is different — knowing when Elasticsearch's default "index everything" instinct stops being the right answer, and what the actual alternative looks like.

Clarifying questions: compliance/audit retention requirements (a fixed number of years, driving the cold-storage design below)? Is full-text ad hoc search a hard requirement, or mostly label-based filtering (changes the Elasticsearch-vs-Loki answer significantly)?

Architecture:

flowchart TD
    Apps["Services"] --> Agent["Log agents
(Fluentd/Filebeat)"]
    Agent -->|scrub PII| K[["Kafka"]]
    K --> Search["Searchable store
(Elasticsearch or Loki)"]

The decision that matters, worked through precisely — this is the entire interview: Elasticsearch's default instinct is to fully index every field of every log line, which stops scaling at high enough volume — the index itself can become larger and more expensive to maintain than the raw data it's indexing. Loki's alternative: index only a small, deliberately chosen set of labels (service, environment, host) and store the actual log lines as compressed, unindexed chunks — a query first narrows down to the relevant chunks via the cheap label index, then greps through only that narrowed set for the full-text portion. This trades slower ad hoc full-text search (you can't instantly search any arbitrary field across the entire corpus the way Elasticsearch promises) for a dramatically cheaper, more horizontally scalable system — worth naming as the direct, considered answer to "Elasticsearch is falling over at our volume, what would you do differently," rather than a vague "use a different tool."

PII scrubbing and retention, worked through as non-negotiable requirements, not nice-to-haves: scrubbing happens in the ingestion pipeline before logs are persisted anywhere searchable — never "we'll redact it later," since anything already written to a searchable, widely-accessed store has already leaked by the time a later redaction pass runs. Retention is explicitly tiered: hot/searchable for a short operational window (days), cold/compliance-only (compressed object storage, Section 2.16) for years — mirroring the same hot/cold tiering instinct as the Metrics platform and the Email Service case study's mailbox storage.

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "Structured (JSON) vs. unstructured (plain text) logs — does it matter?" — structured logs are strongly preferred at scale specifically because label-based indexing (Loki's whole approach above) needs consistent, parseable fields to filter on — a plain-text log line with an inconsistent format defeats cheap label indexing and forces expensive full-text search even for basic filtering.
  • "Log volume is overwhelming storage even with tiering — what else?" — sampling: for very high-volume, low-value debug logs specifically, keep only a statistical sample (e.g., 1 in 100) rather than every line, while never sampling error-level or audit-relevant logs, which is a policy decision made per log level/category, not a blanket global sampling rate.

Design an Online Judge / Code Execution Sandbox

Why this problem, specifically: it's the case study most explicitly about adversarial multi-tenancy — every other system in this guide assumes tenants merely need isolation from accidentally affecting each other; here, the assumption is that submitted code is actively trying to escape its sandbox.

Clarifying questions: which languages need support (each with different sandboxing/runtime needs)? Is this for a coding-interview platform (small, quick submissions) or a competitive-programming judge (potentially large test suites, strict time limits)?

Functional requirements: accept untrusted, arbitrary user-submitted code; compile/run it against a set of test cases; return pass/fail (and often partial-credit) results.

Non-functional requirements: strong isolation — a malicious submission must never be able to damage the host, read another user's data, or access the network; deterministic, reproducible grading; hard resource limits enforced without exception.

Architecture:

Isolation, worked through: each execution happens inside strong isolation — a container with seccomp/cgroups limits at minimum, or a microVM (Firecracker — the same technology underlying serverless cold starts, Group G above) for stronger isolation between mutually-untrusted submissions sharing physical hardware. Determinism and reproducibility, worked through as the actual point of the whole design: grading has to run every submission against a fixed, versioned image with no network access, so the same submission produces the same result regardless of which worker happened to run it or when — this is what makes the platform's results trustworthy at all; a judge whose verdict depends on which physical machine happened to run your code is a broken judge.

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "Award partial credit across multiple test cases with different weights?" — the sandbox returns a structured per-test-case result (pass/fail/time/memory) rather than one aggregate verdict, and scoring is computed by a separate, simple aggregation step applied to that structured result — keeping the sandbox's job (run code safely, report facts) cleanly separate from the scoring policy (a business-rule computation layered on top, the same separation-of-concerns instinct as every Strategy-shaped decision elsewhere in this guide).
  • "Detect plagiarism/code copying across submissions?" — an entirely separate, asynchronous batch job (Section 2.17) comparing submissions via code-similarity algorithms (token-based or AST-based comparison) run well after grading — never on the execution/grading critical path, since it's an integrity-analysis concern, not a correctness-of-execution one.

Design an IoT Telemetry Ingestion Platform

Why this problem, specifically: the constraint that makes this genuinely different from a normal high-volume ingestion pipeline is the devices themselves — battery-powered, intermittently connected, and unable to simply "retry with exponential backoff forever" the way a well-resourced server can.

Clarifying questions: are commands flowing back down to devices in scope (changes the architecture significantly, below), or is this ingestion-only? Roughly how constrained are the devices — full Linux boxes, or genuinely tiny microcontrollers?

Functional requirements: ingest frequent telemetry from millions of devices; support dashboards and alerts over recent and historical data; optionally, send configuration/commands back down to a specific device.

Architecture:

(millions, constrained)"] -->|MQTT/CoAP| GW["Ingestion gateway"]
    GW --> K[["Kafka"]]
    K --> TSDB[("Time-series DB")]

Protocol choice, worked through: devices publish over a lightweight protocol built specifically for constrained hardware and unreliable connectivity — MQTT (a small pub/sub protocol with configurable delivery guarantees via QoS levels 0/1/2, trading reliability for overhead per message) or CoAP (a RESTful protocol over UDP, suited to even more resource-constrained devices than MQTT typically targets) — both are deliberately far lighter-weight than HTTP, whose overhead is disproportionate for a device that might be sending a two-byte sensor reading.

Ingestion and storage, worked through as a direct reuse of this guide's standard heavy-ingestion pattern: telemetry flows into the same durable-log pattern used everywhere ingestion needs to absorb bursty load (Kafka, Section 2.8), and from there into a time-series database (Section 2.5) purpose-built for exactly this data shape: high write volume, almost purely append, queried by time range — this is the same architecture as Part 0's back-of-envelope worked example, with the ingestion and storage layers made concrete.

The decision that matters — commands flowing back down, worked through: sending a command directly to a specific device (update its configuration, request an immediate reading) doesn't work as a direct synchronous call, because a battery-powered or intermittently-connected device might not be reachable at the exact moment the command is issued. The standard pattern: a device shadow / desired-state object — the backend writes the desired state to a durable record regardless of whether the device is currently online, and the device itself, whenever it next connects, fetches this desired state and reconciles it against its own actual state — inverting the naive assumption that the backend needs a live connection to every device at once into "the device checks in when it can, and catches up."

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "Firmware OTA (over-the-air) updates across millions of devices?" — a staged, gradual rollout (Section 2.14's canary-deployment instinct, applied to device firmware) — push to a small percentage first, monitor for a spike in failure/crash telemetry, then expand — never a simultaneous push to the entire fleet, since a bad firmware build pushed everywhere at once is catastrophic and hard to reverse on hardware that might now be unreachable.
  • "Devices at a remote site with poor connectivity to the cloud — do they need to reach the backend for every decision?" — edge computing: push some aggregation/decision logic to a local gateway at the site itself, so routine decisions (a threshold alert, local aggregation before upload) don't require a round trip to the cloud at all, only summarized or exceptional data actually needs to leave the site.

Group H — Distinctive / Less-Common Systems

Worth preparing specifically because they show up in the "how X works" format your saved material used, and because a fresh example (versus the fortieth "design Twitter") tends to stand out to an interviewer who's heard the classics all week.

How Bluesky Works (the AT Protocol)

Why this problem, specifically: it's the best vehicle in this guide for testing whether you can reason about a genuinely decentralized architecture rather than just a distributed one — every other social-platform case study assumes one company owns the whole stack; here, ownership of data, ranking, and moderation are deliberately split across independent, mutually-untrusting parties, which changes the shape of almost every design decision.

Clarifying questions: is the focus the core protocol/data-ownership model, or the federation/scaling mechanics of running an implementation at Twitter-like scale (assume both, since they connect directly)?

Requirements distinctive to this system: a user's identity and data must be portable — movable between hosting providers without losing followers or history; ranking/feed algorithms and moderation must be pluggable, not baked into the core protocol; any party must be able to independently verify a piece of data's authenticity without trusting whichever server currently happens to be serving it.

Architecture:

flowchart LR
    U["User"] -->|writes signed records| PDS["Personal Data Server
(can be self-hosted)"]
    PDS -->|crawled by| Relay["Relay"]
    Relay -->|unified event stream| Firehose[["Firehose"]]

    Firehose --> AppView2["Alternate AppView
(anyone can build one)"]
    AppView1 -->|serves reads,
runs feed algorithm| Client["Client app"]

The Personal Data Server (PDS), worked through: holds one user's actual data (posts, follows, likes) as signed records in a repository structured as a Merkle Search Tree (MST) — a search tree (ordered, so range queries work) where every node is also content-addressed by its hash, exactly like a Merkle tree's verification property. This gives two things at once: anyone can cryptographically verify a specific record is authentic and unaltered (standard Merkle-tree inclusion proof), and anyone can verify a record does not exist without needing the entire dataset — a property a plain hash list doesn't give you, and precisely the structure needed so a relay or AppView can trust data it received secondhand without trusting the party that relayed it.

Portable identity, worked through — the mechanism that makes "switch hosting providers without losing your account" actually work: a user's identity is a DID (Decentralized Identifier), which resolves to a document pointing at their current PDS. Moving to a new host means updating that one pointer — followers, post history, and social graph all resolve through the stable DID, not through whichever server happens to host the data today, so the migration is invisible to everyone following that identity. This is the concrete technical answer to "how is this meaningfully different from just self-hosting a database" — a self-hosted database with a fixed URL isn't portable the moment you need to move it; a DID-resolved identity is portable by construction.

Relay and Firehose, worked through — this is effectively CQRS (Section 2.11) applied at network scale: a Relay crawls PDSs across the entire network and republishes everything as one unified, real-time event stream (the "firehose"). An AppView consumes this firehose and builds its own index tailored to serving an actual app experience — meaning the PDS network is the durable write model (source of truth, owned by users), and any given AppView is a derived, disposable read model (rebuild it from the firehose, and you've lost nothing) — precisely the write-model/read-model split CQRS argues for generally, here enforced across organizational boundaries rather than within one company's infrastructure.

Pluggable ranking, worked through: because the AppView is a separate, swappable component consuming a public firehose, the "algorithm" that ranks a feed is not fixed by the protocol at all — a custom feed generator is just another AppView-like service anyone can build and any client can point at, which is the concrete mechanism behind Bluesky's much-discussed "choose your own algorithm" pitch.

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "The official bsky.app AppView goes down — is the network down?" — no: the underlying PDS data and the firehose are entirely independent of any one AppView's availability — a different AppView (or a freshly rebuilt instance of the same one, replaying the firehose from its stored offset, Section 2.8) continues serving reads; this is precisely the payoff of treating an AppView as a disposable, rebuildable index rather than a source of truth.
  • "Moderation without a central authority that can unilaterally delete content?" — moderation is implemented via labels — a separate, independent "labeler" service annotates content (as spam, as violating a specific community's rules) without altering or deleting the underlying record; different AppViews/clients choose which labelers' judgments to respect, making moderation itself pluggable and contestable rather than a single central switch.
  • "Scaling the firehose to Twitter-scale write volume?" — this is precisely the celebrity/large-scale fan-out problem from the Twitter and Slack case studies, now happening at the network level (a relay fanning out to potentially many independent AppViews) rather than within one company's infrastructure — worth naming the parallel explicitly rather than treating it as an unrelated new problem.

How Apple's Find My Network / AirTag Works

Why this problem, specifically: it's the single most novel architecture in this entire guide — a location-tracking system with no GPS, no cellular radio, and no dedicated backend infrastructure at all — and it's an excellent test of whether you can reason precisely about an end-to-end encryption scheme where the server itself is deliberately kept unable to learn what it's relaying.

Clarifying questions: is the focus the location-reporting mechanism, or also the anti-stalking safeguards (assume both, since the second is a direct, interesting consequence of the first)?

Requirements distinctive to this system: location reports must be useful to the tag's owner but meaningless to everyone else who touches them — the relaying device, and Apple's own servers, must never be able to determine which tag a report is for or where it actually is; the system must work using only infrastructure that already exists (other people's phones), with no dedicated hardware to deploy.

Architecture:

flowchart LR
    Tag["AirTag
broadcasts rotating public key"] -.->|BLE, opportunistic| Finder["Nearby stranger's iPhone"]
    Finder -->|"encrypted location report
(finder never decrypts)"| Apple[("Apple servers

    Owner["Owner's iPhone
(shares the tag's secret)"] -->|derives same key sequence,

The rotating key mechanism, worked through precisely — this is the entire trick: an AirTag and its owner's phone share a secret, established at pairing time. Both independently derive the same sequence of rotating key pairs over time (a symmetric key-ratchet, conceptually the same forward-derivation idea as the Signal Protocol's double ratchet mentioned in the WhatsApp case study — each side can compute the next key in the sequence without any further communication). The tag continuously broadcasts its current public key over Bluetooth Low Energy, changing to the next key in the sequence roughly every 15 minutes specifically so an eavesdropper can't correlate broadcasts over time into a persistent tracking identifier.

The finder's role, worked through — deliberately minimal: any nearby iPhone/iPad/Mac that opportunistically detects the broadcast (over a billion devices worldwide, forming the "network" with zero dedicated hardware) determines its own current GPS location, encrypts a report (location, timestamp) using the broadcast public key it just received — a key it has no way to attribute to any specific tag or owner — and uploads the encrypted blob plus a hash of the public key to Apple's servers. The finder device learns nothing (it never had the private key), and neither does Apple (the server stores ciphertext indexed by a rotating key-hash it can't link to an identity).

The owner's retrieval path, worked through — the other half of why this works: the owner's phone, having derived the exact same sequence of key pairs as the tag (since it shares the originating secret), computes which public keys the tag has likely been broadcasting recently, queries Apple's server for reports matching the hashes of those specific keys, and — critically — is the only party that ever holds the matching private keys needed to decrypt what it retrieves. Apple's server performed real, useful work (storing and returning the right ciphertexts) without ever being able to know whose tag it was serving or where that tag actually is.

Precision Finding, worked through as a genuinely separate mechanism: once the owner is generally near the tag (established via the BLE/server mechanism above), fine-grained "12 feet away, this direction" guidance uses Ultra-Wideband (UWB) radio instead — BLE's signal strength alone is too noisy to derive precise direction and distance, so the last-mile guidance switches to a radio technology actually suited to that specific sub-problem, worth naming as a deliberate protocol handoff rather than one radio doing double duty.

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "Detect an unknown AirTag someone has planted on you to track you without consent." — every phone (not just the owner's) periodically scans for BLE broadcasts from tags it doesn't recognize, and applies a heuristic: if the same unknown tag's identifier is detected repeatedly, over time, away from wherever its actual owner would plausibly be (i.e., it's been traveling with you, not its owner) — it triggers an unwanted-tracking alert. This is a genuinely hard privacy-vs-safety trade-off worth naming explicitly: the same rotating-key anonymity that protects a legitimate owner's privacy from Apple and from finder devices is exactly what a bad-faith use of the same hardware also relies on, and the detection heuristic is the system's attempt to distinguish the two after the fact rather than prevent the anonymity property from existing at all.
  • "Battery life — why 15-minute key rotation and not something more frequent/private?" — a real engineering trade-off between privacy (faster rotation narrows the tracking-correlation window further) and battery life (an AirTag runs for roughly a year on a coin cell, so both the BLE broadcast frequency and the key-rotation cadence are tuned against that hard power budget, not chosen for privacy alone) — worth naming that this is a deliberately engineered trade-off point, not an arbitrary default.

How LLM Serving Works ("Design ChatGPT")

Why this problem, specifically: it's the newest and fastest-evolving case study in this entire guide, and the interview signal is specific — do you understand that transformer inference has genuinely unusual hardware characteristics (two phases with opposite bottlenecks) that make it a real systems problem, not just "call a model API and stream the result"?

Clarifying questions: one model size, or routing between a cheap small model and an expensive large one based on query difficulty (assume routing is a valid extension, covered below)? Multi-turn conversation history in scope? What's the latency SLA — interactive chat (seconds to first token) or tolerant batch processing?

Functional requirements: accept a prompt (plus conversation history); generate a response token-by-token; stream tokens back as they're produced; support a very large number of concurrent conversations.

Non-functional requirements: low time-to-first-token (users notice delay before any output appears); high aggregate throughput per (extremely expensive) GPU, since accelerator cost dominates this system's economics unlike almost anything else in this guide; graceful behavior under GPU memory pressure.

Architecture:

flowchart TD
    C["Client"] --> GW["API Gateway"]
    GW --> Router["Router
(model selection)"]
    Router --> Prefill["Prefill pool
(compute-bound)"]
    Prefill -->|KV cache| Decode["Decode pool
(memory-bandwidth-bound)"]
    Decode -->|SSE, token by token| C
    Decode -.->|reuse across turns| KVStore[("KV cache store")]

Prefill vs. decode, worked through precisely — this is the single most important idea in the whole case study: processing a prompt has two genuinely different phases with opposite hardware bottlenecks. Prefill processes the entire input prompt in parallel in one pass (every input token's attention computation can happen simultaneously via matrix multiplication) — this is compute-bound, meaning it's limited by raw GPU FLOPs. Decode generates output tokens one at a time, and each single new token still requires reading the entire model's weights (potentially hundreds of gigabytes) from GPU memory — this is memory-bandwidth-bound, meaning it's limited by how fast data can move, not by compute capacity, which sits comparatively idle during decode. Because these two phases compete for the same GPU resources in opposite ways, several production serving systems physically separate them onto different GPU pools — a prefill pool optimized/scheled for compute throughput, a decode pool optimized for memory bandwidth — so a burst of new prompts doesn't stall the token-by-token generation of requests already in flight, and vice versa.

Continuous batching, worked through — the mechanism that makes GPU utilization viable at all: GPUs are only cost-effective when processing many requests together in a batch, but a naive static batch (wait for N requests, run them together, return only once every request in the batch is fully done) wastes enormous GPU time — one request generating a 500-token response holds up nine others that finished at 50 tokens, since the whole batch is blocked until the slowest member completes. Continuous batching instead evicts finished requests from the batch and admits newly-arrived ones at every single decoding iteration, keeping the GPU consistently full of active work rather than periodically idling or being held hostage by the batch's slowest member — this one technique is responsible for a large fraction of the throughput improvement in modern LLM-serving systems over naive batching.

The KV cache, worked through — what it is, and why it's the thing that actually limits concurrency: to avoid recomputing a token's attention over the entire preceding context on every single decode step, the serving system caches each prior token's computed key/value tensors (the "KV cache") and reuses them — decode only has to compute attention for the new token against this cache, not recompute everything from scratch. The cost: KV cache size grows with both sequence length and the number of concurrent conversations, and it consumes the same scarce GPU memory the model weights themselves need — this, not raw compute, is usually the actual ceiling on how many concurrent users one GPU can serve. A specific, real technique worth naming: PagedAttention manages the KV cache in fixed-size, non-contiguous blocks (directly analogous to how an OS's virtual memory paging avoids requiring one large contiguous memory allocation per process) — dramatically reducing the memory fragmentation that would otherwise waste a large fraction of available KV cache capacity on gaps between variable-length sequences.

Model parallelism, worked through for when one GPU isn't enough: a model too large to fit on a single GPU's memory is split across multiple GPUs — tensor parallelism splits individual layers' matrix operations across GPUs (each GPU computes part of the same layer, requiring fast interconnect between them since every layer needs cross-GPU communication), while pipeline parallelism assigns different entire layers to different GPUs (a request flows through GPU 1's layers, then GPU 2's, and so on, trading some latency for less required inter-GPU bandwidth than tensor parallelism needs).

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "Route cheap queries to a small, fast model and only use the large model when needed?" — a lightweight classifier (or the router itself, heuristically) estimates query difficulty before dispatch, sending simple queries to a smaller, cheaper model and reserving the large model's cost for queries that genuinely need it — directly analogous to the two-phase candidate-generation/ranking shape used throughout this guide's recommendation-adjacent case studies, here optimizing cost/latency instead of relevance.
  • "Reduce serving cost further?" — quantization (representing model weights with fewer bits — e.g., 8-bit instead of 16/32-bit — trading a small amount of output quality for significantly less memory bandwidth pressure, directly attacking decode's actual bottleneck) and speculative decoding (a small, fast "draft" model proposes several candidate next-tokens at once, and the large model verifies them all in a single parallel pass rather than generating each one sequentially — a real technique that can meaningfully speed up decode when the draft model's guesses are frequently correct).
  • "A GPU node crashes mid-generation — what happens to that conversation?" — the in-flight generation is lost (the KV cache for that request lived only in that node's memory) and the client's stream simply terminates — the client reconnects and either restarts the generation from the last complete turn (safe, resubmits full context) or, if the platform persists KV cache state externally, resumes more cheaply — worth naming that losing an in-progress response here is a far lower-stakes failure than losing a message in the WhatsApp case study, since regenerating a response is cheap and has no correctness implications the way a lost chat message does.

Design a RAG-Based Support Assistant

Why this problem, specifically: the retrieval/generation mechanics themselves are Section 2.15's general RAG pipeline, already covered — the actual interview signal here is whether you treat evaluation and the escalation path as first-class design components, since those are what separates a demo from something a company can actually put in front of customers.

Clarifying questions: what's the corpus (structured documentation, freeform past support tickets, or both — mixed corpora need different chunking strategies)? Does the knowledge base change frequently enough that indexing freshness is a real requirement? Multi-turn conversation, where a follow-up question depends on earlier context?

Functional requirements: answer user questions grounded strictly in a specific knowledge base; cite the sources an answer is based on; escalate to a human when confidence is low, rather than guessing with false authority; stay current as underlying documents change.

Architecture:

    Index -->|chunk + embed| VDB[("Vector DB")]
    Q["User question"] --> Rewrite["Query rewriting

Chunking, worked through in more depth than the general pipeline description: fixed-size chunking (split every N tokens) is simple but can sever a sentence or a table mid-thought, hurting retrieval relevance right at chunk boundaries; semantic chunking (splitting at natural document boundaries — headings, paragraphs) preserves meaning better at the cost of variable, less predictable chunk sizes. A small overlap between consecutive chunks (e.g., the last sentence of one chunk repeated as the first sentence of the next) is a common mitigation either way, so a fact sitting near a boundary isn't fully lost to whichever side of the cut it landed on.

Hybrid search, worked through mechanically: combine dense retrieval (embedding-similarity search, Section 2.15 — good at semantic/conceptual matches) with sparse retrieval (BM25-style keyword matching, Section 2.5's search-engine ranking — good at exact matches: product SKUs, error codes, proper nouns that a pure semantic match can miss entirely). The two ranked lists are combined — Reciprocal Rank Fusion (scoring each document by the sum of 1/(rank + k) across both lists, so a document ranked well by either method contributes meaningfully) is a common, simple way to merge them into one final ranking without needing to carefully tune a weighted blend of two different scoring scales.

Prompt construction, worked through: the retrieved, re-ranked chunks are inserted into the prompt alongside explicit grounding instructions — telling the model to answer only from the provided context and to explicitly say so when the context doesn't contain an answer, rather than falling back on its own general training knowledge (which might be outdated or simply wrong for this specific company's product) — this instruction is doing real work, not boilerplate, since an ungrounded model will otherwise confidently answer from memory even when the retrieved context is empty or irrelevant.

Evaluation, worked through as a genuinely designed pipeline, not an afterthought: a golden dataset of representative question/expected-answer pairs is run against the system on every change to the prompt, retrieval pipeline, or underlying model — the AI-era equivalent of a regression test suite, necessary because LLM outputs aren't deterministic enough to unit-test in the traditional sense. Concrete metrics worth naming (the RAGAS-style framing): faithfulness (does the generated answer actually only draw on the retrieved context, or did the model add unsupported claims?) and context relevance (did retrieval actually surface chunks that address the question at all, independent of how the model then used them?) — separating these two metrics matters because a bad answer could stem from either bad retrieval or bad generation, and conflating them into one pass/fail score hides which half of the pipeline actually needs fixing.

The escalation path, worked through as a first-class component: define a concrete, measurable confidence signal — retrieval similarity score falling below a threshold, or the model's own stated uncertainty — that routes to a human agent instead of returning a low-confidence answer dressed up with the same confident tone as a well-grounded one. This is worth treating as seriously as any other component in the architecture diagram, not a vague "and if it's not sure, ask a human" footnote.

Freshness, worked through as a direct application of an earlier pattern: re-indexing is triggered by a CDC event or webhook from the knowledge-base system the moment a document changes (Section 2.1's CDC pattern, applied here directly) rather than a blind nightly cron job — the practical difference between an assistant that's wrong for up to a day after a doc fix ships, and one that's current within minutes.

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "Two documents in the knowledge base contradict each other — what does the assistant do?" — surface both retrieved pieces of information with their respective citations rather than silently picking one, letting the user (or a downstream human reviewer) see and resolve the conflict — the system's job is faithful retrieval and grounding, not adjudicating which of two internal documents is authoritative.
  • "Multi-turn conversation — a follow-up question like 'what about for enterprise customers?' depends on what was asked before." — a query rewriting/condensation step runs before retrieval, folding the conversation history and the new fragment into one self-contained, retrieval-ready query (an LLM call in its own right, distinct from the final answer-generation call) — without this, the retrieval step would search for "what about for enterprise customers" literally, with no idea what topic it's even about.
  • "Control runaway token-usage costs?" — cap retrieved-context size (top-K chunks after re-ranking, not every remotely-relevant chunk found), cache answers to frequently-repeated questions (semantic caching, Section 2.15 — cache by embedding similarity to a prior query, not just exact string match), and track token usage per conversation as a first-class metric (Section 2.15's AI observability) precisely because it's the direct cost driver, not an incidental log field.

Group J — Personalization, Monetization & Compliance

Less universally asked than Groups A–I, but each of these three shows up often enough at companies with a monetization or data-privacy angle that they're worth having a structured answer ready for, rather than improvising from zero.

Design a Recommendation System

Why this problem, specifically: almost every other case study in this guide optimizes for correctness or latency; this is the one where the interview signal is explicitly about quality under uncertainty — there's no "correct" recommendation, only better and worse bets, and the system has to be architected around continuously measuring and improving those bets.

Clarifying questions: what's being recommended (products, videos, social content — changes candidate-generation signals significantly)? Is this for a logged-in user with rich history, or does it need to handle anonymous/cold-start traffic as a first-class case? Is the evaluation loop (A/B testing infrastructure) in scope?

Functional requirements: given a user (or an anonymous session), surface items they're likely to want from a catalog far too large to score exhaustively; adapt as new interaction data arrives.

Non-functional requirements: low latency per request (recommendations typically load alongside a page, not after a spinner); the quality of recommendations must be continuously measurable and improvable, which is as much an experimentation-infrastructure requirement as a serving one.

Architecture:

flowchart TD
    Hist[("Interaction history
(offline, batch)")] --> CG["Candidate Generation
(collaborative + content-based)"]
    CG -->|few hundred candidates| Rank["Ranking model
(online, per-request)"]
    FS[("Feature Store")] --> Rank
    Rank --> Result["Final recommendations"]
    Result -.->|logged| Hist
    Result -.->|A/B experiment assignment| Eval["Offline + online evaluation"]

Candidate generation, worked through in more depth: production recommenders are almost universally two-phase, precisely because scoring millions of items with an expensive model per request is too slow. Candidate generation itself typically blends multiple independent sources, each cheap and computed mostly offline: collaborative filtering (matrix factorization or embedding-based — learning a vector representation of users and items such that "similar" vectors imply affinity, trained in batch over the full interaction history, Section 2.17), content-based similarity (an item's own attributes/embedding, useful specifically because it works even for items with little interaction history), and simple popularity/trending signals (a fallback that's crude but never fails). Blending several sources rather than relying on one is itself a design decision worth naming — each source fails differently (collaborative filtering fails on new items; content-based fails to capture "people who bought this also bought a totally unrelated but complementary item"), so combining them covers each other's blind spots.

Ranking, worked through: the shortlist from candidate generation (a few hundred items) is scored by a more expensive model that can afford to run online, per-request — incorporating features a batch-computed candidate score can't (current session behavior, time of day, what the user just clicked seconds ago). This is exactly the same "cheap broad filter, then expensive precise scoring" shape as the LLM-serving and ad-ranking case studies in this guide — worth naming the pattern-recognition explicitly.

The feature store, worked through as the component that makes online ranking possible at all: a ranking model needs both slow-changing, offline-computed features (a user's long-term category affinity, computed nightly) and fast-changing, online features (what they clicked in this exact session) — a feature store is the infrastructure that serves both consistently to the ranking model at request time, reconciling data computed on very different schedules into one feature vector the model can actually use.

Cold start, worked through with the more precise "exploration vs. exploitation" framing: a brand-new user or item has no interaction history for collaborative filtering to use at all — beyond the content-based/popularity fallback already named, a more principled framing treats this as a multi-armed bandit problem: deliberately show some variety/exploration to a new user (or promote a new item to some fraction of traffic) specifically to gather the interaction data that makes future recommendations better, rather than only ever exploiting what's already known to work — a real, designed trade-off between today's recommendation quality and tomorrow's, not just a fallback for a broken cold path.

Evaluation, worked through as a two-tier system: offline metrics (precision@K, NDCG — comparing a candidate ranking against historical logged interactions) are cheap and fast to compute on every model change, but only ever approximate real user response, since they're evaluated against what users did under the old ranking, not what they'd do under the new one. Online A/B testing (serving the new ranking to a small percentage of real traffic and measuring actual engagement/conversion) is the ground truth, but slow and statistically expensive to run for every change — the practical answer is offline metrics as a fast filter to reject obviously-worse changes, with online A/B testing reserved for candidates that pass the offline bar.

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "Recommendations create a filter bubble — a user only ever sees more of what they already engage with." — a diversity/serendipity injection step, structurally identical to the News Feed case study's diversity pass (Group B) — deliberately including some candidates outside the user's established pattern, traded off deliberately against pure relevance, and measured as its own metric (not just engagement) so the trade-off is visible rather than accidental.
  • "Re-rank in real time based on what the user does in this exact session (they just clicked into a specific category)?" — a lightweight, session-scoped signal blended into the ranking features online, separate from the slower-moving, batch-computed long-term profile — the same "fast online signal blended with slow offline profile" idea named in the Spotify case study's listening-taste-shift curveball (Group D).

Design an Ad-Serving / Real-Time Bidding System

Why this problem, specifically: the defining constraint is the tightest latency budget in this guide outside the Stock Exchange case study, combined with auction theory as a genuinely different kind of "correctness" than anything else here — getting the auction mechanism right is as much the point as any systems-design concern.

Clarifying questions: is this the publisher/exchange side (running the auction) or the bidder/advertiser side (deciding how much to bid — assume the exchange side, since it's the more complete systems problem)? Real-time bidding specifically, or a simpler fixed-price/reservation ad model too?

Functional requirements: for every ad impression opportunity, select a winning ad (or run an auction among competing bidders) and return it within the latency budget; track impressions/clicks for billing and optimization.

Non-functional requirements: end-to-end latency typically under 100ms, with the bidding step itself often budgeted under 50ms — tight enough that the design constraints look more like the Stock Exchange case study's than a typical web backend's.

Architecture:

The auction mechanism, worked through — this is the part that's genuinely different from every other case study in this guide: a second-price (Vickrey) auction is the standard mechanism — the highest bidder wins, but pays only the second-highest bid amount, not their own bid. This is worth being able to explain why, not just what: it's specifically designed to be incentive-compatible — a bidder's optimal strategy is to bid their true, honest valuation of the impression, since bidding higher than that risks overpaying (you'd only ever pay up to the second bid anyway) and bidding lower risks losing an auction you'd have profitably won. A first-price auction, by contrast, incentivizes bidders to guess and shade their bids strategically below their true value, which is a worse mechanism for the exchange's own long-term price discovery.

The eligibility-and-ranking pipeline, worked through under the latency budget: an ad request carries user/context signals; eligible campaigns are filtered by targeting criteria (demographics, keywords, remaining budget) entirely in-memory — the latency budget rules out any synchronous database call on this path, the same "no database on the hot path" discipline as the LLM-serving and Rate Limiter case studies, here even more strictly enforced. Eligible campaigns are then ranked by expected value (bid amount × predicted click/conversion probability — the second factor itself often a small, fast ML model, another instance of this guide's recurring "expensive model, but scoped to a small shortlist" pattern).

Budget pacing, worked through as a genuinely distinct control-systems problem: an advertiser's daily budget shouldn't be exhausted in the first hour of the day (missing all the rest of the day's opportunities) nor sit unspent at midnight — pacing continuously adjusts how aggressively a campaign bids throughout the day based on spend-so-far versus time-elapsed, a feedback-control problem layered on top of the auction mechanics, worth naming as a separate concern from the auction itself.

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "Frequency capping and fraud detection — don't show the same user the same ad 50 times, and don't get billed for bot clicks." — both are better handled asynchronously with a fast-reject fallback (accept optimistically within the latency budget, then correct/bill afterward) rather than synchronously on the auction path, since neither can be fully resolved within a 50ms budget — worth stating this trade-off explicitly rather than pretending everything happens inline.
  • "Header bidding vs. the older 'waterfall' model?" — a waterfall sequentially asks one ad network at a time until one fills the impression (simple, but slow and leaves money on the table, since the exchange never sees what a later network in the waterfall would have paid); header bidding solicits bids from multiple sources in parallel before the auction, closer to the exchange architecture described above — worth naming as evidence the parallel-fan-out design isn't an arbitrary choice, it's a direct improvement over a real, worse predecessor architecture.
  • "Attribution — a user saw an ad, then converted three days later after also seeing a competitor's ad. Who gets credit?" — last-click attribution (the most recent ad before conversion gets full credit) is simple but arguably wrong; multi-touch attribution splits credit across every touchpoint in the user's path — a genuinely unresolved, business-policy-dependent question worth naming as such rather than presenting one answer as objectively correct.

Design a Privacy / GDPR Deletion Pipeline

Why this problem, specifically: unlike almost every other case study in this guide, the hard part isn't latency or scale — it's cross-system consistency and provable correctness under a legal, not just technical, deadline, which changes what "done" even means.

Clarifying questions: what's the deletion SLA (GDPR itself specifies within one month, extendable)? Does the platform also need to support data export ("right to access") as a mirror-image operation? Are there categories of data legally exempt from deletion (financial records subject to retention law, active legal holds)?

Functional requirements: on a verified user deletion request, remove their personal data from every system that has ever stored or received a copy of it; produce auditable proof the deletion occurred; respect legal holds that override deletion.

Architecture:

The prerequisite most designs skip past too quickly, worth stating first: you cannot delete what you don't know you have — a maintained data catalog/inventory mapping every system that stores or has ever received a copy of user data is the actual foundation this entire pipeline stands on, and building/maintaining that catalog (every new service and every new data pipeline must register what personal data it touches) is arguably a harder, more sustained organizational problem than the deletion workflow itself.

The orchestrated fan-out, worked through: a deletion request becomes a workflow (Section 2.17's orchestration tools — Airflow/Temporal-style) that fans a delete instruction out to every cataloged system, tracks per-system completion independently (since different systems delete at very different speeds — a database row deletes in milliseconds, a data-lake rewrite might take hours), and only marks the overall request complete once every system confirms.

Verification — worth naming as a distinct step from the deletion instruction itself: actually proving deletion happened is a separate, explicit pass — re-querying each system afterward to confirm the data is genuinely gone, not merely trusting that a delete command was sent and assumed to succeed. This distinction (issuing a delete vs. verifying its effect) matters because a delete call can silently fail or partially apply, and "we sent the request" is not the same claim as "we confirmed the outcome," which is exactly what a regulator asking for proof will actually want to see.

Backups and legal holds, worked through as the two genuine hard cases: you typically can't (and often legally shouldn't, for backup-integrity reasons) reach into an immutable backup to delete one user's record — the practical, industry-standard answer is a deletion policy ensuring backups age out and are destroyed on a bounded schedule short enough to satisfy the regulation's spirit, rather than promising instantaneous deletion everywhere including inside frozen backup archives. A legal hold (the user's data is subject to active litigation or a regulatory investigation) can legitimately override a deletion request entirely — the pipeline needs an explicit hold-check gate before executing anything irreversible, exactly as drawn in the architecture diagram above, not as an exception handled after the fact.

The mirror-image operation, worth naming: GDPR's "right to access" requires the same data-catalog-driven fan-out, just aggregating and exporting data instead of deleting it — worth mentioning explicitly, since a design that's built the catalog and orchestration infrastructure for deletion has already built most of what access-requests need too, and naming this reuse is a signal of efficient system thinking rather than treating the two as unrelated features.

Interviewer curveballs:

  • "A user's data was used to train an ML model — can you delete it from the model?" — you generally cannot surgically remove one user's influence from an already-trained model's weights; the practical answer is excluding their data from all future training runs (and, for especially sensitive cases, forcing an earlier retrain without their data) — worth stating this limitation honestly rather than implying a clean technical deletion is always possible, since it isn't, and a strong answer names the real, imperfect trade-off rather than glossing over it.
  • "Anonymization instead of deletion — is that an acceptable substitute?" — genuinely depends on whether the anonymization is irreversible — data stripped of direct identifiers but still re-identifiable via combination with other fields (a classic, well-documented failure mode) does not satisfy a deletion requirement, and a strong answer flags that "we anonymized it" is a claim that itself needs verification, not an automatic pass.
